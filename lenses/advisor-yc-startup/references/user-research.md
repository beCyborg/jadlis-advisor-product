# User Research: Interview Protocol for Early-Stage Founders

Source: "How To Talk To Users" — Gustav Alstromer, YC Group Partner (YC Startup School)

---

## Core Principle

Users and customers are the only stakeholders who pay you and will tell you the truth. Talk to them throughout the entire lifetime of the company — before you have a product, during MVP, post-launch, and at scale. Brian Chesky of Airbnb lived in 50 different Airbnbs to talk to hosts daily. He and Joe still give hosts their personal cell numbers. This is not a one-time research phase — it is an ongoing operating discipline.

Never hide behind info@ emails or do-not-reply addresses. When you hide from users, you lose the only honest signal available to you.

---

## How to Find Interviewees

Start with your immediate network, then expand outward. Three tiers:

**Tier 1 — Personal network**: People you know. Most likely to respond. Potentially less honest to avoid offending you — account for this in signal interpretation. Still worth doing.

**Tier 2 — Professional network**: Former co-workers, industry contacts. High relevance if you are building in a domain they know. Navigate to potential users via LinkedIn.

**Tier 3 — Cold outreach**: LinkedIn, Reddit, Slack communities, Discord servers, in-person events. This is where most early-stage YC founders find their first interviewees.

### Outreach Message Structure (LinkedIn Template)

For known contacts:
1. Brief personal reference ("our time at [company]")
2. One sentence: "I'm starting a new project"
3. Brief description of the domain — not the solution, just the problem space
4. Request for a 20-minute call or video call. That's it.

For unknown contacts:
- Same structure, different tone — more formal, less personal reference
- Keep it short. Do not pitch a product. Do not ask for investment. Ask only for 20 minutes to learn about their experience with a specific problem.

Target outreach at people whose job title suggests they encounter your problem domain. For a carbon emissions product: CFOs, founders, anyone with "sustainability" or "climate" in their title.

## Interview Format

Run interviews over video call, phone, or in person. Never over email or survey forms. A 5-minute video call yields more signal than 5,000 survey responses.

Build rapport before asking questions. The interviewee will answer questions no one has asked them before — they need to trust you and feel comfortable before they will be candid.

**Do not introduce your product or idea until the very end of the call — or not at all.** Introducing your idea early biases every answer that follows. Your role is to listen, not to pitch. If you must share your idea, do it in the last 2 minutes.

Record the interview if possible. If not, take dense notes during the call. You will need to translate recordings to notes anyway — taking notes live saves a step.

Conduct at least 5-10 interviews before drawing conclusions. Pattern recognition requires volume.

## The 6 Good Questions

These questions form the core of every interview. Use them in sequence. Use follow-up probes after each.

### 1. "Tell me how you do [X] today."

X = the specific task, workflow, or goal in your problem domain. Make X concrete — not "how do you handle sustainability" but "how do you calculate and report your company's carbon emissions."

**What you are listening for**: The actual current workflow. Every step, every tool, every person involved. This reveals complexity, dependencies, and workarounds you did not anticipate.

**Good answer**: Specific, sequential, names actual tools — "We fill out a Word doc our consultant sends us, send it back, and get a PDF four weeks later." Real behavior, not idealized behavior.

**Weak answer**: "We have a process for that" with no specifics. Push with a follow-up: "Can you walk me through the last time you did that?"

**Best case**: Ask them to screen share and show you. Watch them actually do the task. Seeing behavior is worth 10x hearing them describe it. What people do and what they say they do are frequently different.

### 2. "What is the hardest part about doing [X]?"

**What you are listening for**: The genuine pain point. This is your candidate problem to solve. You are listening for frustration in their voice, specificity in the description, and evidence they have tried to solve it.

**Good answer**: "The data we put into the report feels inaccurate and we don't know what to do with the output — it's dense and uses terms we don't understand." Specific problem, specific consequence.

**Weak answer**: "It's a bit time-consuming sometimes." Vague and low-stakes. If they cannot identify a hard part, the problem may not be painful enough to build a business on.

### 3. "Why is that hard?"

Follow-up to question 2. The stated hard part is often a symptom. The root cause is what you are building for.

**What you are listening for**: The underlying constraint — missing information, broken process, wrong incentives, skill gap, trust deficit.

In the carbon example: the user says the report is dense. Why? Because the consultant asked them to fill out a Word doc, the data they provided was low quality, and now neither party has the expertise to interpret the output. The root cause is a broken data collection and interpretation process — not just "the report is hard to read."

**Use follow-up probes**: "Can you tell me more about that?" / "What do you mean by [term they used]?" / "Why does that matter to you?" These are not interrogative — they are genuinely curious. Get comfortable using them. Most people do not fully explain themselves in one answer.

### 4. "How often do you encounter this problem?"

**What you are listening for**: Frequency determines urgency. A problem that occurs once a year is categorically different from one that occurs daily. High-frequency problems warrant strong solutions and command higher willingness to pay.

**Good answer**: Daily, weekly, or "every time we try to do [task]."

**Weak answer**: "Once a year during our audit" — low frequency, low urgency. You can still build a business here, but the sales cycle will be harder and the willingness to pay for continuous tooling is lower.

### 5. "Why is this important to your company?"

The deepest question. Understand the motivation behind caring about the problem.

**What you are listening for**: Business motivation (regulatory compliance, investor pressure, cost reduction, competitive differentiation), personal motivation (individual's role and career stake), and willingness to pay.

In the carbon example: "There are regulations coming" is a business driver. "Our investors are asking" is external pressure. Both create urgency and budget. "We kind of care about the environment" does not.

**Dig deep here.** The motivation reveals the buyer, the budget source, and the price ceiling. If they cannot articulate why this is important, the problem may not create enough urgency to drive a purchasing decision.

### 6. "What do you do to solve this problem today?"

**What you are listening for**: Existing workarounds and competitors. This reveals (a) current alternatives you must displace, (b) how much effort users currently invest in solving this problem, and (c) whether they are already paying money for a solution.

**Good answer**: "We pay a consultant $15K/year to produce a PDF report." This tells you: (1) there is already a budget, (2) the solution is manual and therefore improvable, (3) you have a concrete price anchor.

**Strong signal**: They have cobbled together multiple tools and workarounds. High effort = high pain = high willingness to switch.

**Weak signal**: "We don't really do anything." Either the problem is not painful enough, or they have given up — find out which.

Note: If they are using Excel or Google Sheets as their current solution, displacement requires dramatic improvement. Users will not pay for something that is only marginally better than a free tool they already know.

## Follow-Up Probe Protocol

Never ask only the primary question and move on. Use these probes to go deeper after any answer:

- "What do you mean by [term]?"
- "Can you tell me more about that?"
- "Why is that important to you?"
- "Can you walk me through a specific time that happened?"

Do not ask two questions at once. You will confuse the interviewee and conflate two separate signals. Ask one question. Wait. Listen. Then follow up.

## The 4 Forbidden Question Types

These questions produce false or useless data. Never use them.

### 1. "Would you use a product that does [X]?"

**Why wrong**: Hypothetical future behavior is meaningless. People will almost always say yes to avoid being rude. The answer tells you nothing about actual adoption or willingness to pay.

**What to ask instead**: Ask about their current behavior. "What do you do today?" reveals revealed preference. "Would you use" asks for stated preference, which is unreliable.

### 2. "What features would you want in a product?"

**Why wrong**: Users are experts in their problems. They are not product managers. When you ask users to design the solution, you get answers like "I want to see both the inbox and the email at the same time" — which sounds like a feature request but actually signals a latency problem. Users accurately describe pain. They poorly describe solutions.

Early Gmail users asked to see inbox and email simultaneously — because Gmail was slow, not because split-pane was the right UI. Early Airbnb guests wanted host phone numbers — not because calling was the right mechanism, but because they didn't trust the platform yet. Both "feature requests" pointed to real underlying problems that required different solutions than the users proposed.

**What to ask instead**: Ask about the problem. "What is the hardest part?" — then you design the solution.

### 3. Yes/No questions

**Why wrong**: A yes or no answer gives you no information. You learn nothing about context, severity, frequency, or motivation.

**What to ask instead**: Open-ended questions that require a narrative. "How do you..." / "Tell me about..." / "What happens when..." / "Why is...". You want them to explain and give concrete, real examples.

### 4. "How would a better version of [product] look?"

**Why wrong**: This invites fantasizing. Users are not designers or engineers. Asking them to imagine a better product produces a wish list, not a problem map. Their job is doing their job, not designing yours.

**What to ask instead**: Ask about what is painful about the current experience. "What takes the most time?" / "What do you have to do manually that you wish you didn't?" Let the problem description drive your design decisions.

### Additional error: Asking two questions simultaneously

When you are eager to learn, you will be tempted to stack questions: "What is the hardest part, and how often does it happen, and how do you solve it?" This fragments the answer and makes notes useless. Ask one question. Wait. Then ask the next.

## The Iron Rule: Problems, Not Features

Your brain during user interviews is continuously generating solutions. Suppress this. The single purpose of a user interview is to understand problems, workflows, and pain — not to validate your solution or collect feature requests.

Users generally have accurate problems but poor solutions. They know what hurts. They do not know how to fix it. Your job division is:

- **User's job**: Tell you what is hard, slow, confusing, or broken.
- **Your job**: Design the solution.

The moment you start asking about features or solutions, you have crossed roles and you are now collecting unreliable data. Stay in problem space for the entire interview.

## Signal Interpretation

After 5-10 interviews, you will have notes. Organize them by problem category (sticky notes or equivalent software). Look for patterns across interviews.

### Strong signal — act on this:

- Three or more users independently describe the same problem in similar language
- User has already tried multiple workarounds or paid for multiple solutions
- User can describe a specific, concrete instance of the problem ("last Tuesday I spent 4 hours on this")
- User has already allocated budget to solve a related problem
- User expresses frustration or resignation — "we've just accepted that this is the way it is"
- User asks how soon you can build it or offers to pay before the product exists

### Weak signal — validate further before acting:

- "That sounds interesting" or "I'd probably use that"
- Vague enthusiasm without specific examples
- "We've thought about solving that" without any action taken
- Agreement with your problem framing without independently volunteering the pain

### Red flag — reconsider your hypothesis:

- Nobody has the problem you are describing
- People have the problem but consider it minor / not worth solving
- The problem exists but users are satisfied with their current workaround (especially if the workaround is free)
- Only one person out of 10 describes the problem — the others have never thought about it

### Evaluating whether the problem is worth solving:

Ask three diagnostic questions across your interviews:

1. Are people already paying money for other solutions in this space? If yes, budget exists.
2. Do people have existing solutions they are happy with (including Excel)? If yes, your solution must be dramatically better — not marginally better.
3. How easy is this audience to sell to? Plumbers and contractors rarely change software. Startups try new tools constantly. Easy-to-sell audiences accelerate your early traction.

## When to Stop Interviewing and Start Building

Stop interviewing when:

- Three or more users independently describe the same problem without you prompting them
- You can predict what the next interviewee will say before they say it
- You have identified a problem that people are already paying to solve (even with a bad solution)
- You have enough information to write a specific hypothesis for your MVP

Do not over-intellectualize this. You are not writing a research paper. You want accurate enough information to make a good MVP decision. Start assigning an MVP as fast as you can once the pattern is clear.

Interviewing does not stop at the MVP stage. Keep conducting user interviews throughout all stages: during MVP development, post-launch, after each major feature. The best founders do this for the lifetime of the company.

---

## Keeping Interviewees Engaged After Initial Research

Once you have done 5-10 interviews, keep the best interviewees involved as ongoing advisors:

- Create a Slack or WhatsApp group for early users. Make them feel like they have exclusive access to a world-changing product in progress.
- Show them updated prototypes as the product evolves. Give them the experience of watching their feedback translate directly into product changes.
- Ship new screens or versions to this group before anyone else. Fast reaction to their feedback builds trust.
- Enable connections between them — they rarely get to talk to others facing the same problem, and you become the facilitator.

This group becomes your ongoing feedback loop, your first paying customers, and your first word-of-mouth referrers.

## Interview Protocol for Prototype Testing

When you have an early prototype (even a clickable design in Figma or Invision — not functional code):

- Hand them your phone or share your screen
- Give them a specific goal: "Try to book an accommodation" or "Try to add your company's emissions data"
- Do not tell them what to click or how to proceed
- Watch what they do. Where do they hesitate? Where do they click the wrong thing?
- Ask them to narrate their thinking aloud: "Tell me what you're seeing and what you're thinking as you go through this"

This reveals UX issues and vocabulary mismatches (the words on the screen do not match the words they use). You are not standing next to a real user when they use the product — the prototype session is the closest you get.
