# Business Models and Pricing

## The 9 YC Business Models

Nearly every billion-dollar company uses one of nine business models. Do not attempt to invent a new one. Innovate on product. Copy the business model.

**Distribution across YC top 100 companies:**

| Model | % of YC Top 100 | % of YC Top 100 Value |
|---|---|---|
| SaaS | 31% | ~21% |
| Transactional | 22% | 29% |
| Marketplace | 14% | 30% |
| Hard Tech | — | — |
| Usage-Based | — | — |
| Enterprise | — | — |
| Advertising | 3% | — |
| E-commerce | low | — |
| Bio | — | — |

50% of total value of the top 100 YC companies comes from just the top 10. Five of those ten are marketplaces. The power law is steep.

---

### SaaS (Software as a Service)
**Description:** Cloud-based subscription software. Customers pay monthly or annually to access the software.

**When it works:** When the problem is recurring (not one-time), when customers need the software continuously to do their job, when churn can be kept low through ongoing value delivery.

**Key metrics:** Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR), churn rate, Net Revenue Retention (NRR), Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC), Lifetime Value (LTV).

**Margin expectations:** High (70–90% gross margins typical). Scales with software, not people.

**Why it builds winners:** Predictable revenue compounds. Customers keep paying until they explicitly cancel. At 95% monthly retention, you keep 54 of 100 customers after one year. At 90% monthly retention, you keep only 28. A 5% difference in monthly retention is often the difference between a growing business and a dying one.

**31% of YC top 100** — the most common model. High representation because recurring revenue creates predictable growth that compounds.

---

### Transactional
**Description:** Facilitates transactions and takes a cut. Most commonly fintech companies. Revenue tied directly to transaction volume.

**When it works:** When you can position yourself as the platform through which money flows. Works best when you become critical infrastructure — when ripping you out would be painful.

**Key metrics:** Gross Payment Volume (GPV), take rate, transaction volume, revenue per transaction.

**Margin expectations:** Lower gross margins than SaaS (often 40–60%) but extremely high volume can compensate.

**Why it builds winners:** Being in the flow of funds makes it easy to take your cut. Companies like Stripe become so embedded that switching cost is enormous. Transactional companies become "top three problems" for their customers — the thing they would never risk disrupting.

**22% of YC top 100, 29% of total value.** Outperforms on value because of proximity to the transaction.

---

### Marketplace
**Description:** Facilitates transactions between buyers and sellers (two-sided marketplace). Revenue typically from take rate on transactions.

**When it works:** When there is fragmented supply and fragmented demand that needs to be matched, when network effects are possible, when the market is large enough to support winner-take-all dynamics.

**Key metrics:** Gross Merchandise Value (GMV), take rate, supply-side retention, demand-side retention, liquidity (does every buyer find what they need?).

**Margin expectations:** Variable. Take rates typically range from 5–30% depending on the market. Gross margins can be high once marketplace is liquid.

**Why it builds winners:** Network effects. Each new seller makes the platform more valuable for buyers; each new buyer makes it more valuable for sellers. Once a marketplace hits critical mass, it becomes the default. See details below.

**14% of YC top 100, 30% of total value.** Overrepresented in top 10 because of winner-take-all dynamics.

---

### Hard Tech
**Description:** Companies building physical technology with long development cycles — robotics, aerospace, autonomous vehicles, advanced manufacturing.

**When it works:** When the technical barrier is the moat. When replicating what you build requires years of R&D and significant capital.

**Key metrics:** Technical milestones, regulatory approval timelines, burn rate against development milestones.

**Margin expectations:** Low in early phases (capital-intensive development). Can be high at scale if manufacturing is optimized.

**Moat source:** Technical innovation. The time required to replicate the technology creates a durable competitive advantage. Examples: Cruise (self-driving), Boom (supersonic jets).

---

### Usage-Based
**Description:** Customers pay based on how much they use the product. Revenue scales directly with customer activity.

**When it works:** When usage is easy to measure, when customers have variable needs, when usage correlates with the value they receive.

**Key metrics:** Usage volume, revenue per unit of usage, Net Revenue Retention (NRR should exceed 100% as customers expand usage).

**Margin expectations:** Depends on underlying infrastructure cost. Cloud infrastructure costs can erode margins at scale.

---

### Enterprise
**Description:** Selling software or services to large organizations. Long sales cycles, custom contracts, dedicated customer success.

**When it works:** When the problem is a "top three" organizational priority, when purchasing decisions involve procurement and legal, when churn is low once a contract is signed.

**Key metrics:** Annual Contract Value (ACV), pipeline size, sales cycle length, churn rate.

**Margin expectations:** High gross margins on software, but offset by high sales and customer success costs.

**Note:** Once sold in, enterprise churn is low. Long sales cycles are painful but create durable revenue.

---

### Advertising
**Description:** Revenue from displaying ads. Users are the product; advertisers are the customer.

**When it works:** Only when you expect to become a top 10 destination on the internet. Requires organic virality and massive scale.

**Key metrics:** Daily/Monthly Active Users (DAU/MAU), CPM, fill rate, engagement metrics.

**Margin expectations:** High at massive scale. Economically unviable at small scale.

**Warning:** Only 3% of YC top 100 use advertising as the primary model. Do not use advertising as a business model unless you expect to become the dominant hub for a large audience. Reddit and Twitch have advertising models that work because they became the place where communities form — not because advertising is a good early-stage model. If you are not planning to be a top 10 internet destination, choose a different model.

---

### E-commerce
**Description:** Selling physical products online.

**When it works:** When there is a product innovation or distribution advantage.

**Key metrics:** Gross margin on product, Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC), repeat purchase rate, inventory turnover.

**Margin expectations:** Low (typically 20–50% gross margins). Capital-intensive due to inventory.

**Warning:** Barely registers in YC top 100. Low margins and capital requirements make scaling difficult.

---

### Bio
**Description:** Biotech, pharmaceuticals, life sciences. Long development cycles with regulatory approval requirements.

**When it works:** When proprietary science creates a durable IP moat.

**Key metrics:** Clinical trial milestones, regulatory pathway, IP portfolio.

**Margin expectations:** Very high if a product reaches market. Near-zero revenue for years during development.

## Marketplace Dynamics

Marketplaces are the most likely business model to build winner-take-all companies. Five of the YC top 10 are marketplaces. Understand how they work before building one.

**Winner-take-all tendency:**

Once a marketplace reaches critical mass, it dominates because users go where everyone else already is. If you want to rent an apartment short-term, you go to Airbnb because that is where all the inventory is. If you want to buy or sell NFTs, you go to OpenSea because that is where all the buyers and sellers are. The dominant marketplace has more supply, more demand, more trust, and lower friction than any competitor — creating a cycle that compounds.

**Chicken-and-egg problem:**

You cannot attract buyers without sellers. You cannot attract sellers without buyers. This is the founding challenge of every marketplace.

How to solve it:
- Solve one side manually first. Seed the supply side (or demand side) yourself before building the other side.
- Constrain the market geographically. Launch in one city or one vertical, where you can achieve liquidity with less supply and demand.
- Find participants who benefit from being on both sides.
- Use money (credits, subsidies, guaranteed minimums) to attract the harder-to-recruit side until network effects take over.
- Be the demand yourself initially (DoorDash founders placed their own delivery orders to prove the concept).

**Which side to focus on first:**

Usually supply. Supply creates the inventory that buyers come for. However, this depends on the market — in some markets, locking in buyers (demand) through contracts or exclusive relationships first is more effective. Analyze which side is the harder bottleneck in your specific market.

**Take rate expectations:**

Typical marketplace take rates: 5–30%, depending on transaction value and competitive landscape. Higher take rates are sustainable when the marketplace provides significant value (trust, payments, insurance, discovery). Lower take rates are required in commodity markets where participants can transact directly if the platform's fee is too high.

## 5 Pricing Rules

### Rule 1: Charge

This is the most common mistake. Founders are afraid of hearing "no" on price, so they offer free products, free trials, or unpaid pilots to avoid the conversation.

**Why charging is essential:**

- Charging is the fastest way to learn if users see value in your product. It is binary: they either open their wallet or they do not.
- Free users provide unreliable feedback. They have nothing at stake and will not tell you the hard truth.
- If users will not pay, you have not built enough value. This is the critical signal. Do not suppress it with a free tier.
- Charging reveals which customer segment values your product most. If segment A pays and segment B does not, you have learned which market to pursue.

**For B2B, replace free trial with money-back guarantee:**

Consumer free trials work because credit cards are required upfront and users forget to cancel. For B2B, the equivalent mechanism is: charge full price, offer 30–60 day money-back guarantee, or allow opt-out from annual to monthly. This filters out window-shoppers while removing the risk objection.

---

### Rule 2: Price on Value, Not Cost

**Three variables:**
- **Cost:** What it costs you to serve the customer
- **Price:** What you charge
- **Perceived value:** What the customer believes they gain

Cost-plus pricing (cost + fixed markup = price) is the wrong approach. It ignores the gap between your price and the value the customer perceives.

**The correct framing:**
- The gap between cost and price is your margin.
- The gap between price and perceived value is money you are leaving on the table.

**Four value categories customers care about (in order of importance for B2B):**
1. Make more money
2. Save money / reduce costs
3. Move faster (reduce time-to-outcome)
4. Avoid risk / reduce headache

When you understand which of these your product delivers, and how much, you can price against that value rather than against your costs.

**How to find your value:**

Talk to users. Ask: "What problem were you hoping our product could solve for you?" and "What would happen to your business if you could not use it anymore?" Let them articulate the value. Their language tells you how to price and how to pitch.

---

### Rule 3: You Are Undercharging

Almost certainly true for any early-stage startup. Lower price is not a sustainable competitive advantage.

**Why low price is dangerous:**
- A large competitor can undercut you below your cost. They have more money. You will lose a price war.
- Low prices signal low value. Customers evaluating your product use price as a proxy for quality. A price lower than competitors suggests your product is worse.
- Higher margins mean you can outspend competitors on customer acquisition. If your margins are higher, you can afford to pay more per acquired customer, which means you can acquire all the customers before they do.

**The ideal price signal:** Customers complain about the price but still pay. This is the correct calibration. If customers immediately accept your price without hesitation, raise it.

**Raising prices is the easiest way to grow revenue:**

If you have 1,000 customers and want to double revenue, you can either acquire 1,000 more customers (hard, expensive) or double your price (change a number on a page). If your product justifies the higher price, do the latter.

**When users will not pay more:**

Either you need to build more value into the product, or you need to solve a bigger problem. "Nice to have" problems do not support high prices. "Top three priority" problems do.

**Exception — when to give a lower price:**
- First user: getting initial feedback justifies a discount.
- Recognizable logo: a marquee customer at a lower price provides social proof that unlocks full-price customers.
- Lock-in + renewal: charge less upfront if the product creates data lock-in and you can raise price at renewal.

---

### Rule 4: Pricing Is Not Permanent

Do not spend time trying to find the perfect price before you have customers. Find the right order of magnitude and iterate.

**Order of magnitude check:** If you are charging $10 and customers are willing to pay $100, you are off by an order of magnitude — fix it. If you are charging $10 and customers would pay $15–20, you are in the right ballpark. Do not optimize prematurely.

**How to raise prices on existing customers:**
- Grandfather existing customers at their current price and raise only for new customers.
- Give advance notice before raising prices. As long as your product continues to deliver value, most customers will absorb a price increase rather than churn.

Netflix has raised prices repeatedly on 221M subscribers. If they can do it at that scale, you can do it on your handful of early customers.

---

### Rule 5: Keep It Simple

Complexity in pricing creates friction. Friction reduces conversion. Fewer customers pay.

**What not to do:** Five pricing tiers with different feature sets, crossed-out prices, multiple billing periods on the same page, add-on modules with separate pricing, custom enterprise pricing with no published rates. Each additional variable is a question the customer has to answer before giving you money.

**What to do:** Three plans maximum. Clear names. Visible prices. One recommended tier highlighted. A visible call-to-action for each tier. GitLab is cited as a good example: three clean plans, clear pricing, no confusion.

## Segment Case Study: $120/year to $18,000/year

Segment (later acquired by Twilio for $3B+) helps companies capture and use customer data.

**Starting point:** Founders were engineers who thought they had to give the product away free to get adoption. Eventually decided to charge, set price at $10/month ($120/year). Reached out to free users sheepishly.

**Unexpected response:** Free users replied with messages like "I hope you would charge me more than that, otherwise I'm worried about keeping my customer data with you." The low price was signaling unreliability, not accessibility. Customers were worried a $120/year company could not be trusted with their data infrastructure.

**Advisor intervention:** Segment hired a sales advisor who told them to charge $120,000/year. The founders thought this was insane. The advisor said: "If you don't tell this customer the price is $120,000, I quit."

**The negotiation:** Founders told the customer the price was $120,000. Customer countered at $12,000. They settled at $18,000.

**Result:** 150x price increase. From $120/year to $18,000/year. Without this conversation, they would have charged $120. The mechanism: the advisor forced the founders to say a number they believed was too high. The customer's counter-offer proved the real market price was orders of magnitude above their self-imposed ceiling.

**Lesson:** Founders almost always set prices based on what they think customers can afford or what feels "fair," not based on the value the customer receives. The only way to find the real price ceiling is to ask for a number that feels too high and see what happens.

## Stripe Pricing Example

Stripe entered a market where competitors charged approximately 2.9% per transaction. Stripe charged 5%.

**The logic:** Stripe wanted to test how much value customers placed on one-click sign-up, fast onboarding, and high-quality developer API documentation. Rather than competing on price, they set a price that forced the product to prove its value.

**The result:** Customers paid the premium because the product was meaningfully better. Speed of integration and quality of documentation had real economic value for developers.

**Lesson:** Do not compete on price. Competing on price only works if you can sustain lower prices indefinitely. Compete on value. If your product is better, charge more. The higher price forces you to build a product that justifies it — and signals to customers that it does.

## Decision Framework: Choosing a Business Model

**Step 1: Match to market structure.**

- Fragmented buyers and sellers who need matching → Marketplace
- Businesses with a recurring software problem → SaaS
- Money flowing through your product → Transactional
- Massive consumer audience → Advertising (only if top-10 internet destination is plausible)
- Physical product → E-commerce or Hard Tech
- Large institutional buyer → Enterprise

**Step 2: Match to customer type.**

| Customer Type | Best-fit Models |
|---|---|
| Consumer | Marketplace, Advertising, E-commerce |
| SMB | SaaS, Transactional, Usage-Based |
| Mid-Market | SaaS, Transactional, Enterprise |
| Enterprise | Enterprise, SaaS |

**Step 3: Evaluate revenue predictability.**

- Need predictable revenue to plan hiring and growth → SaaS or Enterprise
- Revenue tied to market conditions or transaction volume → Transactional, Marketplace

**Step 4: Confirm it is not in the warning categories.**

Remove these options unless you have a specific reason they apply:
- Services / Consulting: Non-recurring revenue, scales with people not software, low margins. Can use as a temporary strategy to learn, not as a permanent model.
- Affiliate: Too far from the transaction. Too many steps between your work and getting paid. Commissions delayed 30–90 days.
- Hardware: High capital requirements, low margins. Only if the hardware itself is the technical moat.
- Platform-dependent: If your product is built on top of another company's platform and it works, that platform will shut you down and capture the revenue themselves.

**Step 5: Pick one model and go deep.**

Early-stage startups should have exactly one business model. Multiple models create strategic confusion and split focus. Add a second model only after the first is working and generating meaningful revenue.
