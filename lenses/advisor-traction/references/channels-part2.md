### [TRC:Ch11] Viral Marketing
**Source:** Traction (Weinberg/Mares), Chapter 15
**Core Principle:** Getting existing customers to refer others to your product. True virality (K > 1) means every user brings at least one more, creating exponential growth. Even K = 0.5 means you get two customers for every one you acquire. Success requires understanding viral math (viral coefficient + viral cycle time), building loops into the product, and constant A/B testing. This channel powered Facebook, Twitter, WhatsApp, Dropbox, Instagram, and Snapchat.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your product has inherent sharing mechanics (communication tools, social products); you can build viral loops into the product; you have engineering resources for constant A/B testing; your product creates value when shared; you have enough users to seed the loop
- AVOID WHEN: Your product isn't inherently viral and you're just bolting on "share" buttons; your product doesn't add value when shared; you aren't prepared for 2-3 months of optimization work; your product isn't good enough for people to recommend
- COMBINES WITH: [TRC:Ch9] Email Marketing (referral emails like Dropbox), [TRC:Ch15] Existing Platforms (social network sharing), [TRC:Ch4] SEM and [TRC:Ch7] SEO (seeding new users into viral loops)

**Key Tactics:**
1. **Types of viral loops:**
   - **Word of mouth** — Product so remarkable people naturally tell others (Facebook early college growth)
   - **Inherent virality** — Product valueless without others (Skype, WhatsApp, Snapchat). Network effects.
   - **Collaboration** — Product useful alone but better with others (Google Docs)
   - **Communicative** — Every message spreads product (Hotmail "Get free email" signature, Apple "Sent from iPhone")
   - **Incentivized** — Rewards for referrals (Dropbox free space, Uber/PayPal credits, Gilt account credits)
   - **Embedded** — Widgets and buttons (YouTube embed codes, Facebook/Twitter share buttons)
   - **Social broadcast** — User activities shown to connections (Spotify plays on Facebook, Pinterest pins)
2. **Viral math:**
   - K (viral coefficient) = i (invites per user) x conversion percentage. K > 1 = exponential growth. K > 0.5 = meaningful boost.
   - K = i x click-through % x signup %  (break down to find weakest link)
   - Viral cycle time = how long for one loop iteration. Shorter = dramatically faster growth. YouTube: minutes. Focus on shortening this FIRST.
3. **Optimization** — Map entire viral loop. Remove unnecessary steps. Focus weeks at a time on one metric. 1-3 of every 10 tests yield positive results. Even 1% improvement compounds.
4. **Viral pockets** — Calculate K on subsets (country, age group, trait). You may be viral in Indonesia but not Australia. Focus on pockets.
5. **Seeding** — Most loops aren't self-sustaining. Need constant stream of new users entering. Use SEM, SEO for seeding.
6. **Copy what works** — "The easiest way, for a beginner — copy someone else's viral loop down to the detail, including text copy." — Andrew Chen

**Key Examples:**
- **Dropbox** — Tested SEM ($230 CPA for $99 product), then pivoted to viral: referral program giving free space → biggest growth driver → tens of millions of users.
- **Hotmail** — "Get a free email account with Hotmail. Sign up now" in every email signature.
- **YouTube** — Embed codes on every video. Viral cycle time = minutes (see video, click, share). Explosive growth.
- **myZamana** — Grew from 100,000 to 4M+ users in <1 year using targeted emails triggered by user actions ("Mark liked you!").
- **Uber** — Combines inherent (riding with others), collaborative, and incentivized virality.
- **Facebook, Twitter, WhatsApp, Snapchat, Instagram, Pinterest** — All leveraged viral loops through super-platforms.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify which viral loop types naturally fit your product (don't force it)
2. Build the loop into the product — not as an afterthought
3. Measure baseline K and viral cycle time
4. Map every step of the loop; remove friction at each step
5. Focus on shortening viral cycle time first (biggest impact)
6. Run 5+ A/B tests per week on weakest metric
7. Find viral pockets in your user data
8. Use other channels (SEM, SEO) to seed new users into the loop
9. Plan for 2-3 months of dedicated engineering optimization

**Metrics:** Viral coefficient (K), viral cycle time, invites sent per user, click-through rate on invites, signup conversion rate, organic vs paid growth ratio, K by user segment (viral pockets)

**Reversal:** Stops working when your K drops below useful levels, when platforms change sharing rules (Facebook reducing viral reach), when users get fatigued by invites, or when you run out of addressable market. Also fails if bolted on to a product that isn't inherently shareable.

### [TRC:Ch12] Business Development (BD)
**Source:** Traction (Weinberg/Mares), Chapter 17
**Core Principle:** Creating strategic partnerships that benefit both parties. Unlike sales (exchanging dollars for product), BD focuses on exchanging value through partnerships. Even Google got its initial traction from two key BD deals (Netscape default search + Yahoo! search partnership). Requires strategic thinking, understanding partner incentives, building a pipeline, and discipline to avoid off-path deals.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You can clearly articulate mutual benefit; there are larger companies whose customers overlap with yours; you need distribution/inventory/technology that partners can provide; you're disciplined enough to reject deals that don't align with your Critical Path
- AVOID WHEN: You don't understand potential partners' incentives; you'd pursue any deal just because a big company is interested; you can't spare the time for relationship building; you have nothing of value to offer partners
- COMBINES WITH: [TRC:Ch1] Targeting Blogs (content partnerships like Mint + Motley Fool), [TRC:Ch15] Existing Platforms (integration partnerships), [TRC:Ch14] Affiliate Programs (distribution deals), [TRC:Ch13] Sales (BD opens doors for sales)

**Key Tactics:**
1. **Types of BD partnerships:**
   - **Standard partnerships** — Work together to improve products (Apple + Nike = Nike+ shoe)
   - **Joint ventures** — Create entirely new product (Starbucks + Pepsi = bottled Frappuccino)
   - **Licensing** — Use brand/content (Spotify licensing music from labels)
   - **Distribution deals** — Access to partner's customers (Kayak + AOL → Kayak powered AOL travel search)
   - **Supply partnerships** — Secure key inputs (Half.com sourcing used books)
2. **Build a pipeline of 50-100 potential partners** — Exhaustive list. Categorize by attributes (revenue, distribution reach), not names. Prioritize 10-20.
3. **Understand partner incentives** — "You need to understand what's on their side of the table." Research their business. Chris Fralic literally worked in a used bookstore for a day.
4. **Get warm introductions** — Send investors, friends, advisers your partner list for intros.
5. **Keep term sheets simple** — One page. Fewer lawyers = easier partnering.
6. **"How the deal was done" memo** — Document key contacts, sticking points, what interested the prospect. Improve process.
7. **Low-touch BD at scale** — After landing initial traditional deals, offer APIs, embed codes, feeds to standardize. Delicious: traditional deals with WashPost + Mozilla → then public API for thousands of sites.

**Key Examples:**
- **Google** — 1999: Netscape default search + Yahoo! search partnership = critical to becoming world's largest search engine.
- **Kayak** — Distribution deal with AOL (Kayak powered AOL travel search) drove early traction. Later: hotel chains, rental agencies.
- **Half.com** — Chris Fralic built supply partnerships for 1M books/movies before launch. Distribution through affiliate programs. Launched on Today show. Acquired by eBay for $300M+.
- **Delicious** — WashPost integration → Mozilla Firefox extension (tripled user base) → public API for thousands of sites.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Define your traction goal and critical path — only pursue deals that align
2. Create exhaustive list of 50-100 potential partners, categorized by attributes
3. Research each partner's business, incentives, and pain points
4. Get warm introductions through investors, friends, advisers
5. Approach with clear value proposition for THEM
6. Keep negotiations simple (one-page term sheets)
7. Land 2-3 traditional deals, then standardize with APIs/embeds for scale
8. Document "how the deal was done" for each completed deal

**Metrics:** Number of partners in pipeline, deals closed, revenue/traffic from partnerships, time to close, partner satisfaction, leads generated through partnerships

**Reversal:** Stops working when deals don't align with your Critical Path (wasted resources on distracting partnerships), when partners lose interest or priorities change, or when you can't deliver enough value to maintain the relationship.

### [TRC:Ch13] Sales
**Source:** Traction (Weinberg/Mares), Chapter 18
**Core Principle:** Generate leads, qualify them, and convert them into paying customers through direct interaction. Essential for enterprise and expensive products. First customers often come from picking up the phone and cold calling. Scaling requires a repeatable sales model: leads → qualification → closing, designed from the customer's perspective. SPIN Selling framework for initial conversations.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You sell enterprise or expensive products (>$250/month); customers need interpersonal interaction before purchase; you're targeting businesses (B2B); you need first 5-20 enterprise customers
- AVOID WHEN: Your product is low-cost consumer with high-volume needs (use channels that scale without human touch); you can't afford salespeople; your product doesn't require explanation
- COMBINES WITH: [TRC:Ch10] Engineering as Marketing (tools generate qualified leads), [TRC:Ch9] Email Marketing (lead nurturing), [TRC:Ch2] Publicity (builds credibility for sales conversations), [TRC:Ch16] Trade Shows (face-to-face with prospects)

**Key Tactics:**
1. **SPIN Selling framework:**
   - **Situation** — Learn buying context (1-2 questions only: "How many employees? How is org structured?")
   - **Problem** — Clarify pain points ("Happy with current solution? What problems do you face?")
   - **Implication** — Make problem feel larger/urgent ("How many people impacted? What's the cost of not solving this?")
   - **Need-payoff** — Focus on your solution's benefits ("How would this help you? Whose life would improve?")
2. **First customers** — "You get your first customers by picking up the phone." Target mid-level employees who have perspective + authority, not the very top.
3. **Cold calling/emailing** — Set daily/weekly targets. Be judicious about who you contact. Junior employees = same mental cost, much lower success.
4. **Free consulting as sales** — Guidewire spent 7-10 man-days with 40+ insurance companies, providing free benchmarking assessments in exchange for product feedback.
5. **Sales funnel design:**
   - Top: generate leads through other traction channels (not cold calling for scale)
   - Middle: qualify leads (A/B/C buckets — Mark Suster: A=close in 3 months 66-75% of time, B=3-12 months, C=marketing only)
   - Bottom: close with time-line commitments ("We'll set up pilot in 2 weeks. If it works, you'll buy. Yes or no?")
6. **Remove blockages** — SaaS (no IT install), free trials, demo videos, FAQs, testimonials, email campaigns, webinars, low intro price, no committee decisions.
7. **Avoid wasting time on:** free consulting requests from people who won't buy, and "change agents" who claim to transform their company but have never brought technology in before.

**Key Examples:**
- **JBoss** — Open source middleware. Free documentation (previously charged) in exchange for contact info → 10K+ leads/month. Qualify with Eloqua (marketing automation). Inside sales closed 25% (vs industry 7-10%). $65M revenue in 2 years. Acquired by Red Hat for $350M.
- **HubSpot** — Uses lead qualification to determine investment per lead. Etsy/eBay sellers get less time than larger businesses.
- **Guidewire** — Free consulting studies for 40+ insurance companies → deep understanding of customer needs → built product they knew market wanted.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. List 20 local prospective customers. Get warm intros where possible.
2. Use SPIN selling in conversations. Focus on THEIR problem, not your product.
3. Qualify leads into A/B/C buckets. Focus 66-75% of sales time on A deals.
4. Get yes/no commitments at each stage with clear timelines
5. Build repeatable funnel: lead gen (other channels) → qualification → closing
6. Map blockages in funnel and remove them
7. Use lead nurturing (email, webinars, blog) for C leads until they're ready
8. Document what works and iterate

**Metrics:** Leads generated, lead qualification rate, close rate, average deal size, sales cycle length, CPA, LTV:CAC ratio, blockage rates at each funnel stage

**Reversal:** Stops working when sales costs exceed customer value, when your product doesn't require explanation (use self-service), or when you're calling on prospects who don't have budget, authority, or need.

### [TRC:Ch14] Affiliate Programs
**Source:** Traction (Weinberg/Mares), Chapter 19
**Core Principle:** Pay people or companies for performing specific actions (sales, leads). Risk is on the affiliate, not you — you only pay for results. Amazon, Zappos, Netflix use this. Three types: retail (4-8.5% per sale), information products (up to 75% commission on ClickBank), and lead generation ($26B industry). Best for e-commerce, info products, and membership programs.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You know how much you can spend to acquire a customer; you have a product affiliates would want to promote; you sell e-commerce, info products, or memberships; you want pay-for-performance marketing with no upfront risk
- AVOID WHEN: You don't know your unit economics; you can't afford commission rates that motivate affiliates; your product is hard to explain or has a very long sales cycle; you're too early to have a clear conversion path
- COMBINES WITH: [TRC:Ch9] Email Marketing (affiliates with email lists), [TRC:Ch1] Targeting Blogs (bloggers as affiliates), [TRC:Ch12] Business Development (distribution deals), [TRC:Ch8] Content Marketing (content creators as affiliates)

**Key Tactics:**
1. **Use existing networks** — Commission Junction ($2K setup), ClickBank ($50 for digital products), ShareASale ($500), Pepperjam ($1K). Already have affiliates signed up.
2. **Simple payout structure** — Start with flat fee per conversion or percentage of sale. Get complex later (tiered, seasonal).
3. **Types of affiliates:**
   - Coupon/deal sites (RetailMeNot, Slickdeals)
   - Loyalty programs (Upromise, Ebates)
   - Aggregators (Nextag, PriceGrabber)
   - Email lists
   - Vertical blogs/sites
4. **Recruit from customer base first** — They already know and like your product.
5. **Build relationships with content creators** — Help them first (guest posts, free product access). Maneesh Sethi: single email to his list → $30K in 2 years for one product. Referred 3,000+ people to RescueTime.
6. **Product as currency** — Instead of cash, give features or extended subscriptions (Dropbox: free space, QuiBids: free bids).
7. **No risk for you** — Credit card declined = zero commission. Invalid lead form = no payment.

**Key Examples:**
- **Pepperjam** — Kris Jones grew it to 4th largest affiliate network. Had a single advertiser generating $50M annually. Acquired by eBay.
- **Maneesh Sethi (HacktheSystem)** — As an affiliate, one email about an SEO program → $30K in 2 years. Referred 3,000+ to RescueTime.
- **Amazon** — 4-8.5% commission, tiered by volume. Model for retail affiliate programs.
- **ClickBank** — Largest network for digital products. 100K+ affiliates, commissions up to 75%.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Calculate your CPA ceiling (how much can you afford per customer?)
2. Register on the most relevant affiliate network for your product type
3. Set simple payout: flat fee or percentage, below your CPA ceiling
4. Recruit first 20 affiliates from the network. Walk each through your product personally.
5. Contact existing customers who might be good affiliates
6. Reach out to content creators (bloggers, email list owners) in your niche
7. Track performance, reward top affiliates with higher rates or bonuses

**Metrics:** Number of active affiliates, sales/leads per affiliate, revenue from affiliate channel, CPA through affiliates vs other channels, affiliate churn rate, ROI per affiliate

**Reversal:** Stops working when commission rates aren't attractive enough to motivate affiliates, when affiliate quality degrades (spammy/fraudulent leads), or when managing the program becomes more expensive than the revenue it generates.

### [TRC:Ch15] Existing Platforms
**Source:** Traction (Weinberg/Mares), Chapter 20
**Core Principle:** Leverage mega-platforms (Apple App Store, Google Play, Facebook, Twitter, etc.) with hundreds of millions of users. Fill gaps that platforms don't serve. Be first on new platforms for maximum advantage. Every major platform has stories of startups that built massive user bases by being early and filling gaps.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your target customers are concentrated on specific platforms; you can fill a gap the platform doesn't serve; a new platform is emerging and you can be first; your product naturally complements a platform's ecosystem
- AVOID WHEN: The platform is already saturated with competitors; you'd be building entirely on someone else's foundation without diversification; platform changes could destroy your business overnight
- COMBINES WITH: [TRC:Ch11] Viral Marketing (social sharing on platforms), [TRC:Ch5] Social and Display Ads (platform advertising), [TRC:Ch2] Publicity (platform features generate press)

**Key Tactics:**
1. **App store optimization:**
   - Get enough initial traction to show in rankings → organic downloads → higher rankings → more downloads (virtuous cycle)
   - Release free version to boost downloads (Trainyard: free version → #1 Italy → #1 UK → Apple feature → 50x downloads)
   - Ask for ratings after providing value. Consistently high ratings = editors' choice.
   - Use other channels to drive initial installs (blogs, publicity, social ads)
2. **Be first on new platforms** — Evernote strategy: "We really killed ourselves to always be in all App Store launches on day one." First on iPhone, iPad, Android, Kindle Fire. Each new platform = free marketing from platform's own launch push.
3. **Fill platform gaps** — YouTube filled Myspace's video gap → redirect clicks to YouTube. Bitly shortened links for Twitter. Imgur hosted images for reddit.
4. **Target existing large platforms** — Airbnb: "Post to Craigslist" feature drove tens of thousands of users. PayPal: bought items on eBay requiring PayPal payment → became more popular than eBay's own system.
5. **Browser extensions** — Evernote browser extension → 205% increase in web usage.
6. **Create platform-specific features** — Evernote Peek used iPad Smart Cover → Apple showcased it in a commercial → #1 education app → 500K+ new users.
7. **Target emerging platforms** — "When a platform is popular, it's crowded." Zynga dominated early Facebook (impossible to replicate today).

**Key Examples:**
- **Evernote** — First on iPhone, iPad, Android, Kindle Fire. Featured in Android store for 6 weeks straight → hundreds of thousands of new customers. Evernote Peek: Apple commercial → 500K new users. Browser extensions: 205% web usage increase.
- **YouTube** — Filled Myspace's video gap. Users could upload and embed videos easily. Clicks redirected to YouTube.
- **Airbnb** — "Post to Craigslist" feature → tens of thousands of Craigslist users redirected to Airbnb.
- **PayPal** — Targeted eBay users. Bought items requiring PayPal. Became more popular than eBay's own payment system.
- **Trainyard** — Free version → #1 Italy → #1 UK → Apple feature → millions of downloads. 50x spike from feature.
- **Socialcam** — Suggested Facebook/Twitter signup → 60M users in 12 months.
- **Imgur** — Built image hosting for reddit → explosion in usage.
- **Bitly** — Shortened links for Twitter → massive adoption.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Figure out where your target customers hang out online (major and niche platforms)
2. Identify gaps in those platforms your product could fill
3. If a new platform is emerging, bet on being there first (even if it might flop)
4. For app stores: release free version, drive initial downloads through other channels, optimize ratings
5. Build features specifically for the platform (not just a port)
6. Think about what platform editors/curators would want to feature
7. Consider browser extensions if your product adds value across web browsing

**Metrics:** Platform rankings, download/install rate, feature placement, organic vs paid installs, user retention from platform channel, rating/review scores

**Reversal:** Stops working when platforms change rules (Airbnb/Craigslist integration shut down), when platforms get saturated (impossible to replicate Zynga's Facebook growth today), or when your platform bet fails (Evernote on Nokia, Windows, BlackBerry). Diversify across platforms to mitigate risk.

### [TRC:Ch16] Trade Shows
**Source:** Traction (Weinberg/Mares), Chapter 21
**Core Principle:** Showcase products in person at industry events. One of the few times nearly everyone in your industry is in one place. Useful at every phase: early for customer development (bring prototypes), later for major announcements, sales, and partnerships. Can move the needle in a matter of days.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your industry has established trade shows; you sell B2B or physical products; you need face-to-face interactions for sales or partnerships; your target customers are geographically diverse; you want to meet press, partners, and customers simultaneously
- AVOID WHEN: There are no relevant trade shows; your product requires no explanation; the cost doesn't justify the expected ROI; you're a purely online consumer product with no industry events
- COMBINES WITH: [TRC:Ch2] Publicity (press at trade shows), [TRC:Ch12] Business Development (partnership meetings), [TRC:Ch13] Sales (selling at shows), [TRC:Ch18] Speaking Engagements (speaking at events)

**Key Tactics:**
1. **Visit before exhibiting** — Attend as guest the year before to evaluate. If impossible, ask previous exhibitors about quality.
2. **Set goals first** — Brad Feld: Are you trying to get press, investors, customers, partnerships? Goals drive which events and how you approach them.
3. **Work backward from ROI** — If 10K attendees, 30% fit your profile = 3,000 targets. Cost $10K, product $5K → profitable at 3rd sale. If product is $50, probably not worth it.
4. **Schedule meetings ahead of time** — Jason Cohen: "Set up meetings! Trade shows are a rare chance for face time." Email key attendees, journalists, bloggers, customers, vendors, competitors, partners. "5 meetings per day minimum."
5. **Host dinners and drinks** — Mark Suster: invite 3-4 customers + 3-4 prospects + interesting guests. Pick a killer venue. Co-sponsor with other startups to split costs.
6. **Booth strategy** — Location matters for visibility. Impressive display, banner, business cards, compelling demo. Pre-show discount cards to drive booth traffic (Jason Cohen).
7. **Outbound strategy on the floor** — Don't just wait for walk-ups. RJMetrics: 72% of conversations came from proactive outreach (3-4x more value than walk-ups alone).
8. **Bags with your name** — Give out bags to attendees carrying armloads of materials. Your name displayed everywhere.
9. **Call to action on everything** — Every business card, handout, giveaway should have unique link (mobile optimized) with enticing offer.

**Key Examples:**
- **SureStop** — Attended early trade shows with just prototypes → learned technical requirements and price points from industry conversations. Later formed manufacturing relationship with Jamis (major bike manufacturer) at a trade show → 20,000+ sales. "All started and grew through this traction channel."
- **Smart Bear Software** — Jason Cohen: pre-show discount cards, 5+ meetings/day, face time with magazine editors led to coverage in every major programming magazine.
- **RJMetrics** — Outbound strategy on the floor: 72% of conversations were from proactive outreach, yielding 3-4x more value.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. List all relevant industry trade shows for the year
2. Set clear goals for each (press, sales, partnerships, customer development)
3. Budget by quarter; work backward from ROI expectations
4. Visit promising shows as guest first; ask previous exhibitors for feedback
5. Schedule 5+ meetings per day before the show (email attendees, press, bloggers)
6. Prepare impressive booth, demo, giveaways with strong CTAs
7. Have outbound strategy: walk the floor, start conversations
8. Host dinner or drinks with key prospects and customers
9. Follow up with every contact within 48 hours

**Metrics:** Meetings scheduled vs completed, leads generated, deals closed, partnerships formed, press coverage, cost per lead, ROI per event

**Reversal:** Stops working when trade shows decline in your industry, when costs outweigh returns, or when your audience shifts to purely online engagement. Also fails if you only wait for walk-ups without proactive outreach.

### [TRC:Ch17] Offline Events
**Source:** Traction (Weinberg/Mares), Chapter 22
**Core Principle:** Sponsor or run events — from small meetups to large conferences — to engage directly with potential customers, build relationships with power users, and shorten sales cycles. Especially effective when your target customers don't respond well to online advertising. Few startups do this, which means less competition.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your target customers have shared interests and a need for community; you sell enterprise/B2B with long sales cycles; your audience doesn't congregate online; you want to position yourself as an industry leader; you need deep personal connections
- AVOID WHEN: Your customers have no shared identity or reason to meet; you can't afford the logistics; you're purely online consumer with no community angle; you need massive scale immediately
- COMBINES WITH: [TRC:Ch18] Speaking Engagements (speak at your own events), [TRC:Ch2] Publicity (events generate press), [TRC:Ch19] Community Building (events strengthen community), [TRC:Ch12] Business Development (events attract partners)

**Key Tactics:**
1. **Conference launching** — Twitter at SXSW: visualization screens + "join sxsw" text feature. $11K = 20K→60K tweets/day + SXSW Web Award + press coverage.
2. **Create your own conference** — Eric Ries: Startup Lessons Learned. Tested demand first (asked blog readers). One day, few speakers. Live-streamed to meetup groups across country → helped make Lean Startup a bestseller.
3. **High-end events for enterprise** — Enservio: Claims Innovation Summit at Ritz-Carlton with industry leaders as speakers. Attracted top decision makers, established company as industry leader overnight.
4. **Mini-conferences** — One day, 3 local speakers, university lecture hall. <$500. Great way to test.
5. **Meetups** — Seth Godin: organized Linchpin meetups in cities across the country → 10,000+ attendees. Lean Startup Circle: meetups from first conference's live stream still running years later.
6. **Parties** — Evite: threw massive Bay Area party, organized via Evite → exposed product to target customers. Yelp: VIP parties where Yelp Elites got first RSVP → incentivized more site activity.
7. **Startup meetups** — Hardware Startup Meetup: first meeting at TechShop, 60 people, $70 for pizza → now 2,600+ members.
8. **Ticket pricing** — Higher price = higher quality attendees (successful businesses, not tire-kickers). Rob Walling learned this with MicroConf.
9. **Intimate format** — Speakers sit with attendees at lunch, roundtable discussions, no mob after talks.

**Key Examples:**
- **Twitter** — SXSW 2007: $11K for visualization screens → 20K to 60K tweets/day, SXSW Web Award, press coverage.
- **Eric Ries** — Startup Lessons Learned conference → live-streamed to meetups → Lean Startup Circle meetups still running years later → helped Lean Startup become bestseller.
- **Enservio** — Claims Innovation Summit at Ritz-Carlton with top industry speakers → attracted decision makers, established industry leadership overnight.
- **MicroConf** — Rob Walling: sells out in days, hundreds of founders. Facebook/AdWords ads didn't work. Only relational marketing worked initially.
- **Yelp** — VIP parties: Yelp Elites got first RSVP, free food/merchandise → incentivized more site activity.
- **Evite** — Bay Area party for Internet celebrity Mahir Cagri → exposed Evite to target customers.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Start small: host a one-day mini-conference or meetup ($70-$500)
2. Invite 3 local speakers relevant to your audience
3. Test demand before investing (ask your audience, sell tickets early)
4. If successful, scale up. Find sponsors to offset costs.
5. Consider live-streaming to expand reach
6. For enterprise: host exclusive, high-quality events that attract decision makers
7. Throw parties alongside conferences to build relationships
8. Keep quality high through pricing and curation

**Metrics:** Attendance, tickets sold, customer signups from event, partnerships formed, press coverage, community members acquired, cost per attendee, ROI

**Reversal:** Stops working when event quality declines, when events become too large and lose intimacy, or when your target audience stops attending in-person events. First events are hardest to sell because they're unproven.

### [TRC:Ch18] Speaking Engagements
**Source:** Traction (Weinberg/Mares), Chapter 23
**Core Principle:** Give talks at conferences, meetups, and events to get traction. Teaching sells. 45 minutes in front of a room of potential customers is time well spent. Start free at small events to build reputation, then leverage into larger engagements. Event organizers NEED speakers — you're doing them a favor. Can cement your place in an industry.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: There are events where your target customers gather; you have a compelling story or expertise to share; you want to position yourself as an industry leader; you sell enterprise/B2B where personal credibility matters
- AVOID WHEN: You have nothing unique to teach; your audience doesn't attend events; you can't commit time to preparing and delivering talks; your product doesn't benefit from personal credibility
- COMBINES WITH: [TRC:Ch17] Offline Events (create your own events), [TRC:Ch16] Trade Shows (speak at trade shows), [TRC:Ch8] Content Marketing (talks as content), [TRC:Ch2] Publicity (talks generate press), [TRC:Ch12] Business Development (speakers' dinners for networking)

**Key Tactics:**
1. **Start free at small events** — Coworking spaces, nonprofits, local meetups. Build reputation. "To become a speaker you have to speak once." — Dan Martell.
2. **Ask organizers what they want** — Don't pitch your topic; ask what topics they need covered, then craft the perfect pitch.
3. **Tell a story** — "All successful talks tell a story." Why you're doing what you're doing, how you got here, where things are going.
4. **Two core talks only** — Dan Martell: never does custom talks. Reuses and refines 2 talks. Each slide = 7-minute story with beginning, middle, end. Adjust by removing slides for shorter time slots.
5. **Do your own introduction** — Answer audience's two burning questions immediately: "Why are you important enough?" and "What value can you offer me?"
6. **Record everything** — 250 people in room vs thousands online from video. Clips attract future speaking invitations.
7. **Leverage social media during talks** — Include Twitter handle on every slide. Ask for "divided attention" — tweet insights during the presentation.
8. **Call to action at the end** — Ask audience to sign up for mailing list, visit a link, download slides.
9. **Pre-tweet slides** — Rand Fishkin tweets slides before every presentation → builds buzz before video is posted.
10. **Speakers' dinners** — If none scheduled, organize one yourself. Best networking happens at dinner.

**Key Examples:**
- **Eric Ries** — Used speaking engagements to hit bestseller list within a week of Lean Startup launch.
- **Dan Martell (Clarity)** — Two core talks about entrepreneurs getting advice. Founded two companies (Flowtown, Spheric), sold for millions — uses this in introduction. Leverages social media during talks.
- **Rand Fishkin (Moz)** — Tweets slides before every presentation to build pre-buzz.
- **Mark Zuckerberg** — Improving at public speaking vastly improved his management ability.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. List all events in your industry (premier, regional, local)
2. Start by speaking for free at 3 local meetups
3. Ask organizers what topics they want covered
4. Prepare 2 core talks: one for customers, one for partners
5. Each talk = compelling personal story + unique insights only you can give
6. Record every talk; post clips online
7. Include Twitter handle on every slide; ask audience to tweet
8. End with clear CTA (email signup, link to slides)
9. Attend/organize speakers' dinners for networking
10. Use successful small events as social proof to land bigger ones

**Metrics:** Talks given, audience size, social mentions during/after talks, email signups from CTA, video views, follow-up speaking invitations, customers acquired from speaking

**Reversal:** Stops working when you give too many talks (diminishing returns on same audiences), when your story becomes stale, or when virtual events reduce the impact of in-person speaking.

### [TRC:Ch19] Community Building
**Source:** Traction (Weinberg/Mares), Chapter 24
**Core Principle:** Invest in connections among your customers, foster relationships, and help them bring more people into your circle. Companies like Wikipedia, Stack Exchange, reddit, Yelp have built massive communities that ARE the product. Requires a mission people buy into, high quality standards, and fostering cross-connections. Community members become evangelists, product contributors, and even employees.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your company's core function is connecting people; your product improves with more user contributions; you have a mission people can buy into; you can maintain quality standards as community grows; you're willing to invest long-term in relationships
- AVOID WHEN: Your product doesn't benefit from user interaction; you can't maintain quality as community grows; you have no compelling mission; your business is purely transactional with no community angle
- COMBINES WITH: [TRC:Ch17] Offline Events (events strengthen community), [TRC:Ch18] Speaking Engagements (share mission), [TRC:Ch3] Unconventional PR (customer appreciation builds community), [TRC:Ch8] Content Marketing (community members create content)

**Key Tactics:**
1. **Start with existing audience** — Wikipedia from Nupedia users. Stack Overflow from Joel Spolsky's and Jeff Atwood's blog readers (7,000 name submissions!). Startup Digest from 22 friends.
2. **Have a mission** — "We had a manifesto, and an idea of what we wanted to accomplish. And people bought into the vision because it was about them being awesome." — Jeff Atwood
3. **Foster cross-connections** — Jeff Atwood's biggest mistake at Stack Overflow: not creating Meta (place to discuss the community) from day one. "I was telling the people who loved Stack Overflow the most to basically f**k off."
4. **Personal touch** — Alexis Ohanian (reddit): personally emailed every user who wrote about reddit, sent shirts/stickers, organized open bar tours for redditors.
5. **Set quality standards early** — Stack Overflow: strict guidelines for practical, answerable questions. Users policed the site on their own. Wikipedia: strict article guidelines. Quibb: invite-only model.
6. **Bootstrap off meetups** — Chris McCann (Startup Digest): 20-second pitches at local startup events → grew from 22 friends to 250,000+ members.
7. **Open source your product** — GitHub: free for open source → beloved by developers → drove early adoption. Tom Preston-Werner: open-sourcing code generates free advertising and goodwill.
8. **Community as hiring pipeline** — Every DuckDuckGo employee was a community member first.
9. **Prevent quality decay** — Common: quality starts strong, gets diluted as evangelists leave or are drowned out by new members. Set standards that scale.

**Key Examples:**
- **Stack Exchange/Stack Overflow** — Jeff Atwood & Joel Spolsky: solicited community feedback at every step. Community voted on name (7,000 submissions). Strict quality guidelines. Largest repository of programming Q&A in history.
- **reddit** — Alexis Ohanian: personal emails, free shirts, stickers, open bar tours. Karma system for quality. Community-driven content.
- **Wikipedia** — Started from Nupedia user group. Strict article guidelines. Largest repository of human knowledge.
- **Startup Digest** — Chris McCann: started with 22 friends, grew to 250,000+ through 20-second pitches at meetups. Content curated by community members in each city.
- **Yelp** — VIP parties for Yelp Elites. Reviews ARE the product. Active community engagement.
- **GitHub** — Free for open source projects → massive developer community → $7.5B acquisition by Microsoft.
- **DuckDuckGo** — Every employee was a community member first.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Define a compelling mission that's bigger than your product
2. Identify initial evangelists: existing audience, complementary communities, meetup attendees
3. Create a space for community cross-connection (forum, Meta, Discord)
4. Set strict quality standards from day one; build tools for community self-policing
5. Personally engage with early community members (emails, gifts, events)
6. Bootstrap off local events with short pitches
7. Give community members ownership (voting, content creation, governance)
8. Prevent quality decay by enforcing standards as you grow
9. Consider open-sourcing if your product benefits from developer community

**Metrics:** Community size, active member rate, content quality scores, evangelist count, member retention, new members from community referrals, user-generated content volume, community-sourced hires

**Reversal:** Stops working when quality declines and evangelists leave (death spiral), when the community outgrows your ability to moderate, or when your mission becomes unclear. Also fails for products without inherent community value (advertising, retargeting).
