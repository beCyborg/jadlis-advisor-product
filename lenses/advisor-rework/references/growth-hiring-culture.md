# Growth, Hiring & Culture — Staying Small on Purpose (Takedowns, Go, Evolution, Promotion, Hiring, Damage Control, Culture, Conclusion)

The Rework case for revenue-funded, deliberately-small companies: don't grow for
its own sake, refuse outside money, say no by default, hire only when it hurts,
promote by teaching, and let culture emerge from behavior. All principles
paraphrased; cite with `[RW:…]`.

Tag map: `[RW:TD]` Takedowns, `[RW:GO]` Go, `[RW:EV]` Evolution, `[RW:PM]`
Promotion, `[RW:HR]` Hiring, `[RW:DC]` Damage Control, `[RW:CU]` Culture,
`[RW:CN]` Conclusion. Dot notation = principle, e.g. `[RW:HR.2]` = Hire when it
hurts.

## Business model & sustainability

### Why grow? — small is a destination `[RW:TD.4]`

Bigger headcount reads as "impressive," but expansion for its own sake is ego,
not strategy ("economies of scale" isn't a good enough answer). We don't judge
Harvard or Oxford by how many campuses they open — so why judge a business that
way? Maybe the right size is five people, or forty, or just you and a laptop.

- Don't pre-decide your size; grow slow and see what feels right — *premature
  hiring kills companies*, and huge growth spurts make you skip past your right
  size. Once big, shrinking means layoffs, wrecked morale, and reinvention.
- Growth isn't just headcount — it's expenses, rent, IT, furniture. Lock in fixed
  costs and you force yourself into a complex, stressful business. Small is not a
  stepping-stone; it's a great place to stay. Anyone running a sustainable,
  profitable business — big or small — should be proud.

### Outside money is Plan Z `[RW:GO.7]`

Most people reach for outside funding first; make it your *last* resort. Service
businesses (consultants, software, designers) barely need capital to start. Take
as little outside cash as you can, because raising money has a noose attached:

- You give up control and end up taking orders from investors.
- "Cashing out" trumps building quality — investors want their money back fast
  (usually 3–5 years), so long-term sustainability dies.
- It's addictive — you run out, go back, and they take more of your company each
  time; early on you have no leverage, so it's a bad deal.
- Customers drop down the totem pole — you build what investors want, not what
  customers want.
- It's a massive distraction — months of pitches, legal work, and contracts when
  you should be building. Owners who take this road often regret the
  investment-hangover. Look for another way first.

This is the funding half of `[RW:GO.9]` Start a business, not a startup
(profit-from-day-one; see `anti-planning-launch.md`). Together they make
37signals the reference case for a revenue-funded company.

## Evolution — steering the product

### Say no by default `[RW:EV.1]`

Yes is easy — yes to a feature, an optimistic deadline, a mediocre design — until
the yes-pile buries what you should be doing. Build the habit of saying no, even
to good ideas, to keep priorities straight. You rarely regret a no; you often
regret a yes. People dodge no because confrontation is uncomfortable, but the
alternative (dragging things out, working on ideas you don't believe in) is
worse — take the brief discomfort up front. "The customer is always right" is
false: change the food if many say it's too salty, but refuse the few who want
bananas in the lasagna. Cite ING Direct — America's fastest-growing bank built by
saying no (no credit cards, no brokerage, a strict deposit *maximum*) to stay
simple. Don't be a jerk about it — explain politely, even recommend a competitor
if it's a better fit; people are surprisingly understanding.

### Let your customers outgrow you `[RW:EV.2]`

Bending the product to one big-paying customer's requests alienates everyone
else — and when that customer leaves, you're stuck with a product fitted to
someone who's gone. 37signals told long-time customers "no" when they wanted
power-user complexity added: better that customers eventually *outgrow* the
product than never be able to grow *into* it. Scaring away newcomers is worse
than losing veterans — small, simple, basic needs are constant and there's an
endless supply of new customers who need exactly that. Be true to a *type* of
customer, not one individual with changing needs.

### Don't confuse enthusiasm with priority `[RW:EV.3]`

A new idea gives you a rush and you want to drop everything for it — bad move.
Enthusiasm isn't an accurate gauge of worth; today's "sure-fire hit" is often
tomorrow's "nice to have," which isn't worth halting everything. Have as many
ideas as you can, but write them down, park them a few days, and evaluate
priority with a calm mind.

### Be at-home good `[RW:EV.4]`

An "in-store-good" product wins at the shelf (most features, hot packaging,
sensational copy) but disappoints once you use it. Build "at-home good" instead —
the product you like *more* the longer you live with it, and tell friends about.
This may cost some in-store sizzle (executing the basics beautifully looks less
flashy from afar), but you're after a long-term relationship, not a one-night
stand. Same for ads: in-media good matters far less than at-home good — you can't
paint over a bad experience with marketing.

### Don't write it down `[RW:EV.5]`

Don't build a spreadsheet/database to track feature requests. Listen, then
forget — the requests that matter you'll hear over and over until you can't
forget them; your customers become your memory. A request you keep forgetting is
a sign it isn't important. The really important stuff doesn't go away.

## Promotion — attention without a budget

### Welcome obscurity `[RW:PM.1]`

Nobody knows you yet — great. Obscurity lets you make mistakes, tweak, and test
without the world watching, protecting your ego and confidence. Cite retail test
markets (Dunkin' trialing hot sandwiches at ten locations) and Broadway's
out-of-town tryouts. You wouldn't want your first-ever speech to be in front of
10,000 people. Enjoy it — once big, you take fewer risks (pressure for
predictability), and every change hits more people — their image: you can talk a
hundred upset users down, but ten thousand of them is a riot you'd need gear for.

### Build an audience `[RW:PM.2]`

All companies have customers; lucky ones have fans; the most fortunate have an
*audience*. Buying attention with ads is expensive and unreliable ("you waste
half your ad budget — you just don't know which half"). An audience comes back on
its own — the most receptive group you'll ever have. 37signals built 100,000+
daily readers on its Signal vs. Noise blog; reaching them the old way would cost
hundreds of thousands or millions. Speak, write, blog, tweet, make videos — share
valuable info and the right people will already be listening when you have
something to sell.

### Out-teach your competition `[RW:PM.3]`

Everyone advertises, sells, and sponsors — so those don't make you stand out.
*Teach* instead; competitors aren't even thinking about it. Cite Hoefler Type
Foundry (teaching typography), Etsy (seller workshops), Gary Vaynerchuk (Wine
Library TV). Teaching earns loyalty and trust that bought attention never does —
people become fans even if they don't buy. It's something small companies can do
that big ones won't, because big companies are obsessed with secrecy and buried
in legal red tape. Teaching is how you outmaneuver them.

### Emulate chefs `[RW:PM.4]`

You know Batali, Flay, Child, Pépin because they *share everything* — recipes in
cookbooks, techniques on TV. Share everything you know too. A recipe is far
easier to copy than a business, yet nobody buys Batali's cookbook and opens a
restaurant next door to destroy him — it doesn't work that way. Most businesses'
"proprietary" secrets aren't the moat they think. What are your recipes? What's
your cookbook? (Rework itself is 37signals' cookbook.)

### Go behind the scenes `[RW:PM.5]`

Give people a backstage pass to how your business works — don't wait for someone
else to make the "reality show." Even "boring" jobs fascinate when framed right
(Deadliest Catch, Ice Road Truckers, Food Network's Unwrapped). People are
curious how things are made; letting them behind the curtain makes you human, not
a faceless company, and builds appreciation for the effort behind what you sell.

### Nobody likes plastic flowers `[RW:PM.6]`

The "professionals" in perfect uniforms come off stiff and unrelatable. Show your
flaws — imperfections are real and people respond to real (we like real flowers
that wilt, not perfect plastic). This is the Japanese *wabi-sabi* principle:
character over shiny facade; pare to the essence but don't remove the poetry.
Over-polished things lose their soul and seem robotic. Talk like you really talk,
show unfinished work, be upfront about shortcomings — less "professional," far
more genuine.

### Press releases are spam `[RW:PM.7]`

A generic pitch blasted to hundreds of strangers hoping one bites is spam — and
that's exactly what a press release is. They're tired, formulaic, and drowned in
hyperbole ("revolutionary," "groundbreaking") that numbs journalists sifting
dozens a day. Making your first introduction a vague note you also sent everyone
else is a bad impression. Instead: call someone, write a personal note, contact
the specific journalist who covered a similar product, pitch with passion. Be
remarkable and unforgettable.

### Forget about the Wall Street Journal `[RW:PM.8]`

Pitching Time/Forbes/NYT/WSJ is nearly impossible and they won't care — you're
not big enough to matter. Target trade publications and niche bloggers: lower
barrier, same-day email responses, no editorial board or PR pipeline, and they're
*hungry* for fresh stories (big reporters now mine these sites). 37signals got
more traffic and sales from Daring Fireball or Lifehacker than from Wired or
Time — fringe stories go mainstream fast.

### Free samples — the free-sample model `[RW:PM.9]`

If your product is genuinely good, give a small taste away free, confident the
customer comes back to pay. This forces you to make a bite-size, easily
digestible intro people can try with no money or much time. Cite bakeries and
ice-cream shops (samples), car dealers (test drives), software (free trials /
limited versions). Don't fear giving a little away *as long as you have something
else to sell* — and if you're not confident they'll return, you haven't built a
strong enough product.

### Marketing is not a department `[RW:PM.10]`

Accounting is a department; marketing isn't — it's everyone, 24/7/365. Just as
you can't *not* communicate, you can't *not* market: every phone call, email,
product use, word on your site, error message, after-dinner mint, checkout
counter, and invoice is marketing. These small things matter more than which swag
goes in a conference goodie bag. Marketing is the sum total of everything you do.

### The myth of the overnight sensation `[RW:PM.11]`

You won't be a big hit right away — nobody cares about you yet, get used to it.
Dig into any "overnight success" and you'll find years of grinding to reach the
launchpad; rare instant success usually collapses for lack of foundation. Trade
the overnight dream for slow, measured growth and patience. Don't waste money on
a PR firm ($10k+/month) — you're a no-name with no story yet; you'll have one
once you have customers and history. Great brands (Starbucks, Apple, Nike,
Amazon, Google, Snapple) grew over time, not from an upfront PR push. Start
building your audience today.

## Hiring — only when it hurts

### Do it yourself first `[RW:HR.1]`

Never hire for a job until you've tried doing it yourself — then you understand
the work, know what "done well" looks like, can write a real job description and
interview well, and can decide full-time vs. part-time vs. outsource vs. keep
doing it (the last is preferable if possible). You'll also manage better,
supervising work you've done. 37signals didn't hire a sysadmin until one founder
spent a summer setting up servers, and did its own support for three years first.
Try learning your way out of feeling out of your depth before hiring your way
out; you should want to be intimately involved in every aspect of the business.

### Hire when it hurts `[RW:HR.2]`

Don't hire for pleasure — hire to kill pain. Always ask: what if we *don't* hire?
Is the extra work necessary? Can software or a process change solve it? If someone
leaves, don't replace immediately — see how long you get by; you often need fewer
people than you think. The right time is when there's more work than you can
handle *for a sustained period*, things you genuinely can't do anymore, quality
slipping. That's hurting — hire then, not earlier.

### Pass on great people `[RW:HR.3]`

Some companies are addicted to hiring — inventing titles to land someone "great,"
who then sits doing unimportant work. Passing on people you don't need, even
great catches, is right: more people than you need breeds artificial work →
artificial projects → real cost and complexity. Don't fret "the one that got
away" — there's plenty of talent, and when you have a real need you'll find a good
fit. Great has nothing to do with it; if you don't need someone, you don't.

### Strangers at a cocktail party `[RW:HR.4]`

A party of strangers is stiff small talk; an intimate dinner of old friends has
real debate. Hire fast and you get the stranger problem: everyone's unfailingly
polite, avoids conflict, nobody says "this idea sucks" — appeasement instead of
challenge. That appeasement produces work that offends no one and makes no one
fall in love. You need an environment safe enough for honesty when things get
tough. So hire slowly.

### Resumés are ridiculous `[RW:HR.5]`

Resumés are exaggerated, unverifiable, meaningless-action-verb spam that
half-assed applicants shotgun to hundreds of employers. Someone blasting 300
companies clearly hasn't researched you. Start with the *cover letter* — real
communication in the applicant's actual voice, impossible to mass-personalize —
and trust your gut: if the first paragraph is weak and there's no hook in three,
there's probably no match; if your gut senses one, move to interview.

### Years of irrelevance `[RW:HR.6]`

"Five years of experience required" tells you a number and nothing else. A
baseline (six months to a year) makes sense — that's how long to internalize the
idioms and tools — but after that the curve flattens; there's little difference
between six months and six years. What matters is dedication, personality, and
intelligence. How *long* someone's done it is overrated; how *well* is what
counts.

### Forget about formal education `[RW:HR.7]`

Plenty of smart people don't shine in classrooms; don't require "best schools."
Cite: ~90% of CEOs of the top 500 U.S. companies didn't get undergrad degrees
from Ivy League schools, and more came from the University of Wisconsin than
Harvard. Too much academia can harm you — you must unlearn its writing lessons
(longer = matters more; stiff formal tone; big words; hit a word count; format
over content), which is why business writing is dry and wordy. The great-candidate
pool is far bigger than high-GPA grads — consider dropouts, low GPAs,
community-college and high-school-only candidates.

### Everybody works `[RW:HR.8]`

On a small team everyone must produce, not delegate — no one above the work. Avoid
delegators who love telling others what to do; they're dead weight, clogging the
pipes with busywork they invent, and meetings are their best friend (where they
seem important while pulling everyone off real work).

### Hire managers of one `[RW:HR.9]`

Managers of one set their own goals and execute them — no heavy direction, no
daily check-ins; they set the tone and figure out what needs doing, by and for
themselves. They free you from oversight and surprise you with output. Spot them
by background: they've run something on their own or launched a project. Finding
them lets the whole team work more and manage less.

### Hire great writers `[RW:HR.10]`

Among finalists, hire the best writer — marketer, designer, or programmer alike.
Clear writing signals clear thinking: good writers communicate, make things easy
to understand, put themselves in others' shoes, and know what to omit. Writing is
today's currency for good ideas (email, IM, blogging over phone calls).

### The best are everywhere `[RW:HR.11]`

Don't skip the best people because they live far away — technology makes remote
teams easy. Over half of 37signals' team lives outside Chicago HQ (Spain, Canada,
Idaho, Oklahoma); limiting the search to Chicago would have missed half of them.
Keep a few hours of real-time overlap daily (two to four is plenty — full overlap
is actually less ideal since you lose alone time); if time zones don't overlap,
have someone shift hours. Meet in person every few months. Geography doesn't
matter — hire the best talent wherever it is.

### Test-drive employees `[RW:HR.12]`

Interviews are limited — some sound like pros but don't work like pros. Evaluate
work they can do *now*: hire them for a mini-project (even 20–40 hours) to see how
they decide, whether you get along, and what they ask. Cite BMW's simulated
assembly line (90-minute task battery) and Cessna's manager role-play (memos,
phony irate customers — 100+ hires this way). The truth comes out in a real work
environment.

## Damage Control — crisis with customers

### Own your bad news `[RW:DC.1]`

When something goes wrong, someone tells the story — better it's you, or rumors
fill the void. Tell customers even if they never noticed; you can't sweep it under
the rug anymore (someone will post it, there are no secrets). People respect
openness during a crisis. Cite the contrast: Exxon Valdez (1989, ~11M gallons) —
chairman waited two weeks, briefed press in a hard-to-reach town → PR disaster
signaling they were hiding or didn't care; vs. Ashland Oil's John Hall, who went
to the scene, took charge, pledged cleanup, and visited news bureaus — flipping
the narrative within a day. Tips: message from the top, forcefully; spread it far
and wide; "no comment" is not an option; apologize like a real person with
detail; be genuinely concerned for customers and prove it.

### Speed changes everything `[RW:DC.2]`

Getting back to people quickly is the most important thing in customer service —
it defuses bad situations into good ones. People expect slow, canned "we care"
platitudes, which is why support queries often open antagonistically (they're
being a squeaky wheel to get grease). Answer fast and they flip 180° — polite,
grateful. A *personal* response differentiates you further; even without a perfect
answer, "let me research and get back to you" works wonders.

### How to say you're sorry `[RW:DC.3]`

The worst is the non-apology apology that accepts no blame ("we're sorry *if* this
upset you"). A good apology accepts responsibility, has no conditional "if,"
provides real detail on what happened and how you'll prevent it, and seeks to make
things right. Break down "we apologize for any inconvenience this may have
caused": an "I" apology beats a "we"; an outage is a *crisis*, not an
"inconvenience"; "may" wavers and slights a real problem. There's no magic
script — go case by case, and ask: would *you* believe this apology on the other
end? You can't apologize your way out of being an ass — trust built *before*
things go wrong matters more than the words.

### Put everyone on the front lines `[RW:DC.4]`

Restaurants have chefs wait tables so the kitchen empathizes with customers. Many
companies split front-of-house (support) from back-of-house (the makers), so
product creators never hear customers directly. Think of the game Telephone — the
more people between customers' words and the workers, the more distorted the
message. Everyone should connect with customers at least a few times a year — it's
feeling the hurt (and the joy of solved problems) that motivates people to fix
things. Don't shield workers from direct criticism. No time? Make time — Craig
Newmark (Craigslist) still answers support email, often within minutes.

### Take a deep breath `[RW:DC.5]`

Rock the boat and there will be waves — knee-jerk reactions pour in after any new
feature, policy change, or removal. Resist panic and rapid reversal; passions
flare early, then settle if you ride out the first rocky week. People are
creatures of habit and reject change reflexively ("worst thing I've ever seen" —
no, it's a minor change). Negative reactions are louder and more passionate than
positive ones — you may hear only complaints even when the majority is happy.
Don't backpedal on a necessary decision; let it simmer, show you're listening,
but explain you'll wait and see. People usually adjust and may end up preferring
the change.

## Culture — a byproduct, not a program

### You don't create a culture `[RW:CU.1]`

Instant cultures are artificial — big bangs of mission statements, declarations,
and rules; obvious, ugly, plastic. The book's image: manufactured culture is like
paint slapped on, while real culture is patina that forms over time. Culture
happens as a byproduct of consistent behavior: encourage sharing and sharing gets
built in; reward trust and trust gets built in. It's not a foosball table, a
policy, a party, or a slogan — culture shows up in what you do, not what you say.
Don't force it; like fine scotch, give it time.

### Decisions are temporary `[RW:CU.2]`

Don't invent problems you don't have yet — it's not a problem until it's a real
one, and most worries never happen. Today's decisions needn't last forever; you
can shoot down good experiments by assuming they must work for years. If
circumstances change, decisions change. Don't fret whether the concept scales
from 5 to 5,000 — getting off the ground is hard enough. Optimize for now; the
ability to change course fast is a core small-company advantage.

### Skip the rock stars `[RW:CU.3]`

"Rock star" / "ninja" job ads are lame. Instead of landing a room of rock stars,
think about the *room* — environment drives great work far more than most realize.
There's huge untapped potential trapped under lame policies, poor direction, and
bureaucracy; cut the crap and people are waiting to do great work. Rock-star
environments come from trust, autonomy, and responsibility — giving people the
privacy, workspace, tools, and respect they deserve (not casual Fridays).

### They're not thirteen `[RW:CU.4]`

Treat people like children and you get children's work — yet many companies make
employees ask permission for every tiny thing and expenditure. Constant approval
creates a culture of nonthinkers and screams "I don't trust you." Banning
YouTube/social sites gains nothing (the time doesn't convert to work). You won't
get a real eight hours out of anyone anyway — people need diversions to break
monotony. Policing costs real money and IT time; failing to trust employees is
expensive.

### Send people home at 5 `[RW:CU.5]`

The "no life outside work, sleeps under the desk" dream employee lets you get away
with lousy execution and perpetuates "it's the only way to compete." You don't
need more hours, you need *better* hours. People with somewhere to be (kids, choir
practice) get work done efficiently at the office. "If you want something done,
ask the busiest person you know" — you want people with a life outside work who
care about more than one thing. Don't expect the job to be someone's entire life
if you want to keep them.

### Don't scar on the first cut `[RW:CU.6]`

The instant something goes wrong, the urge is a new policy ("someone wore shorts —
we need a dress code!"). No — just tell John not to wear shorts again. Policies
are organizational scar tissue: codified overreactions to unlikely-to-recur
events, collective punishment for one person's misstep. Bureaucracies are born
one policy — one scar — at a time. Reserve policies for things that happen over
and over.

### Sound like you `[RW:CU.7]`

Businesspeople trying to sound big — stiff language, legalese, artificial
friendliness — sound like robots talking *at* you, not *to* you. The
professionalism mask is a joke, and copying it forfeits a small company's greatest
asset: communicating simply and directly without a legal/PR sieve. Sound your own
size; language is your first impression, so don't start with a lie. Talk to
customers like friends, drop jargon and buzzwords ("making money" not
"monetization," "being honest" not "being transparent"), read it aloud to check
it's conversational, and write for *one* person, not a mob.

### Four-letter words `[RW:CU.8]`

The dangerous four-letter words in business aren't profanity — they're *need,
must, can't, easy, just, only, fast* (watch their cousins *everyone, no one,
always, never*). They force black-and-white when truth rarely is, injecting
tension and torpedoing discussion. "Need": very few things truly need doing — try
"maybe" or "what do you think?" "Can't": you probably can (note opposing can'ts).
"Easy": a word for other people's jobs, never your own. Strung together ("we need
this now, we can't launch without it, everyone wants it, it's only one little
thing, it'll be easy, get it in fast") — 36 words, a hundred assumptions, a recipe
for disaster.

### ASAP is poison `[RW:CU.9]`

Stop saying ASAP — it's implied. Tag every request ASAP and you declare everything
high priority, which means nothing is. It's inflationary: it devalues any request
without it until ASAP is the only way to get anything done. Most things don't
warrant the hysteria — nobody dies or loses their job if a task waits; ASAP just
manufactures stress and burnout. Reserve emergency language for true emergencies
with direct, measurable consequences to inaction. Otherwise, chill out.

## Conclusion

### Inspiration is perishable `[RW:CN.1]`

Ideas are immortal, but *inspiration* has an expiration date like fresh fruit or
milk. If you want to do something, do it now — shelve it two months and you won't
be pumped anymore. Inspired on a Friday? Give up the weekend and dive in — high on
inspiration you can do two weeks' work in twenty-four hours; it's a time machine
and a productivity multiplier. But it won't wait. If it grabs you, grab it right
back and put it to work.
