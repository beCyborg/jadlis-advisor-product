# Competitive Alternatives

## Context

Stage 2 of the positioning pipeline. This is the foundation of all positioning — every subsequent stage (unique attributes, value, market frame, best-fit customers) builds on understanding what customers see as alternatives. Skip or rush this stage and your attributes become meaningless because they have no reference point.

---

## The Core Question

**"What would your best customers do if your product didn't exist?"**

This is NOT "Who are your competitors?" The question is deliberately about what customers would DO, not who they would BUY FROM.

Valid alternatives include all of the following:
- Direct competitors — products that look and function like yours
- Indirect solutions — different category of product solving the same underlying problem
- General-purpose tools — spreadsheets, documents, presentations, Notion, Airtable
- Manual processes — "hire an intern," "do it myself," "ask my assistant"
- Status quo — "do nothing," "live with the problem," "suffer through it"

In B2B software, the most common competitive alternative is a combination of spreadsheets plus manual processes. Expect this to appear in most clusters.

---

## Customer POV vs. Industry Knowledge

This is the most common failure mode at this stage. Resolve it explicitly before generating any list.

You know every competitor in your space — funding announcements, feature releases, conference appearances, Crunchbase profiles. Your customers know 1–2 alternatives at most, and those are often not the companies on your competitor matrix.

Apply these filters:
- If a competitor has few customers, they have zero mindshare with your prospects — do not list them
- If you encounter an edge-case customer with unusual market awareness, note it but do not let it represent the general case
- Investors track startup-vs-startup competition that is invisible to most buyers — do not use investor framing here

The question is not "What exists in the market?" It is "What would your best customers actually consider?"

---

## Focus on Best-Fit Customers Only

Do not survey all customers. Pull only from the best-fit customer list established in Stage 1.

When surveyed broadly, results are muddy — no patterns emerge. When limited to ecstatic fans, clear patterns appear. Best-fit customers share these characteristics:
- Understood the product quickly without extensive explanation
- Moved through the sales cycle fast
- Became raving fans and advocates
- Referred others unprompted
- Represent the pattern you want to replicate at scale

Using all customers dilutes the signal. A churned customer or a poor-fit customer sees different alternatives than your ideal buyer. Their input does not help you build better positioning for the customers you want more of.

---

## Asking About Problems vs. Alternatives

Asking "What problem are you solving?" produces technical, narrow answers that miss the competitive frame.

Example from April Dunford's work: database users said "We want to run a fast query" — suggesting the product was being compared to other databases. When asked "What would you use instead?" — none of them named another database. They named BI tools and data warehouses.

The problem question revealed the technical use case. The alternatives question revealed the actual competitive frame.

Use these prompts:
- "What would you do if our product disappeared tomorrow?"
- "How were you handling this before you found us?"
- "If we didn't exist, what's your next best option?"
- "What did you evaluate before choosing us?"

Avoid:
- "Who else did you consider?" — too narrow, focuses on vendors not behaviors
- "What are your alternatives?" — too abstract, customers rarely think in those terms
- "What problem does this solve?" — produces feature-level answers, not competitive context

---

## Ranking and Clustering

After collecting input from best-fit customers, process the raw list in three steps.

**Step 1: Generate the full list**
Write down every alternative mentioned. Do not filter yet. Include anything a best-fit customer named, even if it seems trivial or obvious.

**Step 2: Rank by frequency**
Count how many customers mentioned each alternative. Sort from most common to least common. Absolute numbers matter less than relative frequency — you are looking for the majority pattern.

**Step 3: Group into clusters**
Combine alternatives that represent the same underlying behavior. Aim for 2–5 clusters. Name each cluster by what the customer is actually doing, not by vendor name.

Example cluster structure:
- Cluster 1: "Do it manually" — hire an intern, use Excel, build a custom spreadsheet, do-it-myself
- Cluster 2: "Use small-business accounting" — QuickBooks, FreshBooks, Wave
- Cluster 3: "Enterprise solution" — SAP, Oracle, Microsoft Dynamics

Focus positioning work on the top 1–2 clusters. Rare alternatives in the tail can be noted but should not drive positioning decisions.

## Common Mistakes at This Stage

### Mistake 1: Listing industry competitors

Teams list companies they track on Crunchbase or see at conferences. Customers have never heard of most of them. Real alternatives are usually more mundane — spreadsheets, internal tools, doing nothing. If you find yourself listing five VC-backed startups in the same space, you are describing the competitive landscape for investors, not the alternatives your buyers consider.

### Mistake 2: Starting with features

Teams want to jump to "What makes us special?" before understanding what they are being compared against. Unique attributes only make sense relative to alternatives. A feature is only differentiated if it is absent from what customers would otherwise use. Run Stage 2 completely before touching Stage 3.

### Mistake 3: Exhaustive lists without prioritization

Every possible alternative gets listed, including obscure niche players. The output becomes a fifty-item list with no clear signal. Apply the frequency ranking — if fewer than 10–15% of best-fit customers mentioned it, it belongs in the tail, not in the primary cluster.

### Mistake 4: Mixing customer types

Different customer segments see different alternatives. A Fortune 500 company and a 5-person startup have completely different options available to them and completely different switching costs. If you mix segments in your analysis, the alternatives list will have no coherent pattern. Run Stage 2 for one segment at a time — specifically, your best-fit customer segment from Stage 1.

### Mistake 5: Ignoring "do nothing"

For genuinely innovative products, the biggest competitor is often inertia. Customers do not know they have a problem, or the cost of not solving it is not obvious or urgent. "Do nothing" is a legitimate and often dominant competitive alternative. If it appears frequently, treat it as a primary cluster and plan positioning around making the status quo cost visible.

## Edge Cases

### "We have no competition"

Push back immediately. "No competition" typically means one of two things: either the team is confusing "no direct competitors" with "no alternatives," or the problem is not real or urgent enough for customers to seek any solution. Even the most innovative products exist in a world where customers are doing something to cope — even if it is a bad workaround or a painful manual process. Probe: "What is the customer doing today? What happens if they don't solve this?" There is always an alternative.

### Pre-product stage

Without customers, you cannot validate alternatives empirically. In this case:
- Make educated guesses based on customer discovery interviews
- Treat those guesses as provisional, not confirmed
- Keep positioning loose and explicitly labeled as a hypothesis
- Return to this stage when you have real purchase and usage data from at least 10–15 best-fit customers
- Do not build Stage 3–5 positioning on unvalidated alternatives

### Multiple distinct segments with divergent alternatives

If best-fit customers split into two groups that name completely different alternatives, this is a signal that either:
1. Your "best-fit" definition in Stage 1 was too broad and includes multiple distinct segments
2. You have a multi-segment positioning problem and need separate positioning work for each segment

Do not average across divergent segments. Choose one segment, complete the full pipeline for that segment, then run a separate positioning exercise for the other.

### Alternatives shift over time

Competitive alternatives are not static. As markets mature, as new tools emerge, and as customer awareness grows, what customers consider alternatives changes. Plan to revisit Stage 2 at least annually, or after any significant market event — a major competitor launch, a category shift, a pricing change that changes the accessibility of alternatives.

## Output Format for This Stage

Document the output in a structured block before advancing to Stage 3.

```
COMPETITIVE ALTERNATIVES — [Product Name]

Best-fit customer segment: [description from Stage 1]

Raw alternatives (ranked by frequency):
1. [Alternative] — mentioned by [N] customers
2. [Alternative] — mentioned by [N] customers
...

Clustered alternatives:
Cluster 1 (primary): [Name] — includes [A, B, C]
Cluster 2: [Name] — includes [D, E]
Cluster 3 (tail): [Name] — includes [F, G]

Primary competitive frame: Customers are currently [doing X] or [using Y]
```

This output becomes the input for Stage 3: identifying unique attributes by comparing your product's capabilities against these specific alternatives.

---

## How Alternatives Flow to Next Stages

Once alternatives are ranked and clustered, the output anchors everything downstream:

- **Stage 3**: Compare your features to these alternatives — unique attributes emerge only in contrast to what customers would otherwise use
- **Stage 4**: Determine who cares about the value your unique attributes provide over these specific alternatives
- **Stage 5**: Choose a market frame that makes your advantages over these alternatives immediately obvious to best-fit customers

Every positioning decision you make in Stages 3–7 must be traceable back to what you learned here. If a claimed advantage does not exist relative to the actual alternatives customers consider, it is not an advantage — it is a feature.
