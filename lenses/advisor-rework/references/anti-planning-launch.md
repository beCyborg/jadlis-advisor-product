# Anti-Planning & Launch — Starting and Shipping (Takedowns, Go, Progress)

The Rework case against long-range planning, over-funding, and over-building —
and the case for shipping a small, opinionated thing now. All principles
paraphrased from the original; cite with `[RW:…]` tags.

Tag map for this file: `[RW:TD]` = Takedowns, `[RW:GO]` = Go, `[RW:PR]` = Progress.
Dot notation = principle within chapter, e.g. `[RW:GO.2]` = Scratch your own itch.

---

## Planning is guessing `[RW:TD.3]`

Long-range business plans are fiction dressed as control. Too many inputs
(market, competitors, customers, the economy) sit outside your hands, so a
written plan mostly manufactures a feeling of certainty you don't have.

- Rename them honestly: "business plan" → business *guess*, "financial plan" →
  financial *guess*. Renaming lowers the stress you attach to them.
- Plans let the past dictate the future and put blinders on you ("this is where
  we're going because it's where we said we'd go") — which is incompatible with
  improvising when a better opportunity appears.
- You have the *most* information while doing the work, the *least* before you
  start. Yet plans are written at the start — the worst moment to lock in a big
  decision. Decide right before you act, not far in advance.
- Practical rule: plan what you'll do *this week*, not this year. Thinking about
  the future is fine; you just don't need to write a long document and obsess
  over it (you'll never re-read it anyway).

Anti-pattern this replaces: treating a fat, multi-page plan as a commitment.
Blindly following a plan disconnected from reality is scarier than winging it.

## Learning from mistakes is overrated `[RW:TD.2]`

Failure is not a prerequisite for success. Other people's failures are theirs,
not statistics about you. A mistake teaches you what *not* to do again but not
what you *should* do next; success gives you reusable ammunition — you know what
worked and can repeat it.

- Number to cite: a Harvard Business School study found already-successful
  founders succeed again ~34% of the time, while founders whose first company
  failed hit ~23% — essentially the same rate as first-timers (~23%). Prior
  failure buys you almost nothing; prior success compounds.
- Use when a user is romanticizing "fail fast / fail often" or treating a
  planned failure as a rite of passage.

## Make a dent in the universe `[RW:GO.1]`

Great work needs the felt sense that it matters — that if you stopped, customers
would notice and complain. You don't need to cure cancer; you need your effort
to be genuinely useful and to carry urgency (this is your life's work; a me-too
product wastes it).

- Small teams can flatten incumbents: Craigslist (a few dozen staff) gutted the
  classified-ad business and disrupted newspapers; the Drudge Report was one guy
  on one page yet reset how the news industry sourced stories. Scale of impact is
  decoupled from headcount.

## Scratch your own itch `[RW:GO.2]`

The most reliable way to build something good is to build what *you* need. You
become your own instant quality test and you settle the hundreds of tiny daily
design calls with the light on instead of stabbing in the dark for a stranger.

- 37signals built Highrise (contact management) because they needed to track who
  they talked to and when to follow up — no focus groups, no market study.
- Paraphrased cases to cite: James Dyson (bagless cyclonic vacuum, born from his
  own clogging bag); Vic Firth (matched-pair drumsticks from the symphony pit —
  today ~85,000 sticks/day, ~62% market share); Bill Bowerman (waffle-sole Nike
  from his family waffle iron); Mary Kay Wagner (knew her skin-care worked
  because she wore it herself).
- Bonus: you fall in love with the problem, which matters because you may work
  on it for years.

## Start making something `[RW:GO.3]`

Ideas are cheap and everyone has them; execution is the whole game. The friend
who "had the idea for eBay" built nothing. Kubrick's advice to would-be
filmmakers, paraphrased: grab a camera and some film and make *a* movie of any
kind — the point is to begin. Try to sell a bare idea and you'll learn how
little it's worth un-executed.

## No time is no excuse `[RW:GO.4]`

"Not enough time" is usually "not enough want." You don't have to quit your day
job or pull all-nighters — squeezing a few extra hours a week (go to bed at
eleven instead of ten; skip the TV/World of Warcraft) is enough to test whether
the interest is real. Keeping the job means you risk almost nothing but a little
time. The perfect moment never comes — you'll always be too young, old, busy, or
broke.

## Draw a line in the sand `[RW:GO.5]`

Great businesses stand for something — a point of view, not just a product.
A strong stand attracts superfans who evangelize harder than any ad, and it
makes decisions obvious — with a clear belief, calls settle themselves; without
one, every choice turns into an argument.

- Strong opinions cost you some customers — that's the price, not a bug. If
  nobody is upset, you're not pushing hard enough (and probably boring).
- 37signals is proud of what its products *don't* do; it will lose customers who
  want a pet feature so the rest love the product intensely.
- Cite: Whole Foods (won't stock Coke or Snickers — no repeated debate about
  artificial ingredients; being pricier and mocked as "Whole Paycheck" is fine);
  Vinnie's Sub Shop (closes for the day once the morning-fresh bread runs out
  rather than sell a lesser sandwich on second-rate afternoon bread).

## Mission statement impossible `[RW:GO.6]`

There's a chasm between actually standing for something and posting a mission
statement that *says* you do. The generic "exceed their expectations for
service, quality and value" plaque on a grubly rental-office wall reads as phony
because it's disconnected from the lived experience. Same as the on-hold robot
telling you how much they value you while you wait thirty minutes. Believe it and
live it, or say nothing.

## You need less than you think `[RW:GO.8]`

Interrogate every "requirement" as a question: Do you *really* need ten people
or will two or three do for now? $500,000 or is $50,000 (or $5,000) enough? Six
months or two? A big office or shared space / home? A warehouse or a garage /
outsourced fulfillment? A PR firm or another way to get noticed? An IT
department or outsourcing? A retail store or selling online?

- The pattern: "maybe eventually — but not right now." Frugality is not a flaw.
- 37signals launched their first product on the cheap: shared office space, a
  single server, no ads (promoted by sharing experiences online), the founder
  answering support email himself. Great companies start in garages.

## Start a business, not a startup `[RW:GO.9]`

The "startup" as a magical place — where expenses are someone else's problem and
revenue is a future issue — is a fairy tale. Every business, new or old, obeys
the same rule: revenue in, expenses out, turn a profit or disappear. "We'll
figure out profit later" is building a rocket while pretending gravity is
optional. Something with no route to profit is a hobby wearing a business costume.

- Advisory use: when a user leans on "we're a startup" to excuse deep problems,
  push them to act like an actual business worrying about profit from day one.
  (For the funding side of this, see `[RW:GO.7]` Outside money is Plan Z in
  `growth-hiring-culture.md`.)

## Building to flip is building to flop `[RW:GO.10]`

Obsessing over an exit strategy before you've built anything is backwards — like
writing a prenup on a first date. You need a *commitment* strategy, not an exit
strategy. The odds of an acquisition are tiny (on the order of 1 in 1,000 to 1 in
10,000), and building to be bought makes you optimize for the acquirer instead of
for customers loving you. Founders who do flip often retire ~six months, miss the
game, and come back with a weaker second act. If you get a good thing going, keep
it going.

## Less mass `[RW:GO.11]`

Right now you're the leanest and fastest you'll ever be; from here you only
accumulate mass, and mass makes changing direction expensive. Mass is added by:
long-term contracts, excess staff, permanent decisions, meetings, thick process,
inventory (physical or mental), tech/hardware lock-ins, long-term roadmaps, and
office politics. Keep mass low and you can change your model, product, features,
or message quickly — and, most important, change your mind.

## Embrace constraints `[RW:PR.1]`

"Not enough time/money/people/experience" is not a complaint — it's an
advantage. Limits kill waste and force creativity.

- Cite: sonnets/haiku/limericks (rules that generate creativity); Hemingway and
  Carver (self-imposed plain language → maximum impact); *The Price Is Right*
  (100+ games all from one question, "how much does this cost?"); Southwest (only
  Boeing 737s → any crew works any flight, all parts fit all planes → lower cost,
  simpler operation).
- 37signals built Basecamp under heavy constraints (a running design firm, a
  seven-hour time gap between principals, a small team, no funding) — which kept
  it simple. Even with more resources later they *keep* constraints: one or two
  people per product, features minimized, to avoid bloat.

## Build half a product, not a half-assed product `[RW:PR.2]`

Trying to do everything at once turns great ideas into a bad product. Cut your
ambition in half — a kick-ass half beats a half-assed whole. Most "great" ideas
look ordinary once you have perspective, and the truly great ones survive to be
built later. Things get better as they get shorter (directors cut good scenes,
musicians drop good tracks). The authors cut Rework itself from ~57,000 to
~27,000 words between drafts.

## Start at the epicenter `[RW:PR.3]`

Among the stuff you *could*, *want to*, and *have to* do, begin with the
have-to — the epicenter. The test: strip a candidate element away and ask whether
what you sell still exists. A hot-dog stand survives losing the onions, relish,
and mustard but not the hot dogs. Find the one irremovable thing and pour your energy there first;
everything else rests on that foundation.

## Ignore the details early on `[RW:PR.4]`

Architects finalize the floor plan before choosing shower tiles. Getting
infatuated with details too early breeds disagreement, meetings, and delays over
decisions that will change anyway. Nail the basics first.

- 37signals sketches with a thick Sharpie, not a fine pen — low resolution *on
  purpose*, so you can't drill into shading or line styles that should stay out
  of focus. (Cite Walt Stanchfield, Disney drawing instructor: "forget the
  detail" early — detail buys nothing in the early stages.)
- You often can't even recognize which details matter until you start building
  and feel what's missing. That's the moment to pay attention — not sooner.

## Making the call is making progress `[RW:PR.5]`

Postponed decisions pile up and get ignored, rushed, or dumped. Swap "let's
think about it" for "let's decide on it." A perfect answer won't arrive later —
you're as likely to make a great call today as tomorrow. Decisions build
momentum and morale; you can build on "done," not on "we'll decide later."

- Cite: 37signals delayed an affiliate program because the "perfect" version
  (automated payments, mailed checks, foreign tax law) seemed too complex. The
  unlock was asking "what can we easily do right now that's good enough?" —
  answer: pay affiliates in *credit*, not cash. They shipped that, then upgraded
  to cash later. Decisions are temporary; a mistake can be corrected.

## Be a curator `[RW:PR.6]`

A great museum is defined by what's *not* on the walls — a curator says no; the
result is a sub-sub-subset of everything possible. Constantly remove, simplify,
and streamline; pare to the essential, then do it again. You can add back later.
Cite Zingerman's deli: they *curate* their shelves (e.g., carrying an olive oil
only because the owner tasted it and vouches for it), not just fill them.

## Throw less at the problem `[RW:PR.7]`

The instinct when things break is to add more (people, time, money) — which
enlarges the problem. Go the other way: cut back. Cite Gordon Ramsay's *Kitchen
Nightmares*: the first move at a failing restaurant is almost always trimming a
bloated 30+ dish menu down to ~10, *then* polishing what remains. Do less; the
project rarely suffers as feared and often improves because you're forced to sort
what truly matters. Start pushing deadlines and budgets and you'll never stop.

## Focus on what won't change `[RW:PR.8]`

Building around the hot new thing means chasing fashion over substance. Anchor
the core of the business to what people will want today *and* in ten years.

- Cite: Amazon (fast/free shipping, selection, easy returns, low prices);
  Japanese automakers (reliability, affordability, practicality); 37signals
  (speed, simplicity, ease of use, clarity). Nobody will ever wish software were
  slower or harder to use.

## Tone is in your fingers `[RW:PR.9]`

Fancy tools don't make the work — Eddie Van Halen sounds like Eddie Van Halen on
a pawn-shop rig; Tiger Woods beats you with cheap clubs. People use gear as a
crutch and a shortcut to avoid the hours on the driving range. In business this
is obsessing over software tricks, office space, and furniture instead of the
only things that matter: getting customers and making money. Same for
blogging/podcasting/video — the content is what matters; great gear with nothing
to say is still nothing to say.

## Sell your by-products `[RW:PR.10]`

Everything you make produces a by-product; observant people spot and sell it.
The lumber industry sells sawdust and chips (fireplace logs, particleboard,
fuel). 37signals' book *Getting Real* was the by-product of building software —
swept from blog posts to a workshop to a PDF to a paperback, earning the company
$1M+ directly (and likely another $1M indirectly). Cite also Wilco (documentary
*I Am Trying to Break Your Heart* from filming an album's making) and Henry Ford
(Model-T wood scraps → Ford/Kingsford Charcoal, still the U.S. leader).

## Launch now `[RW:PR.11]`

Your product is shippable far sooner than you're comfortable with — once it does
what it needs to do, get it out. A remaining to-do list doesn't mean it isn't
done; leftovers can be done later (often *better* later).

- Forcing question: "If you had to launch in two weeks, what would you cut?" A
  deadline delivers clarity and surfaces the gut instinct that says "we don't
  need this." Build the necessities now, luxuries later.
- Cite: 37signals launched Basecamp with *no* billing ability — monthly billing
  gave a 30-day gap, so they spent pre-launch time on day-one problems and built
  billing during the gap. Camper's "Walk in Progress" store opened before
  construction finished (customers drew on the walls; shoes on plywood over shoe
  boxes). Crate and Barrel's first store displayed goods on the overturned crates
  and barrels they arrived in.
- This is *not* skimping on quality — it's reaching quality through iteration.
  Stop imagining what will work; find out for real.

## Reasons to quit — the seven questions `[RW:PD]`

Cross-listed here because it gates *whether to build at all* (full framework
lives conceptually with Productivity). Periodically pull your head up and ask of
any work in flight:

1. Why are you doing this? (Who's it for? What's the motivation?)
2. What problem are you solving? (Sometimes it's an *imaginary* problem.)
3. Is this actually useful, or just cool? (Cool wears off; useful never does.)
4. Are you adding value, or just adding? (Too much ketchup ruins the fries.)
5. Will this change behavior? (If not, don't add it.)
6. Is there an easier way? (Usually yes, and it's good enough for now.)
7. What could you be doing instead? (Opportunity cost, acute for small teams.)

Don't be timid about the conclusion — abandoning sunk effort can be the right
move. Don't throw good time after bad work.
