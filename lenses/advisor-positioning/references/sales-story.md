# Sales Story & What Happens Next

## Context

This reference covers Stage 7B of the positioning pipeline and the work that follows. Use it after the Positioning Canvas is complete. The work here translates positioning into a Sales Story Arc, then into messaging, roadmap alignment, pricing alignment, and an ongoing review cadence.

---

## Why Sales Story Before Messaging

Common instinct after completing positioning: go straight to messaging — write website copy, run ad campaigns, produce taglines. This is the wrong sequence.

The Sales Story Arc must come first because:

- It forces the team to agree on how positioning translates into a pitch before any copy is written.
- It defines the narrative structure that all downstream messaging will be built from. Without it, individual writers and marketers each invent their own version of the story.
- It ensures salespeople internalize positioning in a form they can actually use in a conversation — not just in an abstract canvas.
- Messaging written without a shared story arc diverges over time. Emails reference one framing, ads reference another, the sales deck references a third. The positioning disintegrates under normal production pressure.

The order is: Positioning Canvas → Sales Story Arc → Master Messaging Document → Individual assets (website, ads, decks, emails).

## The 6-Step Sales Story Arc

### Step 1: Define the Problem

State the problem the solution was designed to solve. This puts a boundary around the conversation and signals to the customer that you understand their situation.

**Format:** "[Industry/role] today are trying to [goal] but face [obstacle]."

**Examples:**
- "Insurance companies today are trying to make their claims process less painful for demanding, digital-savvy customers."
- "Cash flow is critical to small businesses, where an unexpected expense can threaten the company's survival."

**Rules:**
- The problem must resonate with the target segment identified in Stage 4. If it doesn't land for best-fit customers, rewrite it.
- Use the language your customers use, not internal terminology or product language.
- The problem statement should make it natural to consider your category of solution — it should frame the problem so that the solution type is obvious in retrospect.

---

### Step 2: Current Solutions and Their Gaps

Describe how customers currently solve the problem and where those solutions fall short. This maps directly to the competitive alternatives from Stage 2.

**Format:** "Currently, [target customers] address this by [alternative approaches], but [specific gaps or limitations]."

**Examples:**
- "Insurers have added mobile apps to their claims process, but customers still complete many steps manually and call the office for basic status updates."
- "Some small businesses can get financing from a bank, but many don't qualify, and the approval process takes weeks."

**Rules:**
- Reference the actual alternatives your best customers use — not theoretical competitors, not aspirational ones.
- The gaps you name must connect directly to your unique attributes from Stage 3. If the gap you name isn't something you solve, cut it.
- Be specific. "Current solutions are expensive" doesn't land. "Current solutions charge per seat, so companies stop rolling out access at 20 users even when 200 people need it" lands.

---

### Step 3: The Perfect World

Describe the ideal solution given the problem and the limitations of current alternatives. This step sets up your product as the natural answer without naming it yet.

**Format:** "In a perfect world, [target customers] could [ideal outcome that directly addresses the gaps]."

**Examples:**
- "In a perfect world, customers could complete the entire claims process — reporting, submitting documents, tracking status, receiving payment — without leaving their phone."
- "In a perfect world, small businesses could access cash within days based on invoices they've already closed, without a bank qualification process."

**Rules:**
- The "perfect world" description must map to your value themes from Stage 3B. If you've described something your product can't deliver, rewrite it.
- Do not name the product in this step. Describe outcomes, not your product.
- Make the ideal state aspirational but grounded. If it reads like science fiction relative to current technology, customers won't believe it's achievable.

---

### Step 4: Product Introduction in Market Category

Introduce the product and anchor it in the market category chosen in Stage 5.

**Format:** "[Product] is a [market category] for/that [one-line value description]."

**Examples:**
- "Mobileclaimsorama is a mobile claims management solution for insurers."
- "Financialhoohah offers invoice financing to growing small businesses."

**Rules:**
- Use the exact market category wording from Stage 5. The category name is doing cognitive work — it triggers the right set of assumptions in the customer's mind. Paraphrasing it loses that effect.
- One sentence. The elaboration comes in Step 5.
- If the market category is new (head positioning from Stage 5), add one phrase that anchors it to something familiar.

---

### Step 5: Value Themes with Detail

Walk through each value theme from Stage 3B. For each theme: state the value, explain which features or capabilities deliver it, and provide proof.

This is where the Feature → Benefit → Value chain from Stage 3 gets fully articulated.

**Structure per value theme:**

1. **Value statement**: What does the customer get? State it in customer terms (outcome, not feature).
2. **How it works**: Which specific features or capabilities make this value possible? One to three sentences.
3. **Proof**: Data points, customer quotes, third-party validation (analyst reports, certifications, benchmarks). At least one proof element per theme.

**Example (abbreviated):**
- Value: "Claims resolved in hours instead of days."
- How: "The mobile app integrates directly with your claims database and document verification system, eliminating the manual handoff steps that cause delays."
- Proof: "Our customers report a 68% reduction in average claims resolution time. [Customer quote: 'We went from five days average to under 18 hours for standard claims.']"

**Rules:**
- Cover every value theme from the Canvas. Omitting one leaves the story incomplete.
- Proof must be specific. "Customers love it" is not proof. "87% of customers renewed in the first year" is proof.
- Order themes by resonance with the target segment, not by feature prominence internally.

---

### Step 6: Next Steps

Close the story arc with a specific action. Define what you want the prospect to do after this conversation.

Options by sales stage:
- Schedule a live demo with a technical resource.
- Start a free trial (with a specific onboarding action, not just "sign up").
- Speak with a reference customer in the same industry.
- Agree to a paid pilot with a defined scope and timeline.
- Sign a proposal.

**Rules:**
- One next step per conversation. Give multiple options only if your sales process requires it at this stage.
- Make the next step low-friction. "Let's get a second call on the calendar" is lower friction than "Review our 40-page proposal."
- Align the next step with where the customer is in their buying process, not where you want them to be.

## From Story Arc to Sales Deck

The Sales Story Arc is not the sales deck. It is the agreed-upon narrative structure the deck will follow. After the team aligns on the arc:

1. Marketing creates the presentation with visuals, case studies, objection-handling slides, and formatting.
2. Sales reviews the draft and flags gaps based on common questions encountered in the field.
3. Iterate until the deck flows naturally and addresses the objections that reliably come up.

Common sections added to the final deck beyond the arc:

- A dedicated case study or reference customer section with before/after data.
- An objection-handling section (either explicit slides or speaker notes).
- A pricing overview, if the sales process normally surfaces pricing in the first meeting.
- A team credibility section ("Why us"), particularly for enterprise or high-trust sales.
- A clear timeline for the proposed next step.

The arc defines the story. The deck executes it. Keep them separate so the arc can be reviewed and updated without triggering a full deck redesign every time.

## The Master Messaging Document

Create a master messaging document immediately after aligning on the Sales Story Arc.

**Why it is necessary:** Every campaign, webpage, email, and ad will have slightly different messaging for slightly different purposes. Without a master document, messaging drifts as writers build on previously modified messages, not on the source positioning. The positioning embedded in the master document degrades silently across dozens of assets.

**What to include:**

- Core positioning from the Positioning Canvas (all seven elements).
- Sales Story Arc structure (the six steps above, with approved language).
- Approved key messages for each value theme, including exact phrasing.
- Target audience descriptions from Stage 4 (best-fit customers, their goals, their context).
- Competitive alternative descriptions and approved language for addressing each alternative's gaps.
- Proof points: specific data, statistics, benchmark results.
- Approved customer quotes mapped to specific value themes.
- Terms to use and terms to avoid (the vocabulary list prevents category confusion and competitive misalignment).

**Rules for maintaining the document:**

- All marketing assets start from this document, not from a previous campaign.
- Any writer who needs to deviate from the approved language must flag it and justify it. Justified deviations that work well get folded back into the master.
- Review and update the document quarterly, or immediately following any positioning revision.

## Product Roadmap Alignment

When positioning changes, the product roadmap requires review. Positioning defines the market category and the value themes the product must deliver. The roadmap must reinforce those, not contradict them.

**Actions after a positioning change:**

- Prioritize features that directly reinforce the new value themes.
- Deprioritize or defer features that primarily served the old positioning.
- Identify table-stakes features the new market category requires. A product that positions itself as "accounting software" has a different table-stakes list than one that positions itself as "financial services platform." Moving to the new category means you must eventually deliver what customers in that category expect by default.
- Audit the feature backlog: any feature that maps to an old competitive alternative's gap but not to the new positioning should be evaluated for removal from the roadmap.

**Key diagnostic question:** "Does our roadmap reinforce the positioning we've chosen, or does it pull us back toward the positioning we moved away from?"

If the answer is the latter, reconcile roadmap and positioning before publishing either externally.

## Pricing Alignment

Every market category carries pricing expectations. Positioning in a new category without adjusting pricing creates contradictions customers pick up on.

**Patterns:**

- Narrow, specialized categories command higher prices than broad general-purpose categories. "CRM for investment banks" has fewer competitors and more precise value delivery than "general-purpose CRM." Price accordingly.
- Moving to a premium segment requires raising prices to match category expectations. Underpricing a premium category signals "not the real thing." Customers interpret low price as a signal about capability.
- Moving to a broader market may require adjusting the pricing model (not necessarily lowering price) to match how buyers in that market expect to buy — per seat, per usage, per outcome, site license.

**Action:** After finalizing the market category in Stage 5, compare current pricing to the range typical for that category. If there is a significant mismatch, surface it to leadership before committing to the positioning publicly.

## Tracking Positioning Over Time

Positioning is not permanent. Schedule a review every six months. Also trigger a review when any of the following occur:

**Competitive landscape shifts:**
- A credible new competitor enters the market with a similar positioning.
- A large company acquires a player in your segment.
- The market leader makes a move into your specific subsegment or use case.

**External forces change:**
- Regulatory changes create new compliance requirements that affect your category.
- Economic conditions shift customer priorities (for example, from growth investment to cost reduction).
- A technology shift makes new features or entirely new categories viable.
- Customer attitudes shift in a way that changes which value themes resonate.

**When a review is triggered:**

Start at Stage 4 (competitive alternatives). Ask: has the list of alternatives best-fit customers actually use shifted?

- If the alternatives list changed, re-examine unique attributes, value themes, target segment, and market category in order.
- If the alternatives list is unchanged, the positioning is likely still valid. Check whether the trend layer in the market category description needs updating.
- Not every review requires full repositioning. Often, adjusting one element — refining the segment, updating the trend framing, adding a new proof point — is sufficient.

**When not to trigger a review:**

- A competitor launches an attention-getting marketing campaign. Reaction is not repositioning.
- Sales has a bad quarter. Poor sales performance may reflect execution, pricing, or the sales process — not the positioning. Diagnose before repositioning.
- A trend is generating media coverage. Chasing trends by appending trend language to existing positioning ("now with AI") weakens positioning rather than strengthening it.

## Implementation Checklist

Complete in order after finishing Stage 7 (Positioning Canvas) and aligning on the Sales Story Arc.

1. [ ] Sales Story Arc drafted and reviewed by cross-functional team (marketing, sales, product, leadership).
2. [ ] Sales deck created from the arc and reviewed by field sales.
3. [ ] Master messaging document written and distributed.
4. [ ] Website copy updated to reflect new positioning across all key pages (homepage, product pages, about).
5. [ ] Active marketing campaigns reviewed and updated to align with new value themes.
6. [ ] Product roadmap reviewed against new market category and value themes; conflicts resolved.
7. [ ] Pricing reviewed against new market category expectations; adjustments proposed if needed.
8. [ ] Sales team trained on new story arc and messaging; roleplay or pitch practice completed.
9. [ ] Customer success team briefed on new positioning (they field the same questions post-sale).
10. [ ] Six-month positioning review date scheduled on leadership calendar.
