# Fundraising: Procedures, Instruments, and Decisions

Source: Brad Flora "How Startup Fundraising Works" (YC) + Dalton Caldwell "How to Apply and Succeed at YC" (YC) + Mark Zuckerberg & Anne Wojcicki "How to Build the Future" (YC)

---

## 1. Seven Fundraising Myths (Debunked)

### Myth 1: Fundraising is glamorous
**What founders imagine:** Shark Tank — a formal pitch to a panel of investors, high pressure, rapid-fire term sheets.
**Reality:** A series of one-on-one coffee chats and Zoom calls. No panel, no pitch competition. It is a grind of repeated conversations.
**Example:** Fresh Paint (YC company) raised $1.6M by meeting 160 investors over 4 months 18 days. 39 said yes. Check sizes ranged from $5K to $200K. No fancy VCs. Just relentless conversations.
**What to do instead:** Treat fundraising as a sales pipeline. Volume of conversations × conversion rate = capital raised. Remove the glamour framing.

### Myth 2: You need money before you can start
**What founders believe:** "I have a big idea, I need funding to build it."
**Reality:** Build the first version first — even a toy-like version. Get some users. Then raise.
**Why this works:** It costs less than ever to build a prototype and host software. Users can be found on Product Hunt, Hacker News, Twitter, LinkedIn. A startup with a product and users is a train already in motion; investors want to board moving trains.
**Example:** Solugen (YC W17), a chemical manufacturing startup. Instead of asking for $20M to build a plant, they built a desktop-sized reactor, then a small working version that made enough hydrogen peroxide to sell to hot tub supply stores — $10K/month. This got them $4M seed, and eventually $400M total.
**The reframe:** The sequence is idea → build → traction → fundraise. Reversing it means you have nothing to demonstrate except a pitch deck.

### Myth 3: You must impress investors
**What founders believe:** There are magic words; the pitch must be polished and impressive.
**Reality:** Investors get bored by founders trying to impress them. You need to convince them, not impress them. These are different.
**Why:** Most great startups look terrible at first — Airbnb (renting air mattresses), DoorDash (food delivery for suburbs), OpenSea (digital collectibles paid with crypto). Investors know this. A fancy pitch signals you may be optimizing presentation over substance.
**What actually convinces:** Make something, show it, explain what users do with it, note that there is a 1% chance it gets huge, use plain language. That is it.
**Example:** Retool founder David opened his laptop and built a crude internal tool live in a coffee meeting. No deck. Showed the product, talked about what customers made with it. That put in the investor's head: "a lot of companies probably need this." Check written. Now a $4B company.
**If investors are not investing:** The startup is not good enough yet. Fix the startup, not the pitch.

### Myth 4: Fundraising is complicated, slow, and expensive
**What founders believe:** Massive rounds, months of legal fees, complex documentation.
**Reality:** Early rounds use SAFEs (Simple Agreement for Future Equity, created by YC in 2013). SAFEs are 5 pages, free to download from YC's website, require no lawyers, and can be signed in days via Clerky.
**A typical seed round:** $500K to a few million. Closes in weeks, not months. No legal fees. Two terms to discuss: investment amount and valuation cap. (Discounts exist but almost no one uses them.)
**A typical pre-seed / friends-and-family raise:** $25K–$100K. Same mechanics. Same speed.
**What you read in tech press:** Series A and B rounds ($10M–$50M+). These require lawyers, months, and fees. They are not representative of your first round.

### Myth 5: You'll lose control of your company
**What founders believe:** Investors will take board seats, control decisions, look over your shoulder.
**Reality with SAFEs:** No shares change hands at closing. No board seats. No information rights. Investors on SAFEs get shares only at the next priced round. Until then, it is still just you and co-founders.
**Typical dilution at seed:** Selling 10-20% of the company in exchange for enough capital to operate.
**Example:** Zapier (YC S12) raised over $1M from angels and small funds at demo day using SAFEs. Went fully remote in 2012, a decade before it was common. Ran the company exactly how they wanted. Never raised again. $100M annual revenue.
**Bootstrap reframe:** Bootstrapping forever means funding the company out of revenue, which means constant near-death financial pressure, low salaries, consulting detours to generate cash. Fundraising on SAFEs early concentrates the pain into a short period, then eliminates it.

### Myth 6: You need a fancy network
**What founders believe:** Must know VCs, must have attended the right school, must have connections.
**Reality:** Investors are "coin-operated lizard people" who care about making money. Network matters far less than traction.
**Example:** Podium — two guys from Utah, no Silicon Valley network. Sold software to tire shops. By end of YC batch, making tens of thousands per month. Investors noticed. They raised, scaled to $100M annual revenue, raised $200M+ total. No network.
**Cold outreach works:** If you have traction, a cold email to an investor describing what you have built and what metrics look like will get replies. Traction is the filter, not the introduction.
**Warning:** People who offer to fundraise on your behalf ("I know investors") are not worth giving equity to. They should make introductions, then you take the meetings. The investor relationship belongs to the founder.

### Myth 7: Investor rejection means your idea is bad
**Reality:** Investors reject everything, including companies that become extremely valuable.
**Example 1:** Envision (YC, medical device for cancer detection). Founder Serbia Sarma was rejected 50+ times to raise the first $500K. Company was acquired for $275M.
**Example 2:** Whatnot (YC W20, collectibles marketplace). Raised far less than hoped at seed. Investors were skeptical. Two and a half years later: $3.7B valuation, $400M+ raised.
**The math:** You do not need most investors to say yes. Fresh Paint needed 39 yeses out of 160 meetings (24%). Most said no. Enough said yes.
**The founder's job:** Convince yourself the business is working. A few investors will see it. The rest saying no is irrelevant.

## 2. SAFE: The Default Fundraising Instrument

**What it is:** Simple Agreement for Future Equity. Created by YC in 2013. Now the standard for pre-seed and seed rounds.

**Key terms:**
- **Investment amount**: how much the investor puts in
- **Valuation cap**: the maximum valuation at which the SAFE converts to equity in the next priced round. Protects investors from dilution if the company raises at a very high valuation.
- **Discount**: reduction in conversion price for SAFE holders vs. new investors in the next round. Common in theory; rare in practice.
- **MFN (Most Favored Nation)**: if a later SAFE has better terms, earlier SAFE holders get the same terms. Used in uncapped SAFEs.

**Why SAFE vs. convertible notes:**
- No interest rate accruing
- No maturity date forcing a priced round by a deadline
- Simpler: no debt instrument, no repayment obligation
- Faster: no lawyer required for typical angel-sized investments

**When to use something else:**
- Series A (priced round): when the company has enough leverage and enough capital need to justify the complexity of a full priced round with a lead investor, term sheet, and lawyers
- Never do a priced round at pre-seed unless an investor specifically requires it and the terms are favorable

**Mechanics:** Download from YC website for free. Sign via Clerky. No lawyers for typical transactions. Close individual investors one at a time as they commit — no need to wait for everyone to close simultaneously.

## 3. Build-Before-Fundraise: The Iron Rule

**The sequence:** idea → build (even toy version) → users → traction → fundraise.

**Why reversing fails:**
- A pitch deck without product is a hypothesis without evidence. Investors fund evidence.
- Building while fundraising splits attention. Fundraising should be time-boxed, not ongoing.
- Early investors take more risk and demand more equity when there is nothing to show. Traction improves terms.

**Position of strength:** Fundraise when you do not urgently need the money. This gives you the ability to walk away from bad terms and to take more meetings before accepting.

**"Trains in motion" framing:** Investors want to jump on trains that are already in motion. Even minimal motion (a working prototype, 10 users, $1K MRR) is qualitatively different from a pitch deck.

**Fresh Paint data point:** 160 investor meetings, 39 yeses, 4 months 18 days. That is the reality of early fundraising even with a working product. Without one, the conversion rate would have been lower and the process longer.

**Zuckerberg's formulation:** "Pick something you care about, work on it, but do not commit to turning it into a company until it is working." Facebook became a Delaware company when Peter Thiel invested — roughly 6 months after it launched. The company started as something Zuckerberg and his friends wanted for themselves at Harvard.

## 4. YC Application Strategy

**Why apply even if uncertain:**
- Application is designed to be fast; YC intentionally minimized the cost of applying
- Asymmetric bet: small time cost, potential upside of $500K+ + network + office hours
- Applying multiple times is not negative — many admitted founders applied 5-7 times; persistence demonstrates character
- Address feedback from previous rejections explicitly in the next application; reviewers track this and weight it heavily

**What YC reviewers ask themselves (in order):**
1. Who are the founders? Backstory, how they met, who does what
2. What is the idea? Is it clear? Have I heard it before?
3. What exists today — is it built? Are there users?
4. Is there evidence people want this?
5. Are the founders serious? (Left job, multiple applications with increasing progress, full-time commitment)
6. What is impressive or unique? (Founders, traction, or idea)

**What makes a strong application:**
- Reviewers build a "story" from the application. A strong application lets them say: "Two engineers who quit Google three months ago built a cloud hosting tool for React apps. They have one paying customer whose site is live. Their goal is to be the top host for React."
- Weak applications obscure the basics — the reviewer cannot determine what the company does, whether it is built, or whether there are users.
- Fill out every field including founder biographies. Incomplete applications signal low effort.
- Grammar, punctuation, capitalization: details that signal attention to quality.
- Founder video: follow the stated directions exactly. YC weights direction-following in selection criteria as a proxy for detail orientation.
- Be concise. More words is not better. Answer the question directly.

**The GitLab application example:** A few concrete sentences. Named customers (Apple, 100K organizations). Named user type (programmers). Crystal clear what it is and does. No jargon. Reviewers could build the story immediately.

**Honesty requirement:** YC's system rewards honest people acting in good faith. Any sign of misrepresentation (inflated revenue, fabricated customers, misleading founder background) is automatic disqualification. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence — if you claim a Fortune 500 customer, be prepared to demonstrate it.

**Interview preparation:**
- Interviewers ask basic questions. Treat this as a conversation with people you will work with, not an adversarial pitch.
- Founders who recite memorized speeches fail; founders who give robotic answers to questions they anticipated fail.
- Know your own numbers, risks, and what to work on next. Self-awareness about real challenges while maintaining confidence is the target.
- Be yourself: the interviewers are deciding if they can have a productive working relationship with you. Authenticity matters.
- Technical talent on the founding team improves interview odds 5x vs. non-technical teams working on unlaunched ideas.

**What YC does not want:** Warm introductions, pitch decks, pre-meeting calls. Apply on the website. The system is deliberately built to fund complete strangers.

## 5. Tactical Lessons from Founder Experiences

### Zuckerberg: Start With the Problem, Not the Company
**The procedural lesson:** Work on something because you and people like you want it to exist. Do not start by deciding to build a company. The company is a consequence of the thing working, not the goal.
**Why this matters operationally:** When things get hard (and they will), conviction that you are solving a real problem is what sustains you. Founders who started to "build a company" run out of energy when it is hard; founders who started to solve a problem they care about persist.
**Facebook's actual origin:** Built for Zuckerberg and his Harvard friends to use. The company formation happened ~6 months in when raising from Peter Thiel. No prior intent to build a company.

### Zuckerberg: Build a Learning Culture, Not a Prediction Culture
**The procedural lesson:** At any given time, Facebook ran tens of thousands of simultaneous A/B tests. Individual engineers could ship experiments to 10K-100K users without management approval.
**Why it matters:** Organizations that require approval for every experiment learn slowly. Organizations that empower individual engineers to test hypotheses and measure outcomes learn quickly. Speed of learning is a competitive advantage.
**The growth group:** Facebook's growth team — focused specifically on product levers for user growth — was described as the most important product feature Facebook ever built. It is not marketing. It is product engineering applied to growth: data infrastructure, experiment framework, viral loops (People You May Know).
**Tactical implication:** If you are building a network or social product, treat growth engineering as a product function, not a marketing function. The levers are inside the product.

### Wojcicki: The Trough of Sorrow is Real and Expected
**The procedural lesson:** 23andMe launched, sold a lot on day 1, then dropped to 15-25 kits/day. The trough of sorrow is not a signal to abandon the mission; it is a signal to diagnose what is actually blocking growth.
**What Wojcicki did:** Gave hundreds of talks. Never gave the same talk twice. Each one was slightly different to test which messaging resonated. When she got the feedback that the value proposition was unclear, she developed a concrete answer: "Over 50% of customers get a medically meaningful result." Then she started leading with that.
**The process:**
1. Identify the specific objection or point of confusion blocking conversion
2. Develop a direct answer (backed by data, not claims)
3. Test the answer in the next 10 conversations
4. Refine based on what actually changes behavior

**Wojcicki on persistence:** "The most important thing I've learned is the importance of persistence. When you stick with it, you really see a benefit. It takes a decade to see the consequences of your actions." The companies that persist through the trough are the ones that succeed; most quit during it.

### Wojcicki: Hire for Humility
**The procedural lesson:** Among smart people, choose those with humility over those who perform their intelligence. Humble people are open to feedback, willing to learn from other domains, and make others want to work with them.
**The net output test:** Evaluate people on their net effect on the organization, not their individual output. A high-individual-output person who makes everyone else miserable has a negative net effect. The organization moves slower.
**First hires matter disproportionately:** The first 5-7 hires set the bar for all future talent. Bad early hires are nearly impossible to recover from. Wojcicki's two founding scientists set the scientific integrity standard that all subsequent hiring matched against.

## 6. When to Fundraise: Decision Criteria

Fundraise when all of the following are true. Do not fundraise if any are false.

**1. You have traction.**
Either revenue (any amount, growing week over week) or strong engagement retention (flat retention curve at non-zero). "Traction" means the product is working for some set of users — not just that it exists.

**2. You need capital to accelerate something already working.**
Capital should go toward doing more of what is already working: hiring to handle more customers, spending on acquisition to replicate an already-proven unit economics, building infrastructure that is the current bottleneck. Capital should not be used to discover whether the business works.

**3. You can articulate exactly what the money buys.**
"We need $500K to hire 3 engineers to build X feature that Y customers have requested" is good. "We need money to grow" is not. Investors with capital-efficient companies in mind will ask. Have a specific answer.

**4. Fundraising will not consume >50% of your time.**
At the point you are ready to raise, the business should be at a stage where a co-founder can run it while the other one takes meetings. Fundraising that consumes both founders 100% stalls product development and often stalls the metrics investors are looking at. Time-box the raise: set a deadline, run all meetings in parallel (not sequentially), close SAFEs as they come in, stop when you hit the target.

**Raising when you do not need it:** The best fundraising position is not needing the money urgently. This lets you decline bad terms, take more time finding the right investors, and negotiate from strength. Wojcicki's extended fundraising runway came from persistence; she did not take the first available terms. The $275M outcome for Envision came after 50+ rejections — none of those rejections meant the company was bad.  <!-- privacy-ok: термин из книжного конспекта, не финансы владельца -->
