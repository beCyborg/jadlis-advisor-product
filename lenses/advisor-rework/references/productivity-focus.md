# Productivity & Focus — Getting Real Work Done (Takedowns, Productivity, Competitors)

The Rework model of productivity: protect uninterrupted time, kill meetings,
ship "good enough," break work small, and stop obsessing over competitors. All
principles paraphrased; cite with `[RW:…]`.

Tag map for this file: `[RW:TD]` = Takedowns, `[RW:PD]` = Productivity,
`[RW:CO]` = Competitors. Dot notation = principle, e.g. `[RW:PD.4]` = Meetings
are toxic.

---

## Workaholism `[RW:TD.5]`

Working more is not caring more or getting more done — it's just working more.
The culture's badge-of-honor take on all-nighters is not just unnecessary, it's
counterproductive:

- Not sustainable — the burnout crash comes and hits harder.
- Workaholics throw brute-force hours at problems instead of finding the elegant,
  one-tenth-effort solution → inelegant results, and intellectual laziness masked
  by effort.
- They manufacture crises (often unwittingly) because they *like* the overtime
  and enjoy playing hero.
- They wreck team morale: the ass-in-seat mentality makes people who work
  reasonable hours feel inadequate and stay late out of obligation, not output.
- Tired people make bad judgments — values and decision-making skew; you lose the
  ability to tell what deserves extra effort. The real hero already went home
  because she found a faster way.

Advisory use: when a user equates hours with commitment, reframe toward
efficiency and sustainable pace. Ties to `[RW:PD.8]` Go to sleep and `[RW:CU]`
Send people home at 5.

## Illusions of agreement `[RW:PD.1]`

Reports, diagrams, and specs are dead documents that take forever to make and
seconds to forget. The deeper problem: abstractions create *illusions of
agreement* — a hundred people read the same spec and picture a hundred different
things. Get to something real fast to get true, shared understanding (we each
imagine a book's character differently, but all recognize a real person).

- Cite: Alaska Airlines built its "Airport of the Future" as cardboard-box
  mock-ups (podiums, kiosks, belts) plus a live Anchorage prototype tested with
  real passengers — cutting wait times and raising agent productivity. Sam Maloof
  (furniture) couldn't draw the fine details in advance: he discovers how an area
  is done once the chisel is in hand. Get the chisel out; abstraction is a
  distraction.

## Reasons to quit — the seven questions `[RW:PD.2]`

Periodically pull your head up and ask of any work in flight: (1) Why are you
doing this? (2) What problem are you solving — is it real or imaginary? (3) Is
this actually useful or just cool? (cool wears off, useful never does) (4) Are
you adding value or just adding? (too much ketchup ruins the fries) (5) Will this
change behavior? (6) Is there an easier way? (usually yes, and good enough now)
(7) What could you be doing instead? (opportunity cost, acute for small teams).
Don't be timid about abandoning sunk effort — don't throw good time after bad
work. (Full list also in `anti-planning-launch.md` where it gates *whether to
build*.)

## Interruption is the enemy of productivity `[RW:PD.3]`

If you're always staying late and working weekends, it's not too much work — it's
too little *getting done at work*, because of interruptions. You do your best
work at night or early morning because nobody's around. Interruption is not
collaboration; it shatters the day into a start/stop/start series of "work
moments" where nothing meaningful gets finished.

- The alone zone: long unbroken stretches are where productivity magic happens
  (like a plane with no connectivity). It works like REM sleep — you ramp into it
  gradually, and any interruption forces you to start over.
- Prescriptions: carve out a guaranteed no-talk block (e.g., 10 a.m.–2 p.m. no
  talking except lunch; "no-talk Thursdays" instead of casual Fridays). Go all
  the way — during alone time, drop IM, calls, email, meetings. Let go of
  communication addiction.
- Prefer *passive* tools (email — reply when convenient) over *interruptive* ones
  (phone, in-person) so people aren't forced to drop everything.

## Meetings are toxic `[RW:PD.4]`

Meetings are the worst interruption. Why they fail: about abstract words not real
things; tiny information-per-minute; drift off-subject easily; require prep
nobody does; vague agendas with unclear goals; usually include at least one
person wasting everyone's time; and they procreate (one meeting spawns another).

- The true cost is multiplied by headcount: a 1-hour meeting with 10 people is a
  *10-hour* meeting — really ~15 hours with mental switching costs. On a pure
  cost basis, big meetings are liabilities, not assets.
- Scheduling software also inflates them — nobody books a 7-minute meeting in
  Outlook, so a 7-minute need becomes 30. Don't stretch it.
- If a meeting is unavoidable, the rules: set a timer (over when it rings); invite
  as few as possible; always a clear agenda; begin with a specific problem; meet
  *at the site of the problem* pointing at real things; end with a solution and
  one named person responsible for implementing it.

## Good enough is fine `[RW:PD.5]`

People get high on complex solutions and chase the intellectual rush regardless
of whether it's a good idea. Instead find the *judo solution*: maximum efficiency
from minimum effort. Recognize problems are negotiable — need a bird's-eye view?
Don't climb Everest (ambitious); take the elevator up a tall building (judo).

- Cite: political campaign ads — low production (stills not footage, plain-text
  headlines, unseen narrator) but on-air the next day; timeliness beats polish.
- When good enough gets the job done, take it — far better than wasting resources
  or doing nothing because you can't afford the fancy version. You can usually
  turn good enough into great later.

## Quick wins `[RW:PD.6]`

Momentum fuels motivation, and you build momentum by finishing something and
moving on. Nine months in the trenches with nothing to show is a buzzkill that
burns people out. The longer something takes, the less likely you finish it.

- Build a habit of small victories; even a tiny improvement gives a jolt. On
  long projects, dedicate one day per week (or two weeks) to a shippable small
  win — a steady stream of good news energizes the team and gives customers
  something to be excited about.
- The operating question is what you could ship in the next two weeks — then do
  it and get it into customers' hands. Excitement comes from shipping and getting feedback, not
  from planning a menu for a year.

## Don't be a hero `[RW:PD.7]`

Often it's better to be a quitter than a hero. The sunk-cost trap: you estimated
two hours, you're four hours in and a quarter done, and "I can't give up now"
sends you into hero mode — cape on, cut off from the world and from feedback,
sometimes going further down the wrong path.

- The task was worth it at *two* hours, not sixteen; those sixteen hours could
  have moved many other things. 37signals' rule: if anything takes one person
  longer than two weeks, bring others in for a look — even just a review; the
  obvious solution is often invisible to the person stuck in it.
- The obvious solution may be quitting. Quitting isn't always failure; if you've
  overspent on something not worth it, walk away — you can't get the time back,
  and the worst move is wasting more.

## Go to sleep `[RW:PD.8]`

Skipping sleep buys hours now and costs you in spades: it destroys creativity,
morale, and attitude. Occasional all-nighters are fine if you accept the cost;
as a habit the bills mount:

- Stubbornness — tired, you plow down the bad path instead of reconsidering the
  route (the finish line becomes a mirage).
- Lost creativity — the first thing to go; the 10x-effective people aren't
  working 10x harder, they find the one-tenth-effort solution, and that dries up
  without sleep.
- Diminished morale — a tired brain feeds on easy junk tasks and won't attack big
  problems.
- Irritability — patience and tolerance crater. Don't be impressed by people who
  brag about sleep deprivation; it comes back to bite them.

## Your estimates suck `[RW:PD.9]`

Humans are terrible estimators — we imagine best-case scenarios with no delays,
and reality never cooperates. Multi-week/month/year estimates are fantasies.

- Even simple tasks miss by a factor of two or more (the "quick" grocery run that
  takes an hour). And we're not a *little* wrong on big projects — a "six-month"
  guess can be a year. Cite Boston's Big Dig (five years late, billions over) and
  Denver International Airport (16 months late, ~$2B overrun).
- Solution: break the big thing into small things — smaller is easier to estimate
  and less wrong. Restructure one 12-week project as twelve 1-week projects;
  break 30-hour tasks into 6–10-hour chunks; go one step at a time. (Ties to
  `[RW:PD.11]` Make tiny decisions.)

## Long lists don't get done `[RW:PD.10]`

Long to-do lists collect dust and become guilt trips — the more unfinished items,
the worse you feel, until you stop looking and it turns to stress and mess.

- Break one 100-item list into ten 10-item lists — finishing an item now completes
  10% of a list instead of 1%. Same total work, but you find satisfaction,
  motivation, and visible progress.
- Prioritization: don't use numbers/labels (you end up with everything
  "high-priority"). Prioritize *visually* — put the single most important thing at
  the top; when it's done, the next item becomes the next most important. One
  next-thing at a time is enough.

## Make tiny decisions `[RW:PD.11]`

Big decisions are hard to make and hard to change, and once made, ego, pride, and
inertia lock you into defending them even when wrong (saving face beats making
the right call). Make decisions small enough to be effectively temporary — then
you can't make big mistakes, and there's no big penalty for a miss; you just fix
it. Tiny decisions don't preclude big plans — they're how you *reach* big things,
one step at a time.

- Cite: polar explorer Ben Saunders' solo North Pole expedition (31 back-to-back
  marathons, 72 days alone) — the huge goal was too overwhelming to hold, so his
  day-to-day decisions rarely reached beyond "getting to that bit of ice a few
  yards ahead." Attainable goals you can actually nail and build on beat
  pie-in-the-sky ones you never meet.

## Competitors — focus over reaction `[RW:CO]`

### Don't copy `[RW:CO.1]`

Imitation is fine as a student's learning tool (copying a museum painting, playing
along to a Bonham solo), but copying in business skips *understanding* — and
understanding is how you grow. Copy-paste repurposes only the last visible layer
and misses everything buried beneath, so it delivers no substance and nothing to
base future decisions on. Copycats are stuck in a passive, always-behind position.
Test: if someone else is doing the bulk of the work, you're copying. Be
influenced, don't steal.

### Decommoditize your product `[RW:CO.2]`

Success attracts copycats; the defense is to inject *you* into what you sell —
your way of thinking becomes part of the product and can't be copied. Cite Zappos
(same sneakers as Foot Locker, but Tony Hsieh's customer-service obsession is
baked in — no scripts, unlimited call length, call center at HQ, every new hire
does four weeks on phones and in the warehouse) and Polyface Farm/Joel Salatin
(sells a way of thinking — grass-fed, no antibiotics, no shipping, open to
visitors anytime; customers drive 150 miles for it). Pour yourself into the
product *and* everything around it — how you sell, support, explain, and deliver.

### Pick a fight `[RW:CO.3]`

If you think a competitor is bad, say so — being the anti-______ differentiates
you and rallies people who agree. Conflict makes a story; taking a stand stands
out. Cite Dunkin' Donuts as anti-Starbucks (mocking "Fritalian" sizes,
DunkinBeatStarbucks.com), Audi vs. old-luxury Mercedes/BMW/Lexus, Apple's Mac vs.
PC, 7UP the "Uncola," Under Armour as Nike for a new generation. You can even
fight a whole industry (Dyson Airblade vs. the hand-dryer industry; "I Can't
Believe It's Not Butter" names its enemy in the product).

### Underdo your competition `[RW:CO.4]`

The one-upping arms race (they have four features, you need five; they spend
$20k, you spend $30k) is a dead end — it drains money, time, and drive and forces
you onto permanent defense, where you follow instead of lead. Instead do *less*:
solve the simple problems, leave the hairy ones to competitors. Cite fixed-gear
bicycles (one gear, sometimes no brakes — simpler, lighter, cheaper, low
maintenance) and the Flip camcorder (took big market share while deliberately
lacking big screen, photos, tapes, menus, settings, light, viewfinder, effects,
headphone jack, lens cap, memory card, optical zoom — it does a few things well
and goes where fancier cameras don't). Don't hide that you do less — highlight it,
be proud, sell it as aggressively as rivals sell feature lists. (Ties to
`[RW:PR.2]` Build half a product.)

### Who cares what they're doing? `[RW:CO.5]`

Watching competitors becomes an obsession that breeds stress and anxiety — bad
soil for growing anything — and it's pointless because the landscape changes
constantly and is out of your control. Focus inward: what's going on in here
matters more than out there. Over-watching dilutes your vision and makes you
reactionary instead of visionary — you end up shipping their product with a
different coat of paint. Building "the iPod killer" already lost: you let them set
the parameters, and you can't beat the one making the rules — you must *redefine*
the rules. It's not win-or-lose; their profits and costs are theirs, yours are
yours. If you'd just replicate everyone else, there's no point to your existence.
