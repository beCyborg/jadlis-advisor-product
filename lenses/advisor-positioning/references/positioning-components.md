# Positioning Components

## Context

Use this reference at Stages 1–3 of the positioning process: when identifying competitive alternatives (Stage 1), mapping unique attributes (Stage 2), and translating attributes into value (Stage 3). This file covers the full 5+1 component model, their mandatory ordering, and the Feature→Benefit→Value chain that most teams skip or botch.

---

## The Five (Plus One) Components

Positioning has five required components and one optional. They are not independent — each one constrains and informs the next. Treating them as a checklist (rather than a dependency chain) produces positioning that is internally inconsistent and fails in the market.

### 1. Competitive Alternatives

**Definition**: What customers would actually use if your product didn't exist.

This is the foundation. Everything else is built on top of it. Get this wrong and all subsequent components are wrong.

**Critical mistake**: Teams list only direct competitors — other companies selling a similar product. Dunford is explicit: the real competitive alternatives are almost always broader.

Examples of non-obvious alternatives:
- Spreadsheets (Excel, Google Sheets)
- Manual processes ("we have someone do it in the back office")
- "Hire an intern" or "hire a consultant"
- Doing nothing — accepting the problem as a cost of business
- A combination of three other tools cobbled together
- A custom internal build

**Why it matters**: If you say "our differentiator is X," but the customer's real alternative is a spreadsheet that doesn't have X either, then X is not a differentiator — it's a baseline expectation. You cannot identify unique attributes without knowing what you are unique against.

**How to identify**: Ask customers "If you couldn't use us, what would you do?" not "What other tools did you consider?" The first question gets at real alternatives. The second gets at category competitors.

**Cluster alternatives**: Group similar alternatives so you can see patterns. A segment that would default to Excel is different from one that would buy a direct competitor — they need different positioning.

### 2. Unique Attributes

**Definition**: Features or capabilities your product has that the competitive alternatives lack.

Not everything you have — only what alternatives lack. If every alternative has it, it is not unique.

**Types of attributes that qualify**:
- Technical features (a specific algorithm, architecture, integration)
- Delivery model (on-premise vs. cloud, API-first, white-label)
- Business model (usage-based pricing, no contracts, pay-as-you-go)
- Partnerships or certifications (official partner status, regulatory compliance)
- Domain expertise embedded into the product
- Proprietary data sets or training data

**Provability requirement**: Attributes must be provable. Your opinion does not count. What counts:
- Third-party validation (analyst reports, certifications, awards)
- Customer quotes on the record
- Benchmark data from independent tests
- Certifications (SOC 2, ISO 27001, etc.)

**Consideration vs. Retention — pick the right ones**: Not all attributes belong in positioning. Two types exist:

- **Consideration attributes**: What customers evaluate at purchase. "Does it integrate with Salesforce?" "Can it handle 50,000 concurrent users?" These determine whether they buy. Lead with these.
- **Retention attributes**: What matters for renewal. "Their support team is amazing." "Easy to work with." These matter after the sale. Customers who are not convinced by consideration attributes never stay long enough to experience retention attributes. Do not lead with retention attributes in positioning.

### 3. Value (and Proof)

**Definition**: The benefit that the unique attributes enable — stated in customer outcome terms, backed by evidence.

Value is not a feature. Value is not a benefit. Value is the business outcome the customer achieves because of the benefit the feature enables. See the Feature→Benefit→Value chain below for the full mechanics.

**Proof requirement**: Value claims must be supported by data or third-party opinions. "Our customers save time" is an opinion. "Our customers reduce reporting time from 3 days to 4 hours — here are three case studies" is provable. Data and third-party opinions are difficult to refute. Your marketing copy is not.

**Value frames the customer's cost-benefit analysis**: If the customer cannot clearly see what they gain, they default to lowest price. Value is what justifies a price premium.

### 4. Target Market Characteristics

**Definition**: The specific characteristics of buyers who care most about your value.

**Critical mistake**: Teams describe demographics — "mid-market SaaS companies, 50–500 employees, US-based." Demographics describe who might buy. Positioning requires characteristics that describe who cares intensely about the specific value you deliver.

**What "characteristics" means in practice**: Situational, behavioral, or operational traits that create the need your product addresses.

Examples:
- "Companies processing cross-border payments in multiple currencies" (not "fintech companies")
- "Teams where engineers are on call and get paged at night" (not "DevOps teams")
- "Organizations where salespeople build their own spreadsheet pipeline trackers" (not "SMB sales teams")
- "Founders who have already validated a product but can't hire fast enough to build it" (not "early-stage startups")

**How to find the right characteristics**: Look at your best customers — the ones who bought quickly, paid full price, renewed without negotiation, and refer others. What situational characteristics do they share? That is your target market.

**Derive from value**: The target market is the group that cares most about the value in Component 3. This is why order matters — you cannot define the segment without knowing the value first.

### 5. Market Category

**Definition**: The frame of reference you provide so prospects can quickly understand what you are and what assumptions apply.

**Market category is a shortcut**: When you say "project management tool," the customer immediately loads a set of assumptions — what competitors exist, roughly what it costs, what features it has, what problems it solves. You do not have to explain all of this. The category does it for you.

**Market category works for you or against you**: If you pick a category where your strengths are table stakes and your differentiators are invisible, the category works against you. If you pick a category where your differentiators are exactly what that category promises, it works for you.

**Three market style strategies** (see `market-category.md` for full mechanics):
- Head to Head: compete in an established category
- Big Fish, Small Pond: compete in a sub-segment of an established category
- Create a New Game: define a new category

**Market category is not your company description**: Do not say "we are a SaaS platform for enterprise teams." That describes the delivery model and buyer size — it does not invoke category assumptions.

### 6. Relevant Trends (Bonus, Optional)

**Definition**: A macro trend — technological, regulatory, behavioral — that makes your product important right now.

**Why it is optional**: Trends create urgency and relevance. If a genuine trend maps onto your value, layering it on strengthens positioning. If the trend is weak or only vaguely related, it dilutes everything else.

**The rule**: Trends are always layered on top of an established market category. They never replace the market category. A customer must understand what you are before they can understand why it is timely.

**What counts as a valid trend**:
- Regulatory changes creating new compliance requirements
- Adoption curves (AI, cloud migration, remote work normalization)
- Macro events that change how buyers operate (supply chain disruption, economic contraction)
- Platform shifts (mobile-first, API-first, edge computing)

**What does not count**: Internal growth metrics, your funding round, industry awards, your own product roadmap.

## Component Flow: The Mandatory Order

```
Alternatives → Attributes → Value → Who Cares → Market → Trends
```

**This order is not arbitrary.** Each component is only meaningful in context of the previous one.

- Attributes are "unique" only relative to specific alternatives. Change the alternatives, and what is unique changes.
- Value derives from attributes. Different attributes produce different value.
- The target segment is whoever cares most about that specific value. Value first, then segment.
- Market category is chosen to make the value obvious to the segment. You cannot pick the right category without knowing the segment.
- Trends layer on the market category to add timeliness. Category must be established first.

Working backwards or skipping steps produces circular answers. Teams that start with market category ("we are an AI analytics platform") work backwards to justify the label rather than deriving it from what makes them genuinely different.

## Feature → Benefit → Value Chain

This is the translation that most product and marketing teams skip. Engineers especially conflate features with benefits. Benefits are not value. Value is the business outcome.

**The chain**:
```
Feature (what the product does) → Benefit (what that enables for the user) → Value (what business outcome it produces)
```

**The test**: If a customer's response to your feature or benefit is "So what?" — you have not completed the chain. Keep asking "So what?" until you reach a business outcome that has a dollar sign or a strategic implication attached to it.

**Examples**:

| Feature | Benefit | Value |
|---------|---------|-------|
| 15-megapixel camera | Sharp photo images | Images can be zoomed and printed in large format without degradation — usable in retail, print, and billboard production |
| All-metal construction | Stronger frame that resists impact | Frame lasts 5× longer than polymer alternatives, saving $50K/year on replacements in high-use environments |
| One-click report generation | Fast, easy reporting | Every part of the organization makes decisions on accurate, up-to-date metrics — not last quarter's numbers |
| 24-hour support | Support always available | Global operations have access to help across every time zone without waiting for business hours |
| Patented fuzzy logic algorithm | Faster system response times | Support agents can resolve issues while the customer is still on the phone — reducing average handle time and increasing CSAT |
| API-first architecture | Integrates with any existing stack | No forced migrations or rip-and-replace — teams adopt without disrupting existing workflows |
| Automated audit trail | Every action logged without manual effort | Compliance reviews that took 3 weeks now take 2 hours — regulatory risk is contained |

**Common failure modes**:

1. **Stopping at the feature**: "We have 15 megapixels." Nobody buys megapixels — they buy the ability to produce large-format images without degradation.

2. **Stopping at the benefit**: "Our support is always available." Always-available support is only valuable if the customer is running global operations or has time-sensitive incidents. Who cares — and why?

3. **Claiming value without proof**: "Our customers save significant time." How much? Compared to what? Supported by whose data? Value claims without proof are indistinguishable from marketing copy.

**For engineering-heavy teams**: Run every feature through the chain before positioning conversations. The feature list is internal information. The value list is what goes in front of customers.

## Clustering into Value Themes

After mapping features to value, related value points should be grouped from the customer's perspective — not from the engineering team's perspective.

**Example cluster**:
- "Works on iOS and Android" + "Works without an internet connection" + "Data syncs when connectivity resumes" → **Theme: Supports field teams in remote environments**

The individual features are distinct. The customer outcome is unified.

**Target**: Reduce all value points to 1–4 themes. More than four themes means the positioning has no center of gravity. The customer cannot hold seven value claims in memory — they will default to price or familiarity.

**Clustering reveals**: which value is actually central (the theme with the most contributing features), which features have no value home (candidates for removal from positioning), and which customer segments care about which themes (input into target market definition).

## The Positioning Canvas

### Why the Traditional Positioning Statement Fails

The classic template — "For [target customer] who [has this need], [product name] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [competitors], our product [key differentiator]." — has four structural problems:

1. **Assumes the answers**: It prompts you to fill in blanks you have not yet earned the right to fill. It does not help you discover positioning — it just formats whatever assumptions you already have.

2. **Reinforces the status quo**: Because it starts with a category assumption, it discourages considering alternative framings. You write yourself into the first category that comes to mind.

3. **Produces unusable output**: Nobody references a positioning statement after the meeting where it was written. It is too long to remember, too awkward to say, and too generic to act on. Dunford calls completing it "malicious compliance" — the work is done, the output is useless.

4. **Is impossible to communicate**: Sales cannot quote it. Marketing cannot translate it. Product cannot prioritize from it. It exists only as a document artifact.

### The Positioning Canvas Format

The canvas is the working document — a single-page representation of all five components and their relationships.

```
Product: [name] — [one-line description]
Market category: [category] (+ [trend, if validated])

| Competitive Alternatives | Unique Attributes | Value Themes | Who Cares a Lot |
|--------------------------|-------------------|--------------|-----------------|
| [clustered alternatives] | [mapped attributes]| [1–4 themes] | [characteristics]|

Market Style: [Head to Head / Big Fish, Small Pond / Create a New Game]
Positioning rationale: [one sentence: why this category makes our value obvious to this segment]
```

**Canvas properties**:
- Shows relationships — attributes map to alternatives, value maps to attributes, segment maps to value
- Actionable across functions — marketing writes copy from it, sales uses it to qualify, product prioritizes from it
- Fits on one page — forces prioritization
- Easy to share and update — a living document, not a filing-cabinet artifact
- Comparable across options — when testing multiple positioning approaches, canvases can be placed side by side

The canvas is not the final deliverable. It is the working artifact that teams use to test, stress, and communicate positioning before it goes into sales decks, website copy, or product packaging.

## Single Product vs. Multi-Product Companies

### Single Product

Position the product and the company as the same thing. Customers should not need to remember two separate value propositions. Fewer claims, clearer signal.

All positioning work flows from the product. Company description follows product positioning.

### Multi-Product

Product positioning and company positioning are separate — but must be hierarchically linked.

**Rule of thumb**:
- If most revenue comes from one product: position that product first. Company positioning follows.
- If selling products as a bundle or suite: position the company first, then individual products within the company frame.
- If products serve different segments: each product may require independent positioning; company-level positioning describes the common value or mission.

**Failure mode**: Company positioning is so generic ("we help organizations transform digitally") that it provides no context for any product. Each product is then positioned in a vacuum, with no accumulated trust or recognition from the company brand.

**Avoid cannibalization**: If two products can serve the same customer, clarify which one is recommended for which situation. Unresolved overlap creates sales confusion and internal competition.
