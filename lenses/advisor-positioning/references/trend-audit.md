# Trend Audit

## Context

Stage 6 of the positioning pipeline. After choosing a market category (Stage 5), optionally layer a trend to make the product more relevant right now. The Trend Layer is not required. A well-positioned product without a trend beats a trend-washed product every time. "None" is always a valid answer.

---

## The Venn Diagram Test

Three circles must overlap for a trend to be safely used:

1. **Product Strengths** — The trend connects to capabilities your product actually has.
2. **Market Context** — The trend makes sense within your chosen market category.
3. **Customer Interest** — Your target customers understand and care about this trend enough that it influences their buying decisions.

Only where all three intersect is it safe to use the trend. If any circle is missing, the trend either misleads, confuses, or simply fails to land.

---

## The Four Quadrants

### 1. HOT — All three circles overlap (Product + Market + Customer)

The trend reinforces your positioning and amplifies your value. Safe and powerful to use.

Identify this quadrant by: customers already asking about the trend, competitors scrambling to claim it, and your product genuinely delivering on it.

**Example:** A retail solution with cross-platform data access. Trend: mobility. Retailers understood mobility, the product supported tablets and mobile devices, and the market category (sales associate tools) fit naturally. The trend made the product feel timely and urgent.

### 2. Lies — Trend + Market, but no Product connection

The trend sounds right for your market, but your product doesn't actually deliver on it. Dangerous. This is positioning fraud, and the market eventually finds out.

**Example:** Long Blockchain (iced tea company). Changed its name in 2018 to capitalize on blockchain hype. No blockchain product. No blockchain strategy. Stock soared briefly on speculation, then collapsed. The company was delisted from Nasdaq. The gap between claimed trend and actual product capability destroyed credibility.

Detect this quadrant by asking: "Can we demo how our product uses this trend in 60 seconds?" If no, the connection is a lie.

### 3. Cool but Confusing — Trend + Product, but no Market

Your product genuinely uses the trend's technology or approach, but you haven't established a market category first. The result sounds innovative but baffles prospects.

**Example:** "Sharing economy for pets" or "Uber for cats." The trend (sharing economy) connects to the product (pet service marketplace), but without first establishing the market category (marketplace for pet services), nobody understands what the company actually does.

Detect this quadrant by asking: "If someone hears our positioning for the first time, do they know what we are?" If not, establish the market category first, then return to trends.

### 4. Good but Boring — Product + Market, but no Trend

Well-positioned in a clear market. Not connected to any trendy narrative. This is fine. Many successful, profitable companies live here permanently.

**Example:** Accounting software that does accounting well. No trend needed. The market category is established, the value is clear, the customers know what they're buying.

Key rule: "It's always better to be a little boring than completely baffling." A boring but clear positioning closes deals. A confusing but trendy positioning stalls them.

---

## Trends vs. Market Categories

| Aspect | Market Category | Trend |
|--------|----------------|-------|
| Purpose | Helps customers understand WHAT you are | Helps customers understand WHY NOW |
| Stability | Relatively stable over time | Changes as markets evolve |
| Examples | CRM, accounting software, data warehouse | AI, DevOps, direct-to-consumer, mobility |
| Standalone? | Yes — sufficient for positioning | No — must be layered on a market category |
| Required? | Always | Optional |

"AI" is a trend, not a market category. "AI-powered CRM" = market (CRM) + trend (AI). Establish the market first. Layer the trend second. Never reverse this order.

---

## Safe Trend Rules

**Market first, trend second.** Always establish your market category before layering a trend. Trend without market creates confusion, not clarity.

**Trend is not a substitute for a market category.** "Blockchain" is a trend. "Blockchain-based supply chain management" is a market with a trend applied. The market does the heavy lifting of helping customers understand what you are.

**Connection must be real and demonstrable.** If you cannot explain in one sentence how the trend connects to your product's actual capabilities, do not use it. The test: can you demo the connection in under 60 seconds?

**Trends are optional.** Skip if there is no natural fit. Skipping the trend layer does not weaken Stages 1-5 positioning. It only means you are not adding the "why now" layer.

**Don't force it.** If the team debates for more than 15 minutes about how to connect a trend to the product, the connection is probably not natural. Forced connections become the "Lies" quadrant.

**One trend maximum.** Do not stack trends. "AI-blockchain-IoT-enabled CRM" is a red flag, not a positioning statement. Stacking signals desperation, not innovation.

---

## How to Evaluate a Trend (Step-by-Step Audit)

1. List 3-5 trends your target customers are currently aware of and interested in. Source this from customer conversations, industry press they read, and conference themes they attend.

2. For each trend, answer three questions:
   - Does our product genuinely leverage or embody this trend? (Product circle)
   - Does this trend make sense within our chosen market category? (Market circle)
   - Do our target customers understand and care about this trend enough that it influences their buying? (Customer circle)

3. Score each trend: all three YES = safe to use. Any NO = skip this trend.

4. If multiple trends pass all three tests: choose the one most directly connected to your unique value. Align with the competitive alternative and value attributes already defined in Stages 1-4.

5. If no trends pass: skip this stage entirely. Move directly to Stage 7 (documenting and socializing the positioning).

## Examples from the Book

### Redgate Software + DevOps (HOT quadrant)

- **Market:** Database tools — well-established, Redgate was already a leader.
- **Trend:** DevOps — growing movement around developer-led operations, automation, and continuous delivery.
- **Connection:** Nobody was talking about databases in a DevOps context. New data privacy regulations were also creating pressure around database change management. Redgate's products directly addressed database deployment pipelines, a real gap in the DevOps conversation.
- **Result:** "Database DevOps" positioning drove a 100% increase in inbound leads. Customers who previously bought one Redgate tool began buying multiple products because the trend reframed the entire workflow Redgate supported.

### Sampler + Direct-to-Consumer (HOT quadrant)

- **Market:** Product sampling — a mature, established market.
- **Trend:** Direct-to-consumer — brands building direct relationships with buyers rather than relying on retail intermediaries.
- **Connection:** Sampler helped brands target specific buyer segments, collect consumer data, and build proprietary lists — exactly what brands needed to execute direct-to-consumer strategies.
- **Result:** "Direct-to-consumer sampling" made Sampler a strategic priority purchase instead of a discretionary marketing spend. Deals closed faster. Budget objections decreased because the purchase now connected to a board-level initiative.

### "Sharing Economy for Pets" (Cool but Confusing — anti-example)

- **Trend:** Sharing economy.
- **Market:** Not established. Should have been "marketplace for pet services."
- **Result:** Completely baffling. The trend made it sound innovative. The missing market category made it impossible to understand. Prospects couldn't place it in any existing frame of reference, so they defaulted to confusion and moved on.

### Long Blockchain (Lies — anti-example)

- **Trend:** Blockchain.
- **Product:** Iced tea.
- **Connection:** None whatsoever.
- **Result:** Stock briefly surged on trend hype, then collapsed. The company was delisted from Nasdaq. The market discovered the gap between claimed capability and actual product. Trend fraud does not survive customer scrutiny.

## When to Skip Trends

Skip the trend layer when:

- No trend naturally connects to both your product capabilities and your market category.
- The trend is so broad that every competitor already claims it (e.g., "cloud" post-2015, "AI" in 2024 for many categories). Ubiquitous trend claims neutralize each other and add no differentiation.
- Your target customers are pragmatic buyers who respond to ROI and reliability, not to trend narratives.
- The trend is past peak hype. Gartner Hype Cycle position matters. Claiming a trend in the Trough of Disillusionment creates skepticism, not excitement.
- Adding a trend would make your positioning statement longer and harder to parse, not sharper and easier.
- Your positioning is already strong and distinctive from Stages 1-5. Additional clarity beats additional sizzle.

If you skip, move directly to Stage 7. The positioning work is complete without a trend layer. Do not revisit this stage unless market conditions change or a genuinely relevant trend emerges.

---

## Output from This Stage

A single decision with supporting rationale:

**Trend adopted:** [trend name] — layered on [market category] because [one-sentence connection to product capabilities and customer interest].

OR

**No trend:** Positioning is complete at Stage 5 market category. No trend with sufficient Venn overlap identified.

This output feeds directly into Stage 7: documenting the full positioning statement and socializing it internally.
