# Idea Evaluation

Source: YC Startup School — Modules 1 and 2 (Jared Friedman, Dalton Caldwell, Michael Seibel)

---

## 10-Question Evaluation Framework

Use these 10 questions in order when evaluating any startup idea. Question 1 (Founder-Market Fit) is a hard filter — if the answer is "no," stop and do not proceed to the remaining questions until you have reconsidered the team or the idea.

### Question 1: Do you have Founder-Market Fit?

Ask: Is this team the obvious team to execute this specific idea?

Good answer: The founder has direct domain expertise — prior work, industry experience, or a deeply personal encounter with the problem. Example: Tracy (construction industry background) + Ralph (iPad developer) starting PlanGrid to put construction blueprints on iPads. Anyone imagining the ideal team for that product would describe exactly that team.

Bad answer: The founders are legitimately expert in something, but the idea they are working on is unrelated to that expertise. This pattern appears frequently in YC applications.

Instruction: If FMF is absent, either (a) change the idea to something the team is actually suited for, or (b) acknowledge that this is a deliberate exception and explain why the team can compensate. Do not evaluate the remaining 9 questions as though FMF doesn't matter — it is the most important single criterion.

Reframe for idea generation: Instead of searching for "a good startup idea," search for "a good idea for this specific team." This reframe structurally filters out ideas that lack FMF.

---

### Question 2: How big is the market?

Ask: Does this market currently exceed $1B, or is it small but growing rapidly?

Good answer (big now): Market is already large and the startup is targeting a meaningful slice of it.

Good answer (small but fast-growing): Market is small today but has a clear and plausible growth trajectory. Example: Coinbase launched in 2012 when the Bitcoin trading market was minuscule, but even then it was reasonably clear that if Bitcoin succeeded the market would reach billions.

Bad answer: Market is small and there is no structural reason to expect rapid growth.

Instruction: Do not dismiss a small market if the growth rate is high and the mechanism of growth is identifiable. Do not claim a large market without verifying that the startup is actually targeting the part that generates revenue, not the total addressable market in the broadest possible sense.

---

### Question 3: How acute is the problem?

Ask: Is this a problem people actively suffer from, or merely a mild inconvenience?

Good answer: The alternative to the startup's solution is literally nothing. Example: Brex (Winter 2017) built a corporate credit card for startups. Before Brex, startups literally could not get a corporate credit card from any bank. The alternative was nothing — that is a good problem.

Bad answer: People can articulate the problem in words but in practice do not seek a solution, do not pay for workarounds, and do not complain about the existing situation unprompted. This is the "solution in search of a problem" (CISP) failure pattern.

Instruction: When assessing acuteness, ask: what do users do right now instead? If the answer is "nothing" or "a painful manual workaround," the problem is acute. If the answer is "roughly the same thing but slightly less convenient," the problem may not be acute enough.

---

### Question 4: Do you have competition, and is that good or bad?

Ask: Are there existing competitors, and what does their existence tell you about the market?

Good answer: Competitors exist but none has fully solved the problem. Users of existing products are dissatisfied. Example: When Dropbox launched, there were approximately 20 cloud file storage companies. Most people used none of them — meaning the problem was real but unsolved. Drew had a specific insight (sync via OS integration instead of manual web uploads) that was a step-function improvement.

Bad answer: No competitors exist because no one wants the product, not because the problem is unsolved.

Bad answer: Competitors are entrenched and the startup has no specific insight that competitors have missed.

Instruction: The presence of competition is a positive signal, not a negative one. When competition exists, the evaluation question shifts from "is there a market?" to "what specific insight does this startup have that incumbents have missed?" If no such insight can be articulated, the competitive situation is actually bad.

Instruction: Distinguish between (a) crowded but unsolved — good, (b) crowded and solved — bad unless there is a structural insight, (c) empty because no one wants it — bad.

---

### Question 5: Do you or people you know personally want this?

Ask: Would the founders use this product? Do they know real people — not hypothetical users — who would use it?

Good answer: The founders encounter the problem themselves and have identified specific real individuals who want the solution.

Bad answer: The founders cannot identify a single person who genuinely wants the product. This is a strong signal that the problem may not be real.

Instruction: If the answer to both "do you want this?" and "do you know people personally who want this?" is "no," treat this as a warning flag that warrants immediate user research before further development.

---

### Question 6: Has something recently changed that makes this idea newly viable?

Ask: Is there a new technology, a regulatory change, or a new behavior pattern that did not exist two to five years ago and that creates this opportunity?

Good answer: A structural change in the world created a gap that could not have been filled before. Example: Checker (background check API) became viable because DoorDash, Instacart, and Uber scaled rapidly and needed to run background checks on large pools of gig workers — a use case that did not exist before the gig economy emerged. Existing background check companies were not suited for this use case.

Bad answer: The idea has been around for years and no structural change explains why it would work now when it did not work before.

Instruction: If this question reveals that the idea is not newly possible, check whether the idea is a tar pit (see section below). Ideas that have been tried repeatedly without success, where nothing structural has changed, are unlikely to succeed this time.

---

### Question 7: Are there good proxies?

Ask: Is there a large company doing something similar in a different market or adjacent domain that validates the demand?

Good answer: A proxy exists and is succeeding at scale. Example: Rappi (food delivery in Latin America) used DoorDash as a proxy — DoorDash proved the food delivery model worked; Rappi applied it to a market DoorDash had not entered.

Bad answer: No proxies exist because the concept is entirely novel and unproven. This is not automatically disqualifying but it removes an important validation signal.

Instruction: Proxies are not direct competitors — they are companies that prove the underlying demand without competing in the exact same market.

---

### Question 8: Is this something you could commit to for years?

Ask: Can the founders sustain motivation for this idea over multiple years of difficult execution?

Instruction: Handle this question carefully. The honest answer is often "not sure" at the start. That is acceptable. The key insight from YC data is that passion is frequently acquired rather than pre-existing. Many of the best startup ideas are in boring spaces — tax accounting software, payroll software — where no one starts out passionate. Founders who successfully execute on these ideas typically become passionate about the business as it begins to work.

Do not disqualify an idea solely because the founders are not currently enthusiastic about the domain. Do disqualify if the founders actively find the work ethically objectionable or psychologically damaging, since that will block execution.

---

### Question 9: Is this a scalable business?

Ask: Can this business grow without linearly scaling human effort?

Good answer: Pure software — yes, by default. Check this off.

Bad answer: The business model requires high-skill human labor per customer (agencies, dev shops, consulting). These are services businesses, not scalable startups.

Instruction: Flag services businesses immediately. They can generate revenue but cannot achieve the growth rates that define venture-scale startups.

---

### Question 10: Is this a good idea space?

Ask: Is the category of ideas this startup belongs to one with a historically high success rate?

An idea space is one level of abstraction above a specific startup — not the company itself but the class of companies it belongs to. Examples: fintech infrastructure, vertical SaaS for enterprise, software for hospitals, infrastructure monitoring tools.

Good answer: The idea space has produced multiple successful companies in the past decade. From YC's data: fintech infrastructure and vertical SaaS had astonishingly high rates of producing billion-dollar companies over the past decade.

Bad answer: The idea space has low historical success rates — consumer hardware, social networks, ad tech had significantly lower hit rates.

Why this matters: If the initial idea within the space does not work, a fertile idea space contains adjacent ideas to drift into. Example: Fivetran (YC W13) built two data analysis tools that both failed to sell. Because they stayed in the data analysis idea space, each failed attempt taught them more about what customers actually wanted, and they eventually found a product that worked (data pipeline connectors). They could not have done this if they had been in an infertile idea space.

## CISP and Tar Pit Detection

### What is a CISP

A CISP (Solution In Search of a Problem) is an idea that starts from a technology or capability and then looks for a problem to apply it to.

Pattern: "AI is interesting — what could I apply it to?" → browse for a problem → identify a superficially plausible problem → build for it.

Why this fails: When you search for a problem to fit a solution, you find problems that sound plausible but are not real. Users will not pay for solutions to problems they do not actually experience.

Correct direction: Start with a real problem, then ask what technology enables a solution.

---

### What is a Tar Pit Idea

A tar pit idea is a startup idea that:
1. Is encountered by large numbers of potential founders
2. Seems easy to build and obviously useful
3. Has a structural reason why it cannot be made to work — but that reason is not visible from the outside
4. Has been attempted repeatedly (often for a decade or more) with consistent failure

The structural flaw makes it look like a good opportunity from a distance while trapping anyone who attempts it.

Canonical example from YC: The "social plans app" — a mobile app to coordinate weekend plans with friends. This idea has been submitted to YC applications for over 20 years. The superficial logic is compelling: everyone experiences the problem, the app seems simple to build. The structural flaws that have defeated every attempt include cold start (the app is worthless without your friends on it), weak retention (the problem is intermittent and low-stakes), and competition from existing messaging apps that are already embedded in the social graph.

---

### Common Tar Pit Categories

The following categories have structural issues that cause repeated failure. When an idea falls into one of these categories, require explicit articulation of what structural flaw the current team has solved that previous attempts could not:

- Consumer social apps (requires solving cold start and retention at scale)
- Dating apps (winner-take-all market with extreme network effects; Tinder dominates)
- Event discovery apps (problem is not acute enough; people already use multiple channels and are satisfied)
- Any "app to coordinate plans with friends" variant
- Peer-to-peer marketplaces in categories where trust is the core blocker and the team has no trust-establishment mechanism

---

### Tar Pit Triage Protocol

When evaluating an idea that may be a tar pit:
1. Google the exact idea. Find who has tried this before.
2. Find and contact at least one founder who attempted this idea previously.
3. Ask explicitly: what was the hard part? Why did your attempt not succeed?
4. Determine whether the structural flaw has been resolved. If it has not, this is still a tar pit.

## Idea Generation: 7 Recipes in Priority Order

These recipes are ordered from most likely to produce a good idea (Recipe 1) to least likely. Start with Recipe 1 if the team has relevant domain expertise.

---

### Recipe 1: Start from your team's expertise

For each founder on the team, list:
- Every job held (full-time and internship)
- Other significant life experiences that produced domain knowledge

For each item on the list, ask:
- What problems did I encounter in this context?
- What do I know that most people outside this field do not know?
- What opportunities or inefficiencies was I in a special position to see?

Then restrict the idea search to the idea spaces that emerge from this exercise.

Why this works: Any idea generated this way has automatic Founder-Market Fit. FMF is the most important evaluation criterion. Starting here means the hardest criterion is already met before the search begins.

Example application: Rezi founders had backgrounds in real estate and debt financing. They restricted their YC search to "intersection of real estate and fintech." This is a fertile idea space that had produced many billion-dollar companies. Their search was fast and produced a strong idea with perfect FMF.

Warning: Many founders who are genuine domain experts apply to YC with ideas completely unrelated to their expertise. This is a structural error. Use the expertise you have.

---

### Recipe 2: Start from a problem you personally encountered

The strongest version of this recipe: a problem you encountered that people outside your background cannot see.

Example: Vetcove founders' father is a veterinarian. Growing up, they observed that he ordered supplies by calling a 1-800 number — a clearly antiquated process with no web alternative. Thousands of veterinarians knew this problem existed. No one had built the solution because veterinarians do not start tech startups, and tech founders did not encounter this problem in their daily lives. Vetcove launched with no competition because the problem had been invisible to the people who could solve it.

This is the ideal setup: you see a real problem, you are positioned to build the solution, and the lack of competition does not mean there is no demand — it means the problem was invisible to everyone else.

Instruction: Apply Recipe 2 in combination with Recipe 1. Work through every prior job and life experience. For each, ask what problems were visible to you that outsiders would not notice.

---

### Recipe 3: Things you personally wish existed

Ask: What product do I want that does not exist?

Apply immediately: Is there a structural reason why this does not exist? If the answer is "I don't know," treat this as a potential tar pit and research whether others have tried to build it.

Example: DoorDash founders were Stanford undergrads who wanted food delivered from local restaurants to their dorm. Before DoorDash, this was not possible. They built the thing they wanted.

Warning: This is the recipe most likely to produce tar pit ideas. The test is not "does this seem useful to me" but "is there a structural reason why this doesn't already exist, and can I resolve that reason?"

---

### Recipe 4: Look for what has recently changed

Ask: What changed in the world in the last one to three years — new technology, new regulation, new behavior, new infrastructure — that created an opportunity that did not previously exist?

Apply to a range of changes: new APIs, new platforms, new legal frameworks, new market behaviors post-pandemic, new hardware capabilities, new data availability.

Example: Gather.town pivoted during the COVID pandemic because the change in how people worked created a clear demand for virtual co-presence tools that did not have an obvious market before March 2020.

Example: Checker (background check API) became viable when gig economy platforms scaled and needed to run background checks on millions of workers through an API — a use case that did not exist before Uber, DoorDash, and Instacart reached scale.

---

### Recipe 5: Successful companies in adjacent markets

Find a company that works in one market and ask whether the same model could work in a different market where it has not yet been tried.

Example: Nuvo Cargo is Flexport for Latin America — freight forwarding and import/export logistics for US-Mexico trade. The Nuvo Cargo founder identified Flexport as a proxy proving the model worked, then identified a geography where the model had not been applied.

Instruction: When using this recipe without deep domain expertise in the target market, verify that you have a specific structural advantage that would allow entry — connections, regulatory knowledge, proximity to the customer base, or operational skills suited to the business model.

---

### Recipe 6: Talk to people and ask about their problems

Identify a fertile idea space. Go talk to people inside that idea space — both potential customers and founders of existing companies in the space.

This recipe requires more effort than the others and more skill in extracting useful signal from conversations.

Example: AtoB (fuel cards for truck drivers) founders had no trucking industry expertise. They physically drove to truck stops and spoke with truck drivers. They also spoke with founders of existing trucking software companies. Over many conversations they built a map of the problem space and identified where the real opportunities were. They went through multiple candidate ideas before settling on fuel cards.

Instruction: Do not use this recipe without first selecting a fertile idea space. Talking to people without a focus produces too much noise. Pick an industry you believe is under-disrupted and systematically learn it.

---

### Recipe 7: Find broken industries

Large industries where the status quo is obviously inefficient, outdated, or painful — and where that status quo has persisted for years — are candidate spaces.

The persistence of the broken state is a feature, not a bug: it means incumbents are not solving it, which is an opening.

Instruction: Identify the reason the industry is broken. Is it a technical problem, a regulatory problem, a distribution problem, or a trust problem? Your entry strategy must address the specific structural barrier.

## Three Properties That Make Ideas Seem Bad But Are Actually Good

When screening ideas, these three properties trigger false negatives in most evaluators (including experienced investors). Use them to identify ideas that have been left on the table.

### Property 1: Hard to get started

Ideas that require doing something difficult before the product can be built filter out most potential founders. The difficulty creates a barrier to entry that functions as a competitive moat.

Example: Stripe required (a) negotiating a special deal with a bank and (b) building deep knowledge of credit card processing infrastructure before writing a line of product code. Both tasks seemed so daunting that every developer who encountered the pain of integrating payment processing — and there were thousands — declined to start a competing company. The Stripe founders built a $100B+ company by doing the hard thing everyone else avoided.

Instruction: When an idea seems hard to start, do not treat the difficulty as a reason to disqualify it. Treat it as a reason to investigate whether the difficulty creates a durable barrier that will protect the business once built.

Paul Graham's essay on this phenomenon is titled "Schlep Blindness" — the tendency to be blind to unsexy, tedious work that is actually the key to unlocking a major opportunity.

### Property 2: Boring domain

Boring problems attract fewer competitors than exciting ones, which means boring problems tend to have higher success rates for the startups that attempt them.

Example: Gusto builds payroll software. Payroll is not an exciting domain. But many people who processed payroll knew that existing software was painful to use, and no one attempted to fix it for years because the problem seemed boring. Gusto had an uncrowded path to a large market because the domain repelled competitors.

Instruction: The day-to-day experience of running a startup — writing code, fixing bugs, talking to users — is largely the same regardless of how exciting the initial idea sounded. The excitement of a fun idea decays within months. The size and defensibility of the market do not decay. Evaluate the market, not the perceived excitement.

### Property 3: Existing competitors

Most good startup ideas have existing competitors. Founders who avoid competitive markets often find that the reason there are no competitors is that no one wants the product.

The correct evaluation of competition is not "does it exist" but "what have competitors missed that we can see?"

Example: Airbnb entered a market that already had VRBO, Craigslist, and Couchsurfing. The founders identified two specific structural flaws in the existing products: (a) no payment facilitation — hosts and guests had to transact in cash or check, introducing trust friction, and (b) VRBO charged hosts to list, which discouraged supply growth in a market where supply quality is the primary competitive moat. Airbnb solved both.

Instruction: When evaluating a competitive market, require explicit articulation of: what have all existing competitors missed, and why has no one solved it yet? If the answer is compelling, competition is not a liability.

## Worst-Case Analysis: Framework for the Start Decision

When deciding whether to start a startup, do not evaluate the probability of success. Evaluate whether you can tolerate the worst-case outcome.

### The procedure

1. Define the realistic worst case. For most early-stage founders: the startup fails within one to two years without generating meaningful revenue. During that period, you earn little or no salary.

2. Determine whether you can live with that outcome. Be honest, not aspirational. If the answer is "no," constant anxiety will likely sabotage execution.

3. Factor in learning. The learning acquired from founding a startup — even a failed one — has measurable career value. Founders who shut down failed startups frequently join more successful companies in senior roles. Rippling explicitly hires former founders to run product lines. Nick Grandy founded Wonderbar (shut down), joined Airbnb as employee #1, then founded Outschool (valued at $3B+). Startup experience itself is a career asset regardless of outcome.

4. Assess the reversibility of the decision. For most technical founders, leaving a FAANG job to start a company is reversible. If you leave on good terms, you can return. The actual loss is bounded. Loss aversion makes this feel riskier than it is.

## FAANG Trap: Structural Analysis

FAANG retention systems are not passive — they are engineered, iterated, and explicitly designed to keep employees who would otherwise leave.

### The equity gamification mechanism

1. Founders sign with a four-year vesting schedule on their initial equity grant.
2. After six months, they receive a new equity grant — also vesting over four years.
3. Result: after six months, the amount of unvested equity is disproportionately larger than any savings accumulated. The rational calculation says "stay" even if the person wants to leave.
4. This is loss aversion by design: people irrationally overvalue something they already possess relative to something they have not yet received. Unvested equity feels like something being taken away, not merely an unrealized gain.
5. If the company can keep total unvested equity larger than personal savings by 2-3x, the person will not leave regardless of subjective dissatisfaction.

### The exit conditions

Signs that a FAANG employee should leave:
- They describe their situation in terms of why they cannot leave, not why they want to stay
- They are spending their entire salary (common when surrounded by peers who do the same), so savings never accumulate to break the equity lock
- They are working on speculative projects (moonshots, research labs, peripheral product lines) with a high probability of cancellation — meaning years of work may produce no externally visible output and no startup-relevant learning

### The FAANG exit protocol

If a FAANG employee decides to leave to start a startup:
1. Make the decision before receiving any new equity grant. Fighting the loss aversion head-to-head with no prior plan means the bonus wins.
2. Keep personal burn rate low before exit so that savings accumulate and break the equity lock
3. Have a specific plan: what you are trying to learn, how long you will stay, under what conditions you will leave — decided before entering the retention system, not after
4. Recognize that FAANG skills transfer less than expected: internal tooling, infrastructure, and processes do not exist at a startup, and the quality bar for shipping (high inside FAANG, appropriate externally) must be actively unlearned

## Organic vs. Intentional Idea Origin

At least 70% of top YC companies (by valuation, top 100 cohort) arrived at their startup idea organically — meaning they noticed it in the course of their life and work rather than sitting down to deliberately generate ideas.

Deliberately generated ideas are more likely to be tar pit ideas because the deliberate search process tends to surface ideas that have already been noticed by many other people, which is exactly the condition that produces tar pits.

Implication for preparation: If you are not starting a startup immediately, the highest-leverage activity is creating the conditions for organic ideas to emerge:

1. Work in a domain where you will develop deep expertise (working at a startup is better than FAANG for this purpose, because startup experience builds generalizable founder instincts)
2. Talk regularly with smart people about specific problems — not "interesting technologies" but specific operational problems in specific domains
3. Build side projects, however small — the act of turning an idea into a real thing trains the pattern-recognition needed to identify actual startup ideas

Implication for evaluation: An idea that came to you organically — from direct experience, observation of a specific failure in an existing product, or a problem you personally could not solve — is a priori more likely to be good than an idea generated in a deliberate brainstorming session. Weight the origin in your evaluation.

## Ranking Multiple Ideas

When a team has multiple candidate ideas and needs to select one, apply this ranking procedure:

1. Eliminate any idea that fails on Founder-Market Fit (Question 1). Do not rank these.
2. Eliminate any idea that appears to be a tar pit without a specific structural resolution. Do not rank these.
3. For remaining ideas, score on:
   - Market size and growth rate (Question 2)
   - Problem acuteness (Question 3)
   - Competitive landscape and insight quality (Question 4)
   - Idea space fertility (Question 10)
4. Among ideas with comparable scores, prefer ideas that came organically over those that were generated deliberately.
5. Among ideas that score similarly on all criteria, prefer the idea with the most specific and concrete articulation of the problem. Vague problem statements indicate CISP risk.

When no single idea clearly dominates, pick the idea space with the highest historical success rate and treat the specific initial idea as a starting point rather than a commitment. The Fivetran pattern — staying in a fertile idea space while iterating on the specific product — is a reliable recovery from an initial idea that does not work.
