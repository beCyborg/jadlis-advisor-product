# Market Positioning Styles

## Context

Use this reference at Stage 5 of the positioning pipeline, after completing:
- Stage 1: Best-fit customers identified
- Stage 2: Competitive alternatives mapped
- Stage 3: Unique attributes and value themes defined
- Stage 4: Target segment selected

Stage 5 determines HOW to position in the market — which competitive game to play. The three styles are mutually exclusive at any given time but are not permanent. Choose one style, execute it fully, and revisit when the competitive landscape shifts.

---

## Style 1: Head to Head — Win an Existing Market

### What It Means

Compete directly to be the leader of a well-defined, existing market category. Accept the current market definition and the purchase criteria buyers already use. You are not redefining the game — you are claiming to win it.

### When to Use

Apply Head to Head when one of the following is true:

**You are already the market leader.** Defend your position by reinforcing existing buying criteria and demonstrating continued superiority. Do not concede any criterion to challengers.

**The market exists and is understood by buyers, but no clear leader has emerged yet.** Move fast. If buyers know the category and evaluate solutions within it, but no vendor owns the leadership position, claim it before a competitor does. The window closes quickly.

**You are a large, well-resourced company and market conditions have shifted.** New technology, regulatory changes, or economic disruption can unseat an incumbent. If you have the resources and a clear advantage enabled by the shift, pursue leadership directly.

Do not apply Head to Head if you are a startup going against an established, dominant leader. The resource asymmetry is nearly always fatal.

### Conditions for Success

All of the following must hold:

- The market category is well-defined — buyers know what the category is, what it does, and why they need it. If buyers cannot name the category, this style is not available.
- Clear purchase criteria exist. Buyers know what "good" looks like and how to evaluate vendors.
- You can credibly claim to be best at the established criteria, or you are pursuing a leaderless market where no one yet credibly makes that claim.
- If pursuing a leaderless market: you have the funding or revenue to grow fast enough to establish leadership before competitors arrive.

### The Work

**If you are the market leader:**
- Continuously reinforce current buying criteria — make sure your strengths remain the criteria buyers use.
- Produce benchmark data, analyst reports, and third-party validation that demonstrate continued superiority.
- Monitor challengers for attempts to shift the criteria. When a competitor claims your criteria are outdated, respond aggressively with proof.
- Defend against repositioning attacks by highlighting risks of choosing an unproven alternative.

**If you are challenging for leadership in a leaderless market:**
- Establish your claim clearly and early — be the first vendor to publicly stake out the leadership position.
- Accumulate proof: customer case studies, performance benchmarks, market share data.
- Do not compete on price to gain share — compete on demonstrable superiority at the established criteria.
- Invest in brand visibility so that buyers associate the category with your name.

**If you are a large company challenging an incumbent:**
- Identify which established criteria the incumbent now fails to meet due to the market shift.
- Build the case that the shift renders old purchase criteria insufficient — introduce new criteria where you win.
- Use resources to accelerate adoption: sales coverage, marketing spend, partnership channels.

### Risks

- **Trying to out-cola Coke.** Established leaders have compounding advantages — installed base, brand recognition, analyst relationships, partner ecosystems. Competing on their terms with fewer resources rarely works.
- **Accepting criteria you cannot win.** Head to Head requires accepting the existing market definition. If your strengths do not map to existing criteria, you will lose evaluations.
- **Leaderless markets close fast.** Once a competitor achieves leadership, the window for Head to Head closes. If you move too slowly, you will need to switch to Big Fish, Small Pond.
- **Capital requirements.** Establishing market leadership requires sustained investment in marketing, sales, and product. Bootstrapped companies frequently discover they cannot maintain the pace required.

### Example

A company with a patented query algorithm positioned itself as a "database." Every evaluation put it against Oracle. Oracle was the undisputed market leader — prospects compared the two and chose Oracle. The company repositioned as a "data warehouse." The market existed and buyers understood it, but no clear leader had claimed it. Prospects stopped rejecting the product and started buying it. Average deal size increased because data warehouses commanded a premium over commodity databases.

## Style 2: Big Fish, Small Pond — Win a Subsegment

### What It Means

Carve off a definable piece of an existing market where your product has a decisive advantage over general-purpose solutions. You are not competing for the whole market — you are dominating a specific slice of it. The market leader is not your direct competitor within the subsegment because the subsegment's needs are distinct enough that the leader's general-purpose solution is a poor fit.

This is the default starting style for most startups.

### When to Use

Apply Big Fish, Small Pond when all of the following are true:

- A market exists with a clear, dominant leader you cannot credibly beat directly.
- A definable subsegment within that market has distinct, important, unmet needs that general-purpose solutions do not address well.
- The subsegment is identifiable — you can build a prospect list, attend their conferences, read their publications.
- The subsegment is large enough to meet your near-term revenue goals.
- Your differentiator specifically and demonstrably serves this subsegment's unique needs better than the market leader does.

Do not apply Big Fish, Small Pond to a subsegment whose needs are only marginally different from the broader market. The difference must be real, important to buyers, and hard for the leader to replicate without damaging their broader positioning.

### Conditions for Success

- **The parent category is well-defined.** Subsegmenting a confusing or undefined category creates more confusion. Buyers must already understand the category before they can understand why they need a specialist version.
- **The subsegment's needs are clearly different from the broader market.** The needs must be specific enough that a general-purpose solution leaves a visible gap. Buyers in the subsegment must recognize the gap without being told to look for it.
- **The need must be important enough to override the safe choice.** Buyers default to the market leader because it feels safer. Your subsegment must have a need important enough that buying the general-purpose leader is a meaningful risk or cost.
- **You can prove you meet both the subsegment's special needs AND the basic category criteria.** Buyers will not sacrifice baseline requirements for niche functionality. You must win on the specialist dimension while matching the leader on the table-stakes dimensions.
- **Your advantage is hard for the leader to copy.** If the leader can easily add your differentiating feature without disrupting their product or positioning, your window is short. Ideal differentiators require the leader to make trade-offs that hurt their broader market position.

### The Work

**Educate the subsegment about why general-purpose solutions fail them.**
Do not attack the market leader directly. Show the subsegment what happens when they use a tool not built for them: wasted configuration time, missing features, workarounds, data gaps. Quantify the cost. Use their terminology, their workflow, their data in all materials.

**Provide proof specific to the subsegment.**
General case studies do not close deals with specialists. Produce evidence from customers inside the subsegment — their metrics, their workflow outcomes, their comparison against the general-purpose alternative. A case study from a company outside the subsegment is nearly worthless to a subsegment buyer.

**Demonstrate table-stakes category competence.**
Subsegment buyers must trust that you solve their specialist need AND that you are a full solution, not a niche add-on. Build credibility on basic category requirements. Do not let competitors position you as a one-trick tool.

**Build the subsegment community.**
Word-of-mouth travels fast within tight professional communities. One reference customer who is respected in the subsegment is worth more than ten generic case studies. Invest in the community: attend their events, publish in their channels, speak their language.

**Set a timeline for subsegment dominance.**
Define what "winning" this subsegment looks like: market share percentage, revenue from this segment, reference customers. Once you achieve dominance, evaluate whether to expand to adjacent subsegments or move toward Head to Head against the broader market.

### Risks

- **Subsegment too small.** Validate market size before committing. A subsegment must be large enough to sustain the business at target scale.
- **Market leader responds.** If your subsegment grows large enough, the leader may turn attention your way — through product updates, acquisitions, or pricing pressure. Build reference customers and switching costs before this happens.
- **Failing to prove table stakes.** Subsegment buyers will not trade the market leader's full feature set for your specialist capability alone. You must clear the baseline bar.
- **Staying too narrow too long.** Big Fish, Small Pond is often a starting position, not a permanent destination. If you dominate the subsegment and stop expanding, you cap your growth potential.

### Advantages

- Smaller segments are easier and cheaper to reach than broad markets.
- Word-of-mouth accelerates within tight communities — each customer is adjacent to many prospects.
- Success with one customer is directly applicable to the next, because their problems are similar.
- Once dominant, switching costs and reference networks create a durable moat within the segment.
- Starting narrow does not mean staying narrow. Establish dominance in one subsegment, then expand adjacently using the same playbook.

### Example

A small CRM vendor competed against Siebel, which had $2B in revenue and clear market leadership. Prospects called the small vendor "a cheaper, crappier Siebel." The vendor had one feature that most customers did not value: relationship modeling that tracked complex networks of professional relationships. One group did value it deeply — investment bankers, whose entire business ran on relationship intelligence. The vendor repositioned as "CRM for investment banks." They rewrote all marketing in banking terminology, rebuilt demos with banking data, and focused sales entirely on that subsegment. Revenue grew from $2M to $70M in 18 months. The company was acquired for $1.7B.

## Style 3: Create a New Game — Win a Market You Create

### What It Means

Define an entirely new market category. You set the category boundaries, the purchase criteria, and the definition of the problem the category solves. Because you design the category around your strengths, you position yourself as the obvious leader before competition arrives. You are not capturing existing demand — you are sparking demand that does not yet exist.

This is the most difficult style. Use it only when the conditions genuinely require it.

### When to Use

Apply Create a New Game only when ALL of the following are true:

- You have evaluated every plausible existing category and confirmed that none captures your product's primary value without misrepresenting it or burying your differentiators.
- A massive external change has occurred — new technology, new regulations, new economic conditions, or a fundamental shift in buyer behavior — that makes a new category both possible and timely.
- Your product is demonstrably and inarguably new and different. Not "we believe we're different" — the difference must be observable and provable to a skeptical buyer.
- You have a strong, specific answer to "Why now?" — why this category could not have existed 3 years ago and why the window exists today.
- You have resources (capital and time) to invest in sustained market education over a multi-year period.

Do not create a new category because you cannot find a good fit in existing ones. That is insufficient justification. The cost of category creation is high and most attempts fail. Exhaust the other two styles before committing to this one.

### Conditions for Success

- **The "Why now?" story is airtight.** Investors, analysts, and buyers must immediately understand why the category makes sense at this moment. If the answer requires extensive explanation, reconsider whether the market is ready.
- **The product cannot be credibly placed in an existing category.** If an established vendor can say "that's just a feature of our product," your category creation effort is vulnerable. The product must be sufficiently novel that the claim is untenable.
- **You can defend against incumbents claiming your category is a feature.** Large companies will attempt to dismiss new categories by calling them features. Prepare evidence that the category is too complex, too distinct, or too large to be a feature.
- **You have patient capital.** Category creation takes longer and costs more than winning an existing market. Without investors or revenue that can sustain a long-horizon strategy, you will run out of resources before the market materializes.

### The Work

**Sell the problem before the solution.**
Buyers who do not recognize a problem will not purchase a solution to it. Define the problem clearly — what is happening, what it costs, why existing solutions are inadequate. Make the cost of inaction visible and quantifiable. Only after establishing the problem introduce your product as the solution.

**Define purchase criteria that map to your strengths.**
Since no criteria exist yet, you establish them. Define what a good solution in this category looks like. The criteria must be genuinely relevant to buyers — self-serving criteria that only your product meets will be dismissed. But within the space of genuinely relevant criteria, weight the ones where you are strongest.

**Establish yourself as the category leader before followers arrive.**
Category creation attracts imitators. The first mover has a window to build customer relationships, analyst recognition, and brand association with the category name. Use this window aggressively. A category you created but do not lead is the worst outcome — you educated the market and a competitor captured it.

**Invest continuously in market education.**
Publish original research. Host events. Develop analyst relationships. Build a community of practitioners. Write the definitive content on the category's core concepts. The category leader must also be the category educator — buyers learn the concept from you, and that association becomes a durable advantage.

**Name the category precisely.**
The category name must communicate the problem or outcome clearly without being so narrow that it limits growth or so broad that it sounds like an existing category. Test the name with target buyers. Avoid jargon unless your target audience already uses it.

### Risks

- **Market education cost.** Creating demand from scratch requires sustained investment. Every dollar spent educating buyers is a dollar not spent on product development or direct sales.
- **Timing risk.** If the market is not ready — if the underlying shift has not yet affected buyers' priorities — you burn resources educating people who will not buy yet. A fast-follower who waits for your market education to land can then take leadership.
- **Fast-follower risk.** Large incumbents observe new categories and respond. They may acquire a competitor, launch a competing product, or use their existing relationships to claim leadership in your category. Build customer lock-in and analyst recognition before they move.
- **Investors and analysts may not understand it.** Explaining a category that does not exist yet to investors, analysts, and press is difficult and time-consuming. Expect longer fundraising cycles and more education overhead.

### Example

A company built a tool for sales reps to track prospect behavior via email and website visits. The target audience was "demand generators" — professionals focused on generating sales-qualified leads, not yet a widely recognized job function. The company named the category "demand generation automation" and marketed to that niche. Investors and analysts found the positioning confusing — the target audience seemed too small. The company maintained its position. As demand generation became a recognized function at larger companies, the market expanded. The company later shifted the category label to "marketing automation" as the broader term gained currency. Revenue grew from $12M to $96M over six years. The company went public and was acquired by Oracle for $870M.

## Decision Tree

Work through these questions in order. Stop at the first style that fits.

**Step 1.** Does a well-defined market category exist where your product's primary value is clearly communicated?
- No → Evaluate Create a New Game (Style 3). Confirm all conditions before committing.
- Yes → Proceed to Step 2.

**Step 2.** Is there a clear, dominant leader in this market — a vendor most buyers default to and that sets the purchase criteria?
- No → Head to Head (Style 1). Establish yourself as leader before one emerges.
- Yes → Proceed to Step 3.

**Step 3.** Can you credibly claim to be better than the leader at their established purchase criteria, and do you have the resources to prove it at scale?
- Yes → Head to Head (Style 1). Build the evidence and execute.
- No → Proceed to Step 4.

**Step 4.** Does a definable subsegment exist within this market with distinct, important, unmet needs that your product specifically addresses?
- Yes → Big Fish, Small Pond (Style 2). Define the subsegment, validate its size, and execute.
- No → Re-evaluate. Either identify a different subsegment, identify a different parent market, or revisit Create a New Game.

**Default for startups:** Big Fish, Small Pond. Most startups cannot win entire markets and cannot sustain the cost of creating new ones. Dominating a subsegment is the fastest path to initial traction and the clearest foundation for future expansion.

---

## Transitioning Between Styles

Styles are not permanent. Competitive landscapes shift and positioning must evolve with them.

**Big Fish, Small Pond → Head to Head.** Dominate the subsegment. Use that position, revenue, and reference customers to expand into adjacent subsegments. Once you have enough market coverage and proof of broad capability, compete for overall category leadership.

**Create a New Game → Head to Head.** After the category matures and followers arrive, shift from market education to market defense. Use the leadership position established during category creation to win Head to Head against followers who lack your depth of category knowledge and customer relationships.

**Head to Head → Big Fish, Small Pond.** If a clear leader emerges that you cannot displace, and a subsegment exists with distinct needs, exit the broader head-to-head competition. Avoid the sunk cost of continuing to lose a game you cannot win. Pivot before resources are exhausted.

Review positioning style every six months or when any of the following occur: a major competitor enters or exits the market, a significant technology or regulatory shift changes buyer priorities, your win/loss rates in key segments change materially, or a new subsegment with distinct needs becomes identifiable.
