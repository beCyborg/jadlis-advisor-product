# Audience, Authority & Email-First — Authority (Nathan Barry)

Everything here is drawn from the text of *Authority* (Nathan Barry, 2013). Numbers, names, and cases are the author's own unless marked otherwise.

---

## [AUTH:TE] Teach Everything You Know

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 1-2.

**Core Principle:** The single biggest lever. "Teach everything you know." The people who are well known in an industry are usually **not** the most talented — they are the ones who teach. "You can continue to create cutting-edge work... but until you start teaching and sharing, your reach and influence will be limited."

### [AUTH:TE.1] Those who teach get known (and paid)
- **Marco Polo** is remembered not because he explored first (his father and uncle went before him) but because **he wrote about it**.
- **Chris Coyier** launched CSS-Tricks in 2007 and taught constantly. Barry was at the same skill level and taught nothing. Years later Chris's Kickstarter (goal $3,500) raised **$89,697** "with relative ease, all because he had built up an audience who loved his work." Barry: "He and I started at the same point... The difference was that he taught and shared, whereas I kept what I was learning to myself."
- **37signals** built a blog of **100,000+ readers** by writing "hundreds of blog posts," then launched products to an eager audience "without spending anything on advertising."

### [AUTH:TE.2] You don't have to be an expert to teach
"Chances are someone will always know less about it than you do." Teach what you just learned; be transparent about your level and people follow along (and help when you get stuck). "The best way to make sure you understand a concept is to teach it."

### [AUTH:TE.3] Give away the secrets
"Don't be worried about giving away too much information or too many secrets." Barry's free content and Pat Flynn's near-identical free/paid split both *helped* sales. Withholding builds no audience; teaching does. The paid version wins on packaging, format, and depth (see `book-course-ladder.md` `[AUTH:PR.4]`).

### [AUTH:TE.4] Sell your by-product — teach instead of ship
A reframe for makers who can't finish products. **Mike Rundle** could design *and* build iOS apps but had "started and stopped 15-20 different apps." His epiphany, echoing Jason Fried's "sell your by-product": instead of finishing and marketing an app, **teach others how to design and build apps.** He'd design a simple 1-2 screen app (which his passion could carry to completion) and turn the process into a tutorial. Result: Design Then Code made **~$53,000** with little upkeep, a free intro tutorial read by **400,000+ people**, conference invitations, and repeated (declined) offers to author traditional books — "why wouldn't I just self-publish?!" The teaching by-product outperformed the products themselves.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: always, at every rung. This is the engine under the whole book.
- The give-first move precedes every ask.

## [AUTH:EX] Establish Expertise

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 1.

**Core Principle:** "You don't have to be an expert to teach... but if you want to make a living from your teaching, you should be *perceived* as an expert." That's far easier than being one. "Just the fact that you are writing a book gets you half way there."

### [AUTH:EX.1] The book itself is the credential
"Name your book, set up a landing page, and then start calling yourself the author of 'xxxx.'" Working on *The App Design Handbook* "transformed the perception of my blog from 'an unknown designer writing about random topics' to 'the author of a book on designing iOS apps.'" A few in-depth posts plus a couple of client quotes are enough credibility to start.

### [AUTH:EX.2] Tim Ferriss's 5-step expertise method (Barry's adaptation)
1. **Join trade organizations with official-sounding names** — e.g., National Association of Photoshop Professionals ($100/yr) as a credibility indicator.
2. **Read and summarize the top three best-selling books** on your topic; rewrite the concepts from your own perspective as blog posts. "It only takes a few really solid blog posts for readers to perceive you as an expert."
3. **Write for other sites and magazines** — guest posts are surprisingly easy to land "so long as you have something to say"; larger outlets (Inc, Forbes) take more work but the résumé line is worth it.
4. **Give a talk at a local university or a large company's local office** — Barry spoke at Boise Code Camp (held at Boise State) and can say he "spoke at the university"; speaking at a company's local dev group = "spoke at Microsoft."
5. **Piggyback on other people for press** — Barry is quoted in TechCrunch, Mashable, The Next Web etc. as a **Gumroad** user; when reporters cover Gumroad they ask the team for a user quote, and the team refers them to him.

"None of this is to trick or mislead people, just to demonstrate the necessary level of expertise."

### [AUTH:EX.3] Finding the topic & market
- **What do people ask you for help with?** "A good place to start is by writing about an area in which other people perceive you to be an expert." Setting up web pages? Running a business? Fixing an appliance? Removing viruses? "If people are asking you for help in a particular area, chances are they think you know something about it. That also means there is demand."
- **You don't need to be *the* expert** — teach what you're learning, be transparent about your level, and people follow along (and help when you're stuck).
- **Two market tests:** (1) does it teach a skill people use to make money? (2) do those people gather and communicate online (forums, blogs)? If they don't gather online, "this method probably isn't for you."
- **Competition is good.** "When friends... say 'And there aren't any competitors!' — Yikes." No competitors may mean no market. Barry wasn't first to write on app design (*Tapworthy* existed) and it didn't hurt sales. "Embrace competition."
- **Keyword research:** check whether readers already search for the topic (Google's keyword tool) before committing months to writing.

### [AUTH:EX.4] The accidental expert (Pat Flynn)
The canonical "teach → expertise → income" story. **Pat**, a Southern California architect, studied for the difficult **LEED** green-building exam and kept his study notes in WordPress so he could reach them from any computer — which also made them **public**. He passed, moved on, then got laid off in the downturn. Curious about emails referencing his notes, he added Google Analytics and found **four to five thousand people visiting his site every day** — search engines had indexed his notes and strangers were using them to pass the exam. "Pat had become an expert without even trying."

Monetization path (each rung earned more):
1. **Google text ads** — didn't pay much.
2. **Banner ads sold directly** to related companies — worked, but small.
3. **Packaged the notes as a paid PDF study guide** — *"not new content, just the same content... organized differently and distributed as a PDF."* First month (Oct 2008): **$7,905.88** — more than his old salaried job. Since launch: **roughly half a million dollars in sales.**

Lesson: "The difference between Pat Flynn and the thousands of other people who have taken the LEED exam is that Pat made his study notes public." The paid product being ~95% identical to the free notes did not hurt — it helped.

## [AUTH:AU] Email List First (Email > Social for Selling)

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 2.

**Core Principle:** "Email marketing has been, by far, the most successful method I have used to sell books." Social media is "useless for selling" — good for relationships, not revenue.

### [AUTH:AU.1] The proof that social doesn't sell
Barry's $0.99 app **Commit** was promoted by a blogger with **~80,000 followers** across Twitter + Facebook. The result: **fewer than 20 copies sold.** Later mentions on popular blogs generated "nearly $500 in revenue in a single day." "Getting someone with a popular Twitter account to notice your product is clearly not the path to riches."

### [AUTH:AU.2] Four reasons email wins
1. **Push content** — email new articles instead of waiting for readers to return.
2. **Easier signup** — an incentive (free ebook/video) makes email opt-in far easier than an RSS or social follow. His free **Productivity Manifesto** added **500 subscribers in a few days**.
3. **Higher engagement quality** — email sits next to messages from friends, clients, family; "email is about getting work done," social is where people waste time.
4. **Ownership** — "Do you know anyone who built up a huge following on Friendster or MySpace? What's that worth now?" On a platform you're at their mercy; the email list you own and can move.

### [AUTH:AU.3] The list is the business asset
"The core of my business is being able to contact my readers and customers... All that potential lies in an email list of **6,500 people**" representing "over a hundred thousand dollars in potential future revenue." "There is no way I am trusting my most valuable business asset to Twitter or Facebook."

### [AUTH:AU.4] Email done right — the Brennan Dunn arc
Every email must "provide more value to your reader than for yourself." **Brennan Dunn** is the worked example of email-as-a-flywheel:
- Started with only **a few hundred email addresses** from Planscope (his freelancer SaaS) trials — "he didn't have to start from scratch."
- Wrote an **educational email that also promoted** his new book *Double Your Freelancing Rate* — "a great way to kickstart sales."
- Built the weekly **Freelancers Weekly** list; each issue was useful by itself while promoting a product. It grew as he kept giving value.
- Readers asked how to scale freelancing into agencies, so he taught a **two-day masterclass, $1,000/ticket, 25 seats** — one announcement email to his 1,000+ list **sold 15 seats instantly = $15,000.**
- He sold out that workshop and the next; **over $90,000 in six months** of running it. His later book *The Blueprint* made **over $20,000, almost exclusively from the list.**

"If you focus on email you never have to start from scratch with a new product."

### [AUTH:AU.5] Don't let the list die
The #1 way email marketing fails: a list goes **stagnant** between launches. "Your readers will forget who you are, then be surprised to hear from you months later" — and unsubscribe. "Even a good list will lose its value over time if neglected." Keep sending valuable content every week or few weeks.

### [AUTH:AU.6] What actually drove sales (author-interview evidence)
From the book's author interviews — the marketing channels that mattered:
- **Sacha Greif:** "The only two things that had a serious impact on revenue were posting the book to **Hacker News**, and doing **deals** with AppSumo/Dealotto/MightyDeals... combined they probably account for **90% of revenue**."
- **Jarrod Drysdale:** landing page + HN posts to grow a newsletter, then "the **newsletter became much, much more important. It drives a lot of sales.**" Plus long personal emails to customers and prospects to build loyalty.
- **Brandon Savage:** direct marketing + an email list that **converted at ~25%**; "residual sales are where it's really at" (more from residual than his $3,000 launch day); a weekend **20%-off sale generated $1,400.**

**Metrics:** list size, growth rate, opt-in conversion, open/click rate, list→sale conversion (~25% is a strong benchmark), residual vs launch-day revenue.

## [AUTH:LP] Landing Page & Opt-in

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 2.

**Core Principle:** Before spending months writing, put up a landing page to test demand and start collecting emails. Don't over-build it — "it will change."

### [AUTH:LP.1] The three required elements
1. **A headline** that speaks to the reader's pain. Barry's for *Designing Web Applications*: **"Are you scratching your head wondering why people sign up for your application, but never come back?"** — a known pain point for software companies.
2. **An image + short description** — include an image of a physical book "even though I am selling eBooks" so visitors instantly grasp "this page is about a book."
3. **An opt-in form** — email only (or name + email); "don't ask for anything more." Optionally sweeten it with a free chapter, related article, or launch discount to push them over the edge.

### [AUTH:LP.2] One domain, personal brand
Sell and blog on a **single personal domain** (Barry uses `nathanbarry.com`, books at `/app-design-handbook` and `/webapps`). Personal branding "can always be rolled over to new projects" — a book-specific domain (AppDesignHandbook.com) doesn't carry to the next product.

### [AUTH:LP.3] Drive traffic — take inventory & ask
"Take inventory" of every way you can reach interested people, then share the landing page on all of them:
- An existing blog (the best starting point)
- Friendships with other bloggers in your target market
- Existing email lists
- Facebook friends, Twitter followers
- Forums and online communities you belong to
- Bloggers you're a fan of and could contact
- Offline: friends, club members — "there are people who don't spend their lives online"

Then, once you've exhausted your own channels, **ask influential friends** — softly. Barry's method: give them a content sample "highly relevant to their interests," then at the end ask them to share it *if* they found it interesting, and add "an easy out" that signals you don't expect it. One author with high-caliber friends (whom Barry already followed on Twitter) tried this; **one friend's share crashed the book's server "in a good way."** "Don't be afraid to ask."

## [AUTH:CN] Epic Content / Give Before You Ask

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 2.

**Core Principle:** A landing page shows you exist; **three epic blog posts** prove your expertise and teaching style.

### [AUTH:CN.1] Three epic cornerstone posts
A landing page is quick and thin; three deep posts prove expertise and teaching style. For each:
- **Most valuable content you have to teach** — "don't be worried about giving away too much information or too many secrets." These get people interested early, so don't hold back.
- **1,000-5,000 words each**, original long-form — "not list posts or aggregated content from other sites. Really teach." "A few detailed articles or tutorials will get you most of the way [to perceived expert]."
- **Show your teaching style** — buyers "take a class from" you; an expert who can't teach can't help them. Strong point → you shine; weak point → this is where you practice.
- **Don't fear giving away the best material** — these posts exist to get people interested in you and the book early; holding back defeats the purpose.
- **Reuse existing hits** — if you already blog, use your posts on the topic with the most visits, shares, and positive comments.
- **Capture leads at the end** — every relevant post ends with an email opt-in form; keep the page simple ("the more options you give the user, the less likely they will be to take the one you want").

### [AUTH:CN.2] Give value in every ask
The give-first pattern runs through the whole book: sample chapters, free tutorials, the free-content-with-upsell model, and the email course that teaches for days before any pitch (see `launch-marketing.md` `[AUTH:PM.1]`). "You should make each email provide more value to your reader than for yourself."

### [AUTH:CN.3] One idea, many media
"The same information can be taught in several different media" — a blog post, video, or podcast can carry the same core message. Teach across media because "some people learn better by listening... others prefer reading," and some subjects are far faster in one medium (a design step is painful in text, "I could demonstrate it in 30 seconds with a screen recording"). This does double duty: it serves different learners *and* it feeds the higher-perceived-value packages (`book-course-ladder.md` `[AUTH:PK.2]`). Content you cut from the book isn't wasted — repurpose it as guest posts and teaser articles.

## [AUTH:LM] Lead Magnets & Free Chapters

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 2, 4, 7.

**Core Principle:** A free, genuinely useful asset is the fastest way to grow a list and to convert fence-sitters. Give away the secrets, sell the packaging/implementation.

### [AUTH:LM.1] Free short products as list-builders
- **Productivity Manifesto** (free short ebook): **+500 subscribers in a few days**.
- A free flagship tutorial as the top of the funnel: **Mike Rundle's** free *Building iOS Apps From Scratch* was **read by 400,000+ unique people** and fed his paid tutorials; his announce list grew from **2,000 at launch to 11,000**.
- Incentivize opt-ins with "a free chapter of the book, a related article, or... a discount."

### [AUTH:LM.2] The sample chapter (converts buyers)
On the sales page, offer a **sample chapter as a quick free download**. "Your book will be judged on this sample chapter more than anything else." It needn't be one literal chapter — pull your best sections together to show design style, readability, and skill level. It "goes a long way towards building trust."

### [AUTH:LM.3] Free version → upsell
If you give a full free version (web/HTML), it must have an **obvious upsell**: an email course that teaches then sells, or a direct link to the paid packages. "Don't give away your hard work for free without having a way to either make money or gain subscribers."

### [AUTH:LM.4] Turn a marketplace/deal buyer into a subscriber
Even when a customer arrives via a deal site or marketplace, capture the email: a thank-you landing page (MailChimp/ConvertKit) that delivers the download after they enter their address and opt in for future content. This is the whole reason to own distribution rather than rent it (deal mechanics in `launch-marketing.md` `[AUTH:AF.2]`).

**Metrics:** subscribers added per lead magnet, sample-chapter download → purchase rate, free-reader → paid conversion, opt-in checkbox take-rate on deal/marketplace delivery pages.

## The consistency engine (why any of this ships)

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 1, 3.

- **80% of people want to write a book; almost none do.** "What separates the wannabes from the published authors? Actually writing."
- Barry's rule: **1,000 words a day, every day**, tracked as a streak in his own app *Commit* — **292 days in a row** at time of writing. "It doesn't matter whether your daily goal is 100 words or 1,000 words; you just need to be consistent." At 1,000/day, 30 days ≈ 30,000 words ≈ a short book.
- **Writer's block fix:** "When faced with writer's block, lower your standards and keep going." (Sandra Tsing Loh) Seth Godin never gets it "because he writes like he talks — no one gets talker's block."
- **Total writing load** for a launch (Barry's estimate): the book (25,000-30,000 words) + 7-10 guest posts (7,000-10,000) + 3 launch/update emails (~1,500) + 3+ own-site posts (3,000+) ≈ **37,500 words minimum**. "The more you teach, the more people will hear about your work."

Producing the content (Chapter 3 mechanics):
- **Write out of order.** A rough outline (bold chapter headings + bulleted sections), then a section per point in your writing tool (Barry uses Scrivener); each session, "skim down my list until I find an interesting topic" and write there. Order and polish come later — this beats being stuck on one section.
- **Read the competition first** — not to copy, but "to make sure I'm not missing anything." Cite sources; keep learning as you write.
- **Cut ruthlessly, but don't waste cuts** — reused as guest posts and teaser articles.
- **Fill gaps with your readers** — Barry ran an hour-long Twitter Q&A to surface the questions people wanted answered, then made sure the book answered each.
- **Use multiple editors, sequentially** — send to editor 1, apply fixes, then editor 2 (a technical editor catches factual mistakes). "The more eyes on it the better."
