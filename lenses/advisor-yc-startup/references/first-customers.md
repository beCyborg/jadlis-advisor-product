# First Customers: Sales, Launch, and Early Growth

## Do Things That Don't Scale (DTDTS)

"Do Things That Don't Scale" is the foundational principle for getting first customers. It means founders must personally do work that cannot be automated or delegated — specifically sales, onboarding, and user recruitment.

Startups do not take off by themselves. Founders make them take off. Good product alone does not produce customers. Early product is almost never good when first customers see it — it gets good through iterating with customers. This means founders must manually recruit customers before the product is ready to sell itself.

**Why founders must do sales personally, not hire salespeople:**

- Founders who outsource sales before doing it themselves never learn what "good" looks like. They cannot evaluate a sales hire or a sales process they have never executed.
- Every sales conversation is user research. You hear objections, learn how customers describe the problem, and discover gaps between what you built and what they need. A hired rep filters this out.
- If you do not know how to sell, you cannot know if the product is bad or the sales is bad. You need to try selling first to tell the difference.
- Sales requires intimate knowledge of the problem and the product. Founders have this by default. It takes a hire months to acquire it.
- Do not hire a sales team until you can do sales yourself and know what results look like.

**DTDTS tactics drawn from YC companies:**

- Brex founders recruited their first 10 customers directly from the YC batch. Henrique personally onboarded every single one.
- Airbnb founders went door-to-door to recruit hosts.
- DoorDash founders interviewed 200+ small business owners before building anything.
- Amplitude, Stripe, and Front all used outbound email — founders writing individual cold emails — as their first sales motion.
- Manual onboarding: do not ship and expect users to figure it out. Walk each customer through the product yourself. Churn in early products is often caused by skipping this step.

Doing these things at scale is impossible or inefficient. That is the point. At this stage, you need learning from every interaction more than you need efficiency.

## Sales Funnel Arithmetic

The sales funnel has five stages with known drop-off rates. Most founders fail because they do not send enough outreach to reach statistically meaningful numbers at the bottom.

**The funnel with conversion rates:**

| Stage | Action | Conversion | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Outreach emails or LinkedIn messages sent | 100% | 500 |
| 2 | Email opens | 50% | 250 |
| 3 | Responses | ~8% of opens (5% of total sent) | 20 |
| 4 | Demos scheduled and run | 50% of responses | 10 |
| 5 | Customers closed | 20% of demos | 2 |

**Working backwards from a goal:**

To close 10 customers, reverse the funnel:
- Need 50 demos (at 20% close rate)
- Need 100 responses (at 50% demo conversion)
- Need 2000+ opens (at 5% response rate)
- Need 4000+ outreach emails sent (at 50% open rate)

Round numbers: assume 500 outreach emails yield ~2 customers. To get 10 customers, send ~2500 emails. This is the actual pipeline math.

**The 100-email mistake:**

A common failure pattern: founder sends 100 outreach emails, gets 0 customers, concludes "sales doesn't work." The math shows why this is wrong. At standard conversion rates, 100 emails yield fewer than 1 customer. You do not have enough data to conclude sales is broken. The only valid conclusion is that you sent too few emails.

Do not switch to marketing, SEO, or other channels because 100-email outbound produced no customers. You have not run the experiment. Run the actual numbers first.

**What to track:**

Use a CRM or spreadsheet with columns: industry, company name, contact title, name, email, LinkedIn, current stage. Track conversion at each stage. Without this data, you cannot get feedback on what is working. With it, you can identify whether the problem is open rates (email subject lines), response rates (email body), demo-to-close rate (your pitch), or something else.

Tools that track conversion automatically: apollo.io, close.com, pipedrive, hunter.io.

## Cold Email: 6-Element Template

Cold sales emails must be short. Maximum 6–8 sentences. Long emails fail. Academic writing style (long, structured, formal) does not work in sales.

**The six elements:**

**1. Subject line.** Not covered in detail in the source, but implied: direct, not clever. The subject line must make opening the email feel low-risk.

**2. Opening — state who you are.** Say explicitly that you are the founder. Many founders forget this. "I'm [name], founder of [company]." This matters because founders writing directly to potential customers is unusual and gets attention.

**3. Name the problem.** Address the specific problem the customer is having. Do not describe your solution first. Show that you understand their situation. Write directly toward your target customer — the email should feel like it was written for them specifically, not for a generic audience.

**4. Value proposition.** Describe what your product does in plain language. No jargon. No buzzwords. Write like you are explaining to a friend. The reader should understand what you built from this one sentence. If you cannot explain it clearly in one sentence, the email will not work and neither will your pitch.

**5. Social proof.** Show, do not tell. Do not say "we are experts." Instead: "We went through YC W23" or "Previously worked at [known company]" or "We've already signed [recognizable customer]." This gives the reader a reason to respond even if they do not know you.

**6. Call to action.** Every email must end with a clear next step. A link to book a 15-minute call, a reply with two time slots, or a self-serve signup link — depending on your sales model. No ask = no response.

**Optional: website link.** Include a link to your website. The website should have screenshots of the product and bullets describing what it does. No marketing graphics. A short demo video or GIF can be embedded — but it must get to the point in the first 10 seconds.

**Format rule:** Plain text only. No HTML formatting, no bold, no images, no styled headers. Write it as you would write to a friend. Formatting signals mass email. Plain text signals personal outreach.

**Brex example:** Their first beta outreach email opened with "Hey guys, we're opening up our beta for Winter '17 batch friends with 10 spots for beta users." The limited-spots framing created urgency. The email then named the value prop (no personal guarantee required for startup credit cards) and stated the price (free, merchants pay). This email was slightly too long per the lecturer's assessment — but it worked. The principle: when in doubt, cut.

## Charge From Day One

Do not offer your product for free, as a free trial, or as an unpaid pilot. A customer is someone who pays you money. Everyone else is not a customer and you do not have a company.

**Free product problems:**

- Free users do not give real feedback. They have no skin in the game. They will tell you the product is fine when they mean they would never pay for it.
- Free trial does not validate that anyone wants your product. It validates that people will try free things.
- If they will not pay, you do not have product-market fit. This is the signal. Do not avoid the signal.

**Money-back guarantee beats free trial (B2B):**

For B2B sales, do not offer a free trial. Offer instead:
- A money-back guarantee (30 or 60 days)
- Or the ability to opt out of an annual contract and pay monthly instead

Consumer free trials typically require a credit card upfront, which works because people forget and pay anyway. The B2B equivalent is a money-back guarantee. The customer pays, but the risk is removed. This filters out non-serious customers while still reducing the friction to getting started.

**Price discovery:**

Start your price higher than you think is right. Negotiate down if needed. Never start low and try to raise later — it is much harder to raise prices on existing customers than to negotiate a discount from a high opening.

The correct price signal: customers complain about the price but still pay. If no one is complaining, you are undercharging. If they immediately say "yes" without hesitation, you have priced too low.

If a prospect will not pay at all, do not chase them. Move on to the next prospect. You do not have time to convert non-buyers into buyers. Find the early adopters who will pay.

## Launch Strategy

**Core principle:** Launch is not a one-time event. Launch is something you do continuously. Most founders over-prepare a single launch and treat it as the only shot. This is wrong.

Airbnb launched three times before gaining users. Stripe does continuous launching — each product release is a launch, with blog posts, Hacker News engagement, and social distribution. Launch and iterate, launch and iterate.

**Why launch now (not later):**

- The worst case from launching too early: people think the product is ugly, investors hear about it before it is ready, competitors see it, or — most likely — no one cares. All of these outcomes are recoverable. None of them are fatal.
- The worst case from launching too late: six months of building the wrong thing, with no user signal to correct course.
- Even 10 users who really love the product is enough to start. The job then is to expand that number, not to find a thousand users at once.

**Six launch types:**

**1. Silent launch.** Minimum: a domain, a company name, a one-sentence description, and a call to action (email signup or waitlist). Do this before everything else. Every company should have a landing page. Half of Startup School companies that were checked did not. Fix this first.

**2. Friends and family.** Test your one-sentence pitch on people you know. Once you have an MVP, do a friends and family launch immediately. Watch them use the product. Do not stay in this phase long — their feedback is often not representative of real users.

**3. Strangers.** Talk to 200 potential customers before building. DoorDash interviewed 200+ small business owners. Chloe the macaron shop manager showed them the delivery problem only after they were about to leave. The insight came from the conversation, not from a survey. Go find strangers in your target market. This is uncomfortable. Do it anyway.

**4. Online communities.** Plan a launch for every community you are part of. Hacker News Show HN, Reddit, specific Slack groups, Discord communities, forums relevant to your market. Each community has a different tone — read it before posting. Be authentic; do not use marketing language. Examples: Dropbox launched on Hacker News, Robinhood got 10,000 signups on day one and 50,000 in the first week after an HN post.

**5. Pre-order / waitlist.** For hardware or physical products, use Kickstarter or Indiegogo. For software, a waitlist with a visible queue (like Robinhood's) adds social proof. Warning on waitlists: the longer you wait to ship, the harder it is to convert waitlist signups into actual users. Do not sit on a waitlist.

**6. Press.** Do not prioritize press as an early-stage company that has not raised $1M+. Landing press is very difficult. Even when it works, press does not drive sustained growth and will not get you to product-market fit. Build your own community (email list, social following) instead. Stripe and Y Combinator alumni use owned channels to launch repeatedly.

## The Startup Curve

The startup curve describes the typical trajectory of an early-stage company after launch. Every stage is predictable and has a correct response.

**Stages:**

1. **Launch spike.** Initial energy from the launch event — Product Hunt, Hacker News, online communities. New users, attention, momentum.

2. **Trough of Sorrow.** Launch energy fades. Early adopters who love new things have moved on. If there is no strong retention, growth stops. Many companies die here. Founders give up or move too slowly to test new things.

3. **Crash of Ineptitude.** Companies that are moving but not fast enough also die here. They test new things but do not iterate fast enough or listen closely enough to users.

4. **Wiggles of False Hope.** Companies that survive the trough start seeing small signals of growth. These feel like breakthroughs but are often temporary. Do not scale based on a wiggle.

5. **Promised Land (Product-Market Fit).** Sustainable growth. Retention is high enough that the curve bends upward and stays there.

**What to do during the Trough of Sorrow:**

- Do not conclude that the business does not work.
- Do not switch to a different growth channel hoping it will solve the problem.
- Do not stop talking to users.
- Iterate the product based on what you hear.
- Keep sending outbound emails. Keep running demos. Keep doing manual onboarding.
- The founders who get through the trough are the ones who stay the course and move fast. The trough is normal. It is not a signal to quit.

## Working Backwards From Goals

Set a weekly customer goal. Then use the funnel math to calculate what inputs are required.

**Process:**

1. Decide how many paying customers you need this week (or this month).
2. Apply conversion rates backwards through the funnel.
3. Calculate the number of outreach emails you need to send.
4. If that number is achievable, go execute. If not, adjust: the channel, the target audience, or the conversion rate at a specific stage (by improving the email or the demo).

**Never adjust your ambition downward because the math feels hard.** Adjust the channel or the message instead.

**Common failure mode:** Founders set a goal of 5 customers, send 50 emails, get 0 customers, and conclude the channel does not work. At standard conversion rates, 50 emails yield 0.2 customers in expectation. The experiment was not run. Rerun it with the correct sample size.

**Tracking template (minimum columns):**

- Company name
- Contact name and title
- Email / LinkedIn
- Outreach date
- Open (Y/N)
- Response (Y/N)
- Demo scheduled (Y/N)
- Demo run (Y/N)
- Closed (Y/N)
- Notes

Review these numbers weekly. If open rates are low, fix the subject line. If response rates are low, fix the email body. If demo-to-close is low, fix the pitch or the qualification criteria (you may be demoing the wrong people).
