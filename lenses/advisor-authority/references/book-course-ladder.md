# The Info-Product Ladder, Pricing & Packaging — Authority (Nathan Barry)

Everything here is drawn from the text of *Authority* (Nathan Barry, 2013). Numbers, names, and cases are the author's own unless marked otherwise.

---

## [AUTH:LD] The Info-Product Ladder

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 1, 4, 9.

**Core Principle:** A single skill becomes a business by climbing rungs, each one built on the audience the previous rung created. The book is not the destination — it is the first rung. "Your first book is just the beginning."

**The rungs (as the book demonstrates them):**
1. **Teach in public** → get known and build a list (Ch. 1-2).
2. **Self-published book / guide** → first paid product (Ch. 3-8).
3. **Packages / expanded editions** → same content, higher-value tiers (Ch. 4).
4. **Courses, workshops, email courses** → deeper teaching at higher prices (Ch. 9).
5. **SaaS / recurring product** → built on the audience (Barry → ConvertKit).

### [AUTH:LD.1] The author is the case study
Nathan Barry taught web/app design in public, released **two eBooks within a year and made over six figures in profit** from them. In "the last year" he **sold over $150,000 in eBooks**. His core business asset became an **email list of 6,500 people** representing "over a hundred thousand dollars in potential future revenue." He then built **ConvertKit**, his email-marketing SaaS — the next rung above books and courses. The ladder is literal: teach → list → books → email courses → software.

### [AUTH:LD.2] Book → workshop → SaaS in his peers
- **Brennan Dunn**: ran Planscope (SaaS for freelancers) → wrote *Double Your Freelancing Rate* ($49) → built the *Freelancers Weekly* list → sold a $1,000 masterclass → cross-sells book ↔ SaaS. He made "nearly $90,000 from two books... in the last year" and "an additional $95,000 from hosting workshops."
- **Brandon Savage**: *Mastering Object-Oriented PHP* made ~$10,000 in four months → **first workshop made an additional $9,000**.
- **Mike Rundle**: free *Building iOS Apps From Scratch* tutorial (read by **400,000+ unique people**) as the base rung → paid Design Then Code tutorials (~$53,000 total) as the rung above it.

### [AUTH:LD.3] Your goal chooses the rung and the price
"Why are you writing this book?" — money, expertise/positioning, or more consulting clients — "heavily affects our distribution methods and pricing." A money goal → price high, own the list, self-publish. A reach/positioning goal → a lower price or a free version, wider distribution. Decide the goal *before* pricing and packaging, because the same product is built differently for each. "It's not just about the money."

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: the user has (or is building) an audience and one product, and asks "what's next?"
- Route them UP one rung, not sideways. List → book. Book → email course + packages. Course → workshop/SaaS.
- AVOID WHEN: they have no audience yet — send them to `[AUTH:AU]` first; a product with no list launches to no one.

## [AUTH:PR] Value-Based Pricing

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 4 ("Pricing & Packaging").

**Core Principle:** Price on **value delivered**, never on market comparison. The "hardback is $25, so my ebook should be $3" logic "is directly responsible for the poor, starving author." If the skill helps buyers make money, the ROI justifies a premium.

The trap is the "race-to-the-bottom" reasoning chain Barry lays out and rejects: hardback $25 → Amazon $15 → self-published, knock off a few → $12 → but it's *just* an ebook, no per-copy cost → $6-7 → but some $2-3 ebooks sell millions → $3. "This line of reasoning is directly responsible for the poor, starving author." It only works with massive distribution, which a small blog doesn't have.

### [AUTH:PR.1] The skill-that-makes-money test
"If you know a skill that other people use to make money, you can make a living by teaching that skill." Barry prices his design books at **$39** because software professionals use them to build better products that earn more — "$39 is inexpensive to my target market." Brennan Dunn's *Double Your Freelancing Rate* at **$49** "will pay for itself if the reader can increase their rate — even slightly — with just one client."

Handling the "Who would pay $39 for an eBook?!" objection: it's not about the format, it's about value delivered. "Deliver tons of value and charge based on that." A book that teaches a valuable skill is worth many times its price to a buyer who uses it to earn.

### [AUTH:PR.2] Small audience → charge more
The doubling rule from Barry's own testing: **"when I double the price of a product — within a certain range — I lose 25% of the sales."**

Worked example (*The App Design Handbook*, base book only):
- Real: **724 copies × $39 = $28,236** over ~9 months.
- Hypothetical at $9: he estimates ~2× units → ~1,450 copies × $9 = **$13,050**. "Ouch. That's a massive loss in revenue."

"When you have a small audience you need to charge a higher price in order to make a living."

### [AUTH:PR.3] Not everything must be priced high — price to the goal
Two design ebooks launched the same day, March 2012:
- **Sacha Greif — *Step-by-step UI Design*** (42-page single-app case study): **$3** ebook / $6 deluxe. Sold **1,476 copies in the first 48 hours**. Later raised to $6 / $12.
- **Jarrod Drysdale — *Bootstrapping Design*** (full-length): **$39**. Sold **242 copies in the first 48 hours**.

Both launches succeeded. Jarrod made more money; Sacha got a far larger paying audience. **Price to your goal:** money → price high; reach/positioning/audience → a lower price (or a free version) can make sense. A small product can carry a small price.

### [AUTH:PR.4] Free + paid can coexist
*Getting Real* (37signals) and *Rails Tutorial* (Michael Hartl) are free online yet sell well. Pat Flynn's paid LEED guide was **~95% the same content** as his free site — it still sold.

Why release a free version at all:
- **More people read it** — reach thousands, not a few hundred buyers; positions you as a thought leader.
- **More people share it** — *Rails Tutorial* became the de-facto Rails starting point precisely because it's free; shares and backlinks compound.
- **More content to index** — Google can't index a paywalled PDF, but a free web version feeds search traffic.

Why people still pay for a free thing:
- **To support the author** — "surprisingly common... people are eager to support the people who have taught them meaningful things."
- **For a specific format** — free as a web page, paid for the PDF/ePub; "people will pay for convenience."
- **For extra paid-only content** — Barry's favorite reason: *Rails Tutorial* bundles **20 hours of video** for buyers.

Any free version needs an obvious upsell (opt-in to an email course, or a link to the paid packages) — never give away the work "without a way to either make money or gain subscribers."

### [AUTH:PR.5] Don't chase pirates
Barry uses **no DRM, no PDF stamping**. Rationale: DRM tells paying customers you don't trust them; it is trivially bypassed; and unpaid downloads "wouldn't have purchased anyway" — you can't multiply pirate downloads by sticker price and call it lost revenue. Spend that energy on great products and happy customers instead.

### [AUTH:PR.6] Premium price beats a wider discount (buyer's own testimony)
**Brandon Savage** was "terrified" to charge **$39** for *Mastering Object-Oriented PHP* — then customers happily paid. His own estimate: "**lowering the price $20 would only bring in 20% more customers, while reducing my revenue severely. A premium product deserves a premium price.**" His one regret was **not adding value-added components**: "$39 is a great purchase to win, but a **$99 purchase is far better** if you can get it. Nathan Barry and Brennan Dunn both do this." This is the packaging argument (`[AUTH:PK]`) seen from the buyer's side.

**Metrics:** revenue vs units (not units alone), revenue at a doubled price test, refund rate (worry only above ~5%).

**Reversal:** if the goal is maximum reach or a huge paying base rather than revenue, deliberately price low or free-with-upsell (Sacha's path). Never let market-comparison anchoring set the price by default.

## [AUTH:PK] Packaging & Perceived Value

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 4.

**Core Principle:** The same content sold as tiered packages "can at times increase your revenue by four times." Perceived value rises when the **medium** changes (text → video) and when the **label** changes (book → guide/course/kit).

### [AUTH:PK.1] Three-package pricing (the core play)
*The App Design Handbook* was not sold as one $39 book. It was three tiers:
- **The Book — $39**: Units **353**, Revenue **$12,287**
- **The Book + Videos — $79**: Units **233**, Revenue **$16,487**
- **The Complete Package — $169**: Units **138**, Revenue **$20,802**

Total: **$49,576** — nearly double the $28,236 the base price alone implied, from the **same number of buyers**. The higher tiers added video tutorials, Photoshop files, code samples, and extra training. Barry repeated this on his second book "with higher prices and a stronger top-end package" and made even more.

**Package-design rule:** each tier must *increase in perceived value* to match its price. Barry's structure:
- Base = all text (1 medium)
- Middle = text + video + Photoshop files (3 media)
- Top = the above + code samples (4 media)

### [AUTH:PK.2] Perceived value follows the medium
"PDF eBooks are worth more than blog posts" — identical content feels more valuable packaged as a downloadable PDF, and more valuable again as video. **Patrick McKenzie** took content that "could have been an eBook (sold for $10 to $40) on email marketing and turned it into a video course that he sold for $497." Same content, different medium, different perceived value. Include multiple media to raise the perceived value of a higher package.

Media you can stack into higher tiers (from the book): short video tips, video interviews, text interviews, design resources, sample code, explanation screencasts, video lessons (the expert on camera), Photoshop/source files. Note the perceived-value trap: adding *more text* to a higher package barely lifts perceived value "because the medium is unchanged" — the uplift comes from a genuinely different medium.

### [AUTH:PK.3] Labels matter — guides/courses/kits > books
"Books are everywhere for $12." Higher-value words command higher prices:
- **Chris Guillebeau** never calls his products books — his *Frequent Flyer Master* is a **"guide"** at **$49**.
- Barry paid ~$200 for a "**Web Design Business Kit**"; the "kit" label plus the high price made him pay more attention and get more value.

"Courses, guides, kits, lessons, and classes are worth far more than books." Choose the label deliberately — Barry noted paying more for the "kit" actually made him extract more value from it.

### [AUTH:PK.4] Format & delivery shape both perceived value and distribution
The file format is a packaging decision, not just a technical one — it controls where you can sell and how the book feels.
- **PDF** (Barry's primary): full control of colors/layout/typography, reads on any computer/tablet; "what you create is exactly what the reader will see." Weak on phones. Design-heavy books favor PDF.
- **ePub / Mobi**: required for iBooks/Nook (ePub) and Kindle (Mobi); reflow nicely across screen sizes but "provide limited control over graphics and typography" (Drysdale's own warning to buyers), so they're "less ideal for a design book."
- **Web-based book** (like 37signals' *Getting Real* and Hartl's *Rails Tutorial*): a WordPress site; can be free-with-upsell or a paid membership site (S2Member, Wishlist Member). Editable anytime; great for indexing and sharing.
- **Authoring tools** authors actually used: **iBooks Author** (Barry's favorite for PDF — chapters don't reflow the whole doc), **Pages** (Sacha Greif, short books), **InDesign** (Jarrod Drysdale, designer-grade), **LaTeX** (Michael Hartl, Garret Dimon), **HTML→PDF** (Mathias Meyer's *Riak Handbook*).

### [AUTH:PK.5] The print option (perceived value + a physical artifact)
Print is often cheaper than authors assume via print-on-demand. **Rob Walling** uses Amazon **CreateSpace** for *Start Small, Stay Small*: ~**$3 to print + a few dollars shipping ≈ $6 delivered**, drop-shipped on demand — no inventory, no fulfillment, and it's carried in the Amazon Marketplace. "Don't underestimate the perceived value of having a print book" — sell/give at conferences, and "it's really hard to sign an ebook for one of your fans."

**Metrics:** revenue-per-buyer across tiers, take-rate of each tier, upsell conversion to the top package.

**Reversal:** don't invent tiers with no added value — a higher package that is "just more text" barely lifts perceived value (Barry's own observation). The uplift comes from a genuine change in medium/format/depth.

## [AUTH:NM] Naming

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 3.

**Core Principle:** "Be clear first and clever second. If you have to throw one of those out, throw out clever." — Jason Fried. Don't burn weeks on a name.

### [AUTH:NM.1] Clear beats clever (proven by A/B reality)
- **Brandon Savage** launched as *Do This, Not That: Object Oriented PHP* — sales tapered; the title was "too clever." Renamed ***Mastering Object-Oriented PHP*** → "immediately noticed an increase in sales."
- Barry deliberately chose plain titles: **The App Design Handbook**, **Designing Web Applications** — clarity "hasn't hurt sales at all."

### [AUTH:NM.2] Focus on outcomes
Name around the reader's career/lifestyle outcome. List the benefits under "career" and "lifestyle" and you'll often find the title (or at least a subtitle) there. Brennan Dunn's **Double Your Freelancing Rate** — "I'm not sure it is possible to be any more clear as to what your book does."

### [AUTH:NM.3] Naming formulas (from the book)
`____ Handbook` · `Bootstrapping ____` · `Mastering ____` · `Hacking ____` · `Step-by-Step ____` · `____ Bible` · `Learn ____ from Scratch` · `The ____ Survival Guide` · `Introduction to ____` · `How to ____` · `Professional ____` · `____ for ____` (Color Theory for Startups) · `Principles of ____` · `The ____ Guide to ____` · `Discover ____`.

## [AUTH:VA] Validation & Preorders

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 2, 4.

**Core Principle:** "Don't trust someone's opinion as validation. Only trust their money." Friends say yes to be kind; "you can't trust the opinion of your friends and family, but you can trust their wallets."

### [AUTH:VA.1] The ConvertKit lesson
Before building ConvertKit, Barry got a "resounding Yes!" and most people said they'd preorder. Weeks later, when he could actually take preorders, **most weren't interested** — "the two people I was the most sure would preorder never did." The real objections (missing features, switching cost) only surfaced when he asked for money.

The ask that works isn't "would you buy this if it existed?" — it's **"I can process credit cards on my iPhone right now. Do you want to preorder?"**

### [AUTH:VA.2] Preorders as validation + revenue
**Brennan Dunn** offered a **20% preorder discount** and **sold over $10,000 before launch** — proof of demand plus early cash. For his second book he leveraged his existing list for even more preorders.

### [AUTH:VA.3] Test demand with a landing page first
Before writing, put up a landing page and see who opts in (full mechanics in `audience-email-first.md` `[AUTH:LP]`). **Jarrod Drysdale** pushed a landing page live, got featured on Hacker News, and collected **several thousand email subscribers before writing any of the book** — validation before the work.

### [AUTH:VA.4] How to run a preorder
- **Offer a discount for committing early** — Brennan Dunn used **20% off**; it both validates demand and pulls forward cash.
- **Leverage an existing list first** — Dunn's second book got even more preorders from his book/SaaS customers than his first; you rarely start from scratch.
- **Treat paid preorders as the only real signal** — a "sure, I'd buy it" from a friend is politeness; a card charge is data.
- **Start small if unproven** — Sacha Greif shipped a 42-page book in three weeks; a small first product de-risks the whole test (see `launch-marketing.md` `[AUTH:MS]`).

**Metrics:** preorder count/revenue, landing-page opt-in conversion, ratio of "yes I'd buy" to actual paid preorders.
