# Launch, Sales Pages & Post-Launch Monetization — Authority (Nathan Barry)

Everything here is drawn from the text of *Authority* (Nathan Barry, 2013). Numbers, names, and cases are the author's own unless marked otherwise.

---

## [AUTH:LN] The Launch Sequence

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 6, 9.

**Core Principle:** "Write it and sales will come" is a myth. Marketplaces don't market for you — every successful author (even with a publisher) drives their own promotion. A launch is a planned event, not a publish button.

### [AUTH:LN.1] Pre-launch: don't go silent, then drip
The most common failure: a landing page collects emails, then **nothing** until a surprise launch email lands and subscribers can't remember who you are. "Pitches from random people don't convert well." Instead, stay in contact **every week or two**: drip a sample chapter, the outline, quotes about the book, process notes — "just keep them engaged." Give early subscribers a **time-limited discount (Barry uses 24 hours)** for being fans from the start.

### [AUTH:LN.2] Launch-day checklist (Barry's exact routine)
He launches at **8:00 a.m. Eastern** (6:00 a.m. his Mountain time). In order:
1. **Move the sales page to its final URL** — replace the opt-in page so old links point to the sale. Re-test.
2. **Check analytics/tracking** is firing on the sales page.
3. **Publish a blog post** — a summary of the book with a strong CTA to buy (for readers who didn't follow the whole build-up).
4. **Re-read and send the launch email** — clear subject line, no cleverness: the book they've waited for is ready, buy now. Both of Barry's books had **sales within the first 10 minutes.**
5. **Ask friends/connections for shares** (see `[AUTH:BS.2]`).
6. **Check scheduled guest posts** actually went live (mind time zones).
7. **Walk away** — refreshing stats all day doesn't raise sales. Check hourly, then live your life.

### [AUTH:LN.3] Make the purchase decision happen the day before
Send an email the **day before** stating the exact launch date/time. "You want them to make their purchase decision by the day before at the latest" — so the launch email only requires getting out a credit card, not deciding. Don't fear the unsubscribes from this email: "those people weren't going to purchase anyway."

## [AUTH:BS] The Big Splash — Guest Posts & Shares

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 6, 9.

**Core Principle:** Be **everywhere on launch day** so a prospect sees you from multiple angles (a friend's tweet, a guest post, an excerpt) and finally buys. Tailor content to each audience — don't pitch a generic excerpt.

### [AUTH:BS.1] Tim Ferriss's tailored-content model
For *The 4-Hour Body* (Dec 2010), Ferriss wrote for **37signals** an article on using *Basecamp* and *Highrise* to write the book — highly relevant to that audience, with a subtle plug — and separately worked with **BodyBuilding.com** on fitness content. Barry copied it on a smaller scale for *Designing Web Applications*, writing guest posts across design, tech, productivity, business, and lifestyle blogs (ProBlogger, ThinkTraffic, LifeHack, etc.): "I made a big splash in a small corner of the internet." Best if all posts go live on launch day.

### [AUTH:BS.2] Landing guest posts & asking for shares
- **Build the relationship first**: comment helpfully, converse on Twitter, then send a genuinely useful email (link them to a relevant post) *without asking for anything*. Later pitch a targeted, non-salesy guest post with an easy out.
- **Ask for shares on launch day** with a customizable email. Barry's template:
  > Authority is live, can you help?
  > Hey ___, Thanks so much for your help and support through this process... The book is finally live and sales have started to roll in. You can check out the site here: [Link]. Would you mind helping me promote the book? Tweets, Facebook posts, and Hacker News submissions are all welcome... Let me know if I can help on your next project. — Nathan
- **Make sharing frictionless** — supply ready-to-paste sample tweets. Barrett Brooks's *Career Kickstarter* email included 5 pre-written tweets; "all I had to do was scan the list, find my favorite, tweak a few words, then paste it into Twitter."
- **Contact order:** everyone you interviewed for the book first (they have large followings), then anyone with a related audience, then friends. "Just don't be afraid to ask."

### [AUTH:BS.3] Interviews as reach and testimonials as social proof-in-advance
Interviewing experts "let[s] you leverage the audience of the person you are interviewing and borrow some of their status" — once published, most share it with their followers. Barry feared rejection but the common answer was "Of course, I'd be honored." Keep the ask low-effort (a 30-minute call, no homework) or your acceptance rate drops. **Don't offer to pay** an expert for a short interview — Jason Fried (37signals) wouldn't be moved by $500-$1,000, and it just creates awkwardness; only offer a joint venture if they must *prepare* something. The reach from interviewees resharing on launch day "makes it worth doing the interviews just by itself."

### [AUTH:BS.4] Giveaways keep the same product fresh
Because the product is digital, giveaways cost nothing (you only risk losing a sale you might not have made). They let you keep talking about one product "without saying anything new, while still delivering value." Simplest mechanic: comment-to-enter + a random number generator picks the winner. When App Advice gave away one copy of Barry's $0.99 app *Commit*, they got **nearly 100 comments** — "I had no idea how much people loved free stuff!"

## [AUTH:SP] The Sales Page

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 7.

**Core Principle:** "A good sales page spends the first few seconds getting the visitor interested, then the rest of the time overcoming objections." Length scales with the ask: short opt-in pages for emails, long detailed pages for a $249 product.

### [AUTH:SP.1] Write it before you design it
"First you need to open up a text document and write out the entire page." The common mistake is starting with design and filling copy into blanks. Write an objection-crushing narrative first; add design only after. Bonus: a pre-written page is cheaper/easier for a designer to lay out.

### [AUTH:SP.2] Lead with the pain
"A good product solves a painful problem." Barry asked his Twitter followers "What is your biggest obstacle keeping you from writing a book?" and got: *discipline, time, self-confidence, an audience, insecurity, fear of not making sales.* He listed each pain and wrote a narrative overcoming it — "having them listed out like that practically wrote the sales page for me." The reader should think "Wow, he really understands me."

### [AUTH:SP.3] Required elements (in order)
- Why someone should care about the book
- A **sample chapter** (free download — see `audience-email-first.md` `[AUTH:LM.2]`)
- The table of contents
- Social proof
- About the author
- The packages listed
- A purchase option
- **FAQ** to overcome remaining objections (file types, refund policy, skill level) — "every question is an objection to the sale"
- Another purchase link
- Plus: an **image of the product as a physical book** for instant recognition; assume the visitor knows nothing about you.

## [AUTH:SO] Social Proof & Testimonials

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 6, 7.

**Core Principle:** Trust is built by implying others like the product. Testimonials are the easiest form; gather them from everyone who previews the book.

### [AUTH:SO.1] Two testimonial mistakes to avoid
1. **Plain text, no photo** — add a **photo, job title, and link** so readers know "John Smith" is real, not an imaginary friend.
2. **All lumped in one section** — **mix testimonials throughout** the page where they have context (right after the copy about the pain that testimonial addresses).

Other social proof: logos of companies you've worked for, publications you've been featured in, prominent users, and (for popular authors) copies-sold counts.

### [AUTH:SO.2] Barry's own testimonial mistake
For *The App Design Handbook* he gave **no preview copies** and had **zero testimonials at launch**. Fence-sitters emailed asking for reviews from happy customers; he had none. Garrett Dimon did it right — he launched *Starting and Sustaining* with a list of endorsements from influential bloggers who'd read his drafts.

### [AUTH:SO.3] Ask for testimonials — and write them
"It's really rare that busy people will volunteer good testimonials." Ask directly. Even better, **draft it for them**: Barry wrote *"Nathan's pricing strategies made me an extra $15,000 from my self-published book"* for Brennan Dunn (true, from private conversation), sent it, and asked him to sign off. "You get a higher quality endorsement and your reviewers only have to approve what you've written."

## [AUTH:PV] Preview Copies

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 6.

**Core Principle:** Sending drafts to friends and influencers does two jobs: better feedback → better product, and reviewers who feel invested → promote at launch. Barry regrets not doing it ("I was working on the book up until launch day — don't do that").

### [AUTH:PV.1] The two-step ask
Don't email the whole book cold. **First** send a short note: who you are, the project, "would you be interested in a review copy?" — make clear you expect nothing. **Then**, if they say yes, send the content. "The less you ask of them the easier it is to get a buy in." Once they've given a soft commitment they're far more likely to help.

### [AUTH:PV.2] Target the content
When nobody had heard of Tim Ferriss and he pitched *The 4-Hour Work Week* to bloggers, he sent **one chapter** he thought each target would find interesting — not the whole book. "One chapter is a lot less intimidating... his results improved significantly."

## [AUTH:AF] Affiliates & Deal Sites

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 6, 9.

**Core Principle:** When you reach the end of your own audience, borrow others' — for a share of sales. On digital products you carry no marginal cost, so generous splits still work.

### [AUTH:AF.1] Affiliates: pay well, stay selective
- Typical digital-book affiliate commission is **~50%**; many authors offer more to keep top affiliates motivated. "Negotiating the lowest possible commission may not actually be in your best interest."
- **90/10 rule:** ~90% of affiliate sales come from <10% of affiliates. **Chris Guillebeau** makes affiliates fill out an application (how they'll promote) to fight the **98/2 rule** — 98% never sell a single copy — leaving a small, high-quality group to support. "Fire anyone who uses questionable sales techniques."

### [AUTH:AF.2] Deal sites: cash + list, if you own the customer
Barry ran deals with **AppSumo (largest), Dealotto, MightyDeals**. Actual terms and numbers:

| Deal | Package | Split (to Barry) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| App Design Handbook — AppSumo | Middle, 50% off | 60/40 | ~**$5,700** sales, 373 copies, 8 refunds |
| App Design Handbook — Dealotto | Complete, ≥50% off | — | 20 copies, profit **$822.40** |
| Designing Web Apps — AppSumo | Book $19, 50% off | 50/50 | 899 copies, ~**$8,540** profit, 8 refunds |
| Designing Web Apps — MightyDeals | Book $19, 50% off | 50/50 | 343 copies, **$3,138** profit |

Total: **~$20,000 from deals + over 1,500 new email subscribers** ("which I value just as much").

Rules for deals:
- **Negotiate the split.** 70/30 (yours = 30%) is the opening offer; digital margins are ~95%, so you can push to 60/40 or 50/50 (Barry got 50/50 with AppSumo and MightyDeals "after a few rounds").
- **Own the customer.** Deal sites keep the customer by default. Barry sets up a MailChimp/ConvertKit thank-you landing page: buyer enters email to receive the download + opt-in for future content. Without the email you can't sell the next rung.
- **Wait several months after launch** — running a deal right after launch upsets fans who just paid full price.
- **Cap discounts at ~50%.** Deeper discounts dilute perceived value / brand ("shows I don't believe my product is worth it").
- **Upsell inside the deal:** run the deal on the cheapest package (his cut on a $19 book ≈ $9.50), then offer deal-buyers an upgrade to a higher package at 50% off ($125, normally $249) where he keeps all proceeds. "I expect to make far more than that original $9.50 off of each customer."

## [AUTH:EC] E-Commerce & Checkout

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 8.

**Core Principle:** Use a simple third-party checkout you can integrate with a link; don't build payments into your own site for a single product. "Very few customers will cancel their purchase because you send them to an outside payment provider."

### [AUTH:EC.1] Provider comparison (Barry's actual choices)
- **Gumroad (his favorite):** merchant account included, **fee 5% + $0.25**, instant setup, direct deposit every two weeks; best checkout on the web (only 4 fields: email, card, expiry, CVC — no name/address). The **Gumroad modal** embeds the purchase in an SSL iFrame on your page — "the security of an offsite process with the trust and seamless checkout... the exact method I use to sell my books." Downside at the time: no affiliate/A-B-test integration.
- **Space Box + Stripe:** excellent UX, **fee 3.9% + $0.30** (cheaper than Gumroad). Barry: had he used it for his last **$142,000** in sales he'd have **saved ~$1,485** in fees — but it's one more setup step than Gumroad.
- **PayPal:** the default, but **freeze risk** — the day after a $20,000 transfer cleared, PayPal froze his account for over a month. If frozen, **call them** (30 minutes on the phone beats a month of emails). Still offer it, because "someone always asks" (no card, or a country blocking cards).
- **E-junkie:** dated interface, but the **leading affiliate platform** — many thousands of affiliates ready to sell (quality is mixed). Handles cart/delivery/admin only; still needs PayPal or Google Checkout for money.

### [AUTH:EC.2] Kill the cart, own the security offload
For a single product (a book + variations) a **shopping cart is just confusing** — it assumes multiple items. Cutting the cart removes steps. Sending buyers to an offsite provider also offloads SSL/PCI security to experts. High digital margins (~95%) mean fees barely matter versus the convenience.

## [AUTH:PM] Post-Launch Monetization

**Source:** Authority — Chapter 9.

**Core Principle:** "Book sales start high and decrease over time." The launch only carries you so far; a real business keeps teaching and keeps building rungs. "Your first book is just the beginning."

### [AUTH:PM.1] The automated email course (the key long-term engine)
A great blog post or one-off email only reaches whoever's subscribed *that moment*; new subscribers miss it. Fix: an **email sequence** ("course") that drips your best content to every new subscriber over days, with the sale woven in. Trust first, sell second — it turns you "from being a creepy person at a random party to being a trusted advisor."

Barry's *Designing Web Applications* 30-day, 8-email course (note where the pitch appears):
1. Trimming down features
2. Learning to wireframe
3. Designing blank slate screens *(soft sell)*
4. Think about designing forms in a new way
5. Designing form fields and labels
6. Take your web app design to the next level *(hard sell)*
7. Adding style to your application design
8. Typography basics *(soft sell)*

He waits until email **3** to even mention the product, and only in a P.S.; email 4 returns to pure teaching. "This process takes time to make the sale — up to 30 days — but since it is automated... it increases conversion rates (it does)." (Built for exactly this in ConvertKit.)

### [AUTH:PM.2] Fight the dip by teaching
Barry's **worst week ever: $216** total, days with zero sales. The cause wasn't a broken page — it was that his recent posts had drifted to marketing/productivity, "everything but design." He wrote one **design-focused article with a soft sell** at the end → **instant +$1,200 that day.** "When sales drop, keep teaching and giving content away for free." His baseline target: **$5,000/month per book.**

### [AUTH:PM.3] Upsell, cross-sell, future products
- **Upsell** the higher package to base-package buyers at a discount — converts weakly (they chose base for a reason) but "I have made at least $500 for each book I tried it with."
- **Cross-sell** the next product to existing customers. **100+ people** who bought *The App Design Handbook* also bought *Designing Web Applications*. Brennan Dunn cross-sells his book ↔ his Planscope SaaS.
- **Future products** to the same list cost nothing to reach — "you don't have to start from scratch with each new product."

### [AUTH:PM.4] Workshops (the biggest revenue jump)
"A few friends have **doubled their book revenue** by hosting online workshops." **Brandon Savage**: ~$10k from the book in 4 months → **+$9,000 from his first workshop.** **Brennan Dunn**: ~$90k from two books → **+$95,000 from workshops** in a year. Tickets run **$200-$1,200** — "you don't need to sell very many seats before it becomes worthwhile."

### [AUTH:PM.5] Refunds & updates
- **Refunds:** don't worry unless **more than ~5% (4-5 per 100)**. Issue quickly, optionally ask why for product feedback.
- **Updates:** make minor fixes free; charge for major new versions (with up to 50% off for existing buyers). Michael Hartl charges for annual *Rails Tutorial* updates and sees a revenue spike. Don't promise "free updates forever" on a fast-changing technical topic — it commits you to an unpaid annual project.

## [AUTH:MS] Mindset (why to ship at all)

**Source:** Authority — Chapters 1, 10.

- **Self-publish — don't wait to be picked.** The artist/musician/author waiting to be discovered has handed control to someone else. Self-publishing means no permission needed. Brett Kelly (*Evernote Essentials*): self-publishing keeps full creative freedom — "you don't have editors or a publisher telling you what can and cannot go into the project."
- **Start small.** Sacha Greif wrote and shipped his first book in **three weeks** (~30 pages). "Just because someone else spent thousands of hours... doesn't mean you need to do that for your first book." Had it failed, he'd have lost weeks, not years.
- **Start before you feel ready** (James Clear, via Barry): "You may not feel like an expert qualified to write your book. But if you don't start, you won't ever become that expert... you may not feel like your work is good enough to charge for it. That's fine. Charge anyway." "That one habit will change everything."
- **The payoff is freedom** — Barry: "The most important thing writing books has given me is freedom. I haven't submitted a time-off request in over a year and a half."
