# Positioning Examples

## Context

Use this file throughout all stages as concrete illustrations of positioning in action. Each example shows what changed, why it changed, and what resulted. When a user struggles to understand what good positioning looks like, or when a stage concept needs grounding, pull the relevant example. Do not narrate these as inspiration stories — extract the procedural lesson: what decision was made, what framing was used, why it worked.

---

## Example 1: Database → Data Warehouse

**Market style**: Head to Head (in a leaderless market)

**Before**: Startup founded by PhDs with deep database expertise. Patented query algorithm for fast analysis of very large datasets. Self-described as "database company."

**The problem**: The database market was mature. Oracle was the undisputed category leader. Every sales meeting opened the same way: "How are you better than Oracle?" The startup lost that comparison every time — Oracle had more features, more integrations, more trust, more budget justification. Prospects rejected the product before the actual technical differentiation could be demonstrated. Investors saw no fundable path to dethroning established database vendors.

**Turning point**: A customer said, unprompted: "You're not a database — you're more like a data warehouse." That observation reframed the whole problem. In a side-by-side comparison against databases, they lost. In a side-by-side comparison against data warehouses, they won clearly.

**After**: Repositioned as a data warehouse. The market category existed and was understood by buyers. Critically, no clear leader had emerged in it.

**What changed operationally**:
- Prospects stopped rejecting them at the start of meetings and started buying
- Prices increased — data warehouses commanded a premium over commodity databases
- Product roadmap shifted to reinforce warehouse value, not database parity
- The team's self-perception changed: "not only changed the way customers viewed our product, it changed the way we saw ourselves"

**Procedural lesson**: Market category choice is business strategy. The identical product in a different category frame goes from losing to winning. The question is not "what are we?" but "in which competitive context do our strengths become obvious and our weaknesses become irrelevant?"

**Stages illustrated**: Stage 2 — customers were actually comparing against BI tools and warehousing tools, not databases. Stage 5 — market category shift from mature (Oracle-dominated) to leaderless. Stage 5 decision — Head to Head works here because no clear leader has emerged in warehousing.

## Example 2: Janna CRM → CRM for Investment Banks

**Market style**: Big Fish, Small Pond

**Before**: Small CRM company competing against Siebel Systems ($2B revenue, hundreds of Fortune 1000 clients). Positioned as "enterprise CRM." Prospects called them "cheaper, crappier Siebel." One differentiating feature existed: relationship modeling — the ability to map and visualize connections between people. Most prospects didn't care.

**The problem**: Positioned directly against Siebel in a category Siebel owned. The one genuinely unique capability was invisible in a generic CRM comparison. Resources were too thin to win broad enterprise deals.

**Turning point**: A meeting with the head of investment banking at a large New York bank. He became visibly excited about the relationship modeling feature. Closed the deal. Post-sale discovery: investment banking is structurally driven by relationships. Knowing who knows whom, and the strength of those connections, translates directly into revenue. The unique feature was not a marginal improvement — it was a critical business tool for that specific segment.

**After**: Repositioned from "enterprise CRM" to "CRM for investment banks." All demos used banking data. All language used banking terminology. The relationship modeling feature was positioned as core, not differentiating.

**Internal pushback**: The team worried that narrow targeting meant less opportunity. Investors feared the market was too small.

**Counter-argument used**: "We weren't flooded with prospects anyway." Owning a niche beats being rejected by everyone. Adjacent segments (insurance, retail banking) remain accessible after proving the model. Category dominance in a narrow segment is a platform, not a ceiling.

**What changed operationally**:
- Revenue: $2M → $70M in 18 months
- Siebel was rapidly eliminated as a competitor in banking — they couldn't match the domain depth
- Prices increased — investment banks expected and accepted premium pricing
- Expansion into adjacent segments followed naturally
- Company acquired for $1.7B (largest Canadian software acquisition at the time)

**Procedural lesson**: Big Fish, Small Pond works when a specific behavioral segment has a strong, unmet need that your unique attribute directly serves. Narrow the target segment to the buyers for whom your differentiator is not a nice-to-have but a business-critical capability. Dominance in a narrow segment is faster to achieve and more defensible than middling presence in a broad one.

**Stages illustrated**: Stage 3 — unique attribute is relationship modeling, which most buyers undervalue but one segment treats as essential. Stage 4 — target segment defined behaviorally (businesses where relationship intelligence drives revenue), not just vertically. Stage 5 — Big Fish, Small Pond selected because segment is defined and underserved, and niche dominance is achievable.

## Example 3: Cake → Cake Pop

**Market style**: Context shift (conceptual example)

**Before**: Baker creates a small cake mounted on a stick — portable, single-serve, ball-shaped. Pitches it as "cake on a stick." Reactions: confused, wrong. Sticks don't belong in cake. The ball shape violates what cake is supposed to look like.

**The problem**: The word "cake" activates a specific set of expectations — large format, fork-required, served at a table. The features that make the product distinctive (the stick, the size, the portability) are interpreted as defects in a cake context. The category frame is working against the product.

**After**: Positioned as "a lollipop for grownups, made of cake." Now the stick is expected — lollipops have sticks. The ball shape is natural. The single-serve format fits. It's a snack, not a dessert. The grownup framing adds appeal.

**Procedural lesson**: Default positioning — whatever category label comes to mind first — triggers assumptions that may actively suppress what makes the product valuable. Deliberate positioning finds a frame where the unique features are expected, natural, or desirable. The product does not change. The evaluative context changes.

**Stages illustrated**: Stage 1C — releasing the "cake" label requires recognizing that the inherited category is causing the confusion, not the product. Stage 5 — choosing a market frame where the product's actual strengths are central.

## Example 4: Email for Lawyers → Team Collaboration

**Market style**: Category correction

**Before**: Two ex-lawyers built "better email for lawyers." No calendar feature — standard in any email product. Key feature: secure, context-aware file sharing between team members.

**The problem**: "Email" as a category triggers hard expectations — threading, calendar integration, contacts, rich formatting. The product lacked many of them. In every evaluation, the missing features overshadowed the unique one. The genuine differentiator (file sharing) was not valued in the email context — email is for messages, not for file management.

**Discovery**: Happy customers were not using it as email. They were using it for collaboration — sharing documents, coordinating on matters, managing team workflows. The value was in the file sharing and context management, not the messaging.

**After**: Repositioned as team collaboration software. In that category, secure file sharing is expected and central. The missing calendar is irrelevant — collaboration tools don't require calendars.

**What changed operationally**:
- Customers immediately understood the value without explanation
- Evaluation criteria shifted to the product's actual strengths
- "Missing calendar" objections disappeared — wrong category for that requirement

**Procedural lesson**: When customers are happy but using the product differently than positioned, trace what they actually value. That is the real category. Positioning in the wrong category forces prospects to discount real strengths and fixate on irrelevant gaps. Find the category where the key value is not a differentiator but an expected and welcomed capability.

**Stages illustrated**: Stage 2 — real alternatives were collaboration tools, not email clients. Stage 5 — market category mismatch discovered by observing actual usage.

## Example 5: Eloqua → Marketing Automation

**Market style**: Create a New Game

**Before**: Tool built for "demand generators" — the people inside companies responsible for email campaigns, website visitor tracking, and lead qualification for sales teams. The founders called it "demand generation automation." Investors and analysts found it unfocused — a little email, a little tracking, a little workflow automation. The market was deemed too small or undefined.

**Evolution**:
- 2000–2005: Focused on a narrow, specific customer — demand generators. The segment was small but real. Revenue grew steadily with the audience.
- 2005+: The demand generation function began to grow across companies. Headcount in this role expanded. The total addressable audience scaled.
- 2006: Renamed "demand generation automation" to "marketing automation." The broader term captured a wider set of buyers without changing the core product. It also distinguished the platform from simple email tools and made the comprehensive value more legible.

**Category creation note** (from founder Mark Organ): "The most successful efforts in category creation do not result from company executives creating an acronym at an offsite. Rather they are discovered from deeply understanding a narrow set of customers. These customers are often 'freaks,' extreme in their attitudes and behavior, forged by tectonic technological and societal shifts."

**What changed operationally**:
- Revenue: $12M (2006) → $96M (2012)
- IPO
- Acquired by Oracle for $870M

**Procedural lesson**: Create a New Game positioning works when no existing category captures the product's value, and when a specific narrow customer segment has an acute unmet need. Start with those "freak" customers — the ones whose behavior is extreme because their need is extreme. Name what they do. The category becomes broadly understood when that behavior becomes mainstream. Do not create the category by proclamation — discover it by observation.

**Stages illustrated**: Stage 4 — target segment is demand generators, defined behaviorally. Stage 5 — Create a New Game selected because no existing category fit. The category name evolved as the market matured, not as an early marketing exercise.

## Example 6: Clearpath Robotics → OTTO Motors (Self-Driving Industrial Vehicles)

**Market style**: Category reframing

**Before**: Robots that move intelligently around manufacturing facilities — autonomous navigation, facility mapping, dispatch, fleet management. Positioned as "manufacturing robots."

**The problem**: "Robot" in manufacturing means stationary, programmed, repetitive-task machinery — welding arms, assembly line actuators. Clearpath's products moved around the floor autonomously. Every sales conversation started with the same detour: explaining why their robots were nothing like the robots prospects assumed they were buying. The category label was generating the wrong mental model before the product could speak for itself.

**After**: Repositioned as "self-driving vehicles for industrial use." Physical design reinforced the frame: white headlights, red taillights added to hardware. Website and sales materials designed to look like an automotive manufacturer, not a robotics company.

**What changed operationally**:
- Sales cycle shortened — prospects arrived with the correct mental model
- Raised $30M
- Won Silver at Edison Awards

**Procedural lesson**: When the product's core strengths (autonomous mobility, navigation, fleet coordination) are not just different from the default category's norms but actively incompatible with them, switch categories. Find the category where your product's strengths are not differentiators but expected baseline capabilities. "Self-driving vehicle" makes mobility the entire premise. "Manufacturing robot" makes mobility an anomaly.

**Stages illustrated**: Stage 1C — releasing the "robot" label required recognizing that the word itself was the barrier, not the product. Stage 5 — market frame selected where mobility and autonomy are central to the category definition.

## Example 7: Wattpad → Entertainment Company

**Market style**: Market evolution

**Before**: Social storytelling platform — users write serialized fiction, readers consume it on mobile. Positioned as a reading and writing platform.

**Evolution**: Popular Wattpad stories were demonstrably audience-validated. In 2014, a partnership with a Philippine television station produced Wattpad Presents, which increased viewership by 30%. The discovery: Wattpad stories were not just content — they were pre-validated IP with built-in audiences. The platform's data revealed what stories, genres, and narrative structures actually engaged readers at scale.

**After**: 2016 — launched Wattpad Studios. Repositioned as a "global multi-platform entertainment company." Partners include NBC, Sony, Hulu, iflix, and eOne. Machine learning applied to guide adaptation decisions.

**What changed operationally**:
- 70M monthly users
- A new revenue stream: licensing pre-validated IP to studios
- Data became a core product offering, not a side capability

**Procedural lesson**: Positioning should follow value evolution. Wattpad did not change its product — it recognized that the value had shifted. The platform was not just a place to write and read stories; it was a mechanism for identifying audience-validated IP before production investment. When the market's perception of value changes, or when new use cases emerge that dwarf the original, update the positioning to match where the value actually is. Holding a legacy frame suppresses the new opportunity.

**Stages illustrated**: Stage 3 — value evolved from content creation platform to IP + audience data source. Stage 5 — market frame updated to match evolved value.

## How to Apply These Examples

When a user is stuck on Stage 5 (market category selection), use these examples to illustrate each style concretely. Match the style to the user's situation:

- Product losing to an established leader → show Example 1 (find the leaderless adjacent category) or Example 2 (narrow to a segment the leader cannot serve as well)
- Product's key feature feels wrong or out of place in the current category → show Example 3 or Example 6 (find the frame where the feature is expected)
- Customers using the product differently than marketed → show Example 4 (reposition to match actual usage)
- No existing category captures the value → show Example 5 (start narrow, discover the category from extreme users)
- Product's value has evolved significantly → show Example 7 (update positioning to match where value actually is now)

Do not use these examples to inspire. Use them to diagnose — identify which pattern matches the user's situation, then extract the specific decision that resolved it.
