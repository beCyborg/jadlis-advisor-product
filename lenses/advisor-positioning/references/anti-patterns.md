# Positioning Anti-Patterns

## Context

Use this file as a diagnostic reference at any stage of the positioning process. When you detect positioning problems in a client's situation, match their symptoms to the named anti-patterns below. Each entry specifies what the anti-pattern is, why it fails, and what to do instead. Do not enumerate all anti-patterns unprompted — identify which ones apply to the situation and address only those.

---

## The 10 Anti-Patterns

### 1. Default Positioning Trap

**Stage**: Pre-process (or any stage where the team resists repositioning)

**What it is**: Sticking with the market category originally imagined when the product was conceived, without deliberate evaluation. The team treats the original market as a given rather than an assumption.

**Diagnostic signal**: Team says "We're obviously a [category]" or "We've always been [category]." Resistance to examining alternatives. Discomfort with the idea that the current category might be wrong.

**Why it fails**: Products evolve through prototyping and customer feedback — the product you actually ship is rarely what you set out to build. Markets also shift independently of your product. What was once a "diet muffin" is now a "gluten-free paleo snack." Keeping the original market category hides your true strengths because that category carries assumptions that no longer fit your actual product.

The cake-to-cake-pop example illustrates this precisely: positioned as "cake on a stick," the format sounds wrong because cake is the wrong frame. Repositioned as an adult lollipop, the stick and ball shape become strengths rather than oddities. The product didn't change — the frame did.

**What to do instead**: Complete the positioning baggage release before beginning the process (Stage 1C). Explicitly acknowledge that the original market category was an assumption made before the product existed. Evaluate alternatives with fresh eyes using the full positioning canvas. Do not carry the original category forward as a default.

### 2. The Positioning Statement (FOR/IS A/UNLIKE)

**Stage**: Stage 7 (output capture)

**What it is**: Using the traditional positioning statement template — FOR [target], our [product] IS A [category] that [benefit], UNLIKE [alternative] — as the primary method of "doing" positioning.

**Diagnostic signal**: The team produces a FOR/IS A/UNLIKE statement and files it away as the deliverable. No one references it afterward. Marketing, sales, and product all work from different understandings of the positioning.

**Why it fails**:

- It assumes you already know the answers. The template is a container for positioning, not a process for discovering it.
- It reinforces the status quo. Filling in blanks encourages default thinking rather than deliberate evaluation of alternatives.
- It produces unusable output. The statement is too abstract to operationalize. No one wakes up and reads the positioning statement before writing copy.
- It is impossible to remember. The structure is contrived and awkward.
- Dunford's DB2 example: the positioning statement her team produced was circular nonsense — an act of malicious compliance with the process, not an act of genuine positioning work.

**What to do instead**: Capture positioning in the Positioning Canvas. The canvas shows relationships between components, is actionable across all departments (marketing, sales, product, customer success), fits on one page, and is actually referenced. The canvas is a working document, not an artifact to file.

### 3. Starting with Features

**Stage**: Stages 2-3 (competitive alternatives, differentiation)

**What it is**: Beginning positioning work by listing your product's features and working backward to find a market or a value proposition.

**Diagnostic signal**: The team jumps to "What makes us special?" before asking "What would customers use instead?" Feature lists appear before competitor lists. The team compares itself to competitors it knows, not competitors customers recognize.

**Why it fails**: Starting with features unconsciously locks you into comparing against the wrong set of competitors. You see competitors through your expert lens, not the customer's lens. A database startup that lists features against other databases may discover that customers actually compare the product to data warehouses and BI tools — a completely different competitive set that demands completely different differentiation.

Feature-first thinking produces feature-first positioning: a list of attributes with no context about why they matter relative to what the customer would otherwise do.

**What to do instead**: Follow the correct sequence. Start with best-fit customers (Stage 1). Ask those customers what they would use if your product didn't exist (Stage 2). Only then identify which of your attributes are genuinely unique relative to those specific alternatives (Stage 3). The competitive alternatives define what "unique" means. Without them, "unique" is meaningless.

### 4. Investor Positioning = Customer Positioning

**Stage**: Stage 5 (market category) / General

**What it is**: Using the same story, deck, or language for both investors and customers.

**Diagnostic signal**: The sales deck looks like the investor pitch deck. The website talks about "disrupting" the market. Prospects are told they are part of a revolution they didn't ask to join.

**Why it fails**: Investors buy your future — a 5-10 year vision, large addressable market, disruption of incumbents. Customers buy your present — solve my specific problem today, work with the tools I already have, don't break anything. "Disruption" excites investors because it signals a large opportunity. It terrifies customers because it means replacing things they have invested in and depend on.

**What to do instead**: Maintain separate positioning for investors and for customers. The website, sales materials, pricing pages, and product roadmap should reflect customer positioning. Investors will understand the distinction between what you tell customers and what the long-term opportunity looks like. Do not let investor positioning leak into customer-facing materials.

### 5. Demographic Segmentation

**Stage**: Stage 4 (target segment)

**What it is**: Defining the target market using demographic characteristics: age, gender, company size, industry, geography.

**Diagnostic signal**: The segment description reads like a census form. "Men 25-40." "Companies with 100-500 employees." "Small businesses in the retail sector."

**Why it fails**: Demographics rarely predict who cares about your specific value. "Small businesses" is too broad. Some small businesses send 100 invoices per day — a perfect fit for your invoicing tool. Others send 2 invoices per month — they have no problem worth solving. Company size tells you nothing about workflow, tools in use, or pain intensity.

**What to do instead**: Define the segment using behavioral and situational characteristics:

- What tools have they invested in?
- How do they currently solve this problem?
- What specific pain do they experience?
- What skills do they lack internally?
- What regulatory or compliance requirements apply?
- What business model do they use?

The target market is not who they are — it is what they do. "Customers who buy quickly, rarely ask for discounts, and refer others" is a behavioral description. It is far more predictive than any demographic filter.

### 6. Broad Targeting ("Boil the Ocean")

**Stage**: Stage 4 (target segment)

**What it is**: Targeting the entire market or refusing to narrow focus in order to avoid excluding any potential customer.

**Diagnostic signal**: Team says "Anyone could use our product" or "We don't want to limit ourselves." Marketing spend covers wide audiences. Win rates are low. Sales cycles are long. No clear pattern of which customers close.

**Why it fails**: Broader focus means a harder connection with any individual prospect, longer sales cycles, lower win rates, and wasted resources. Your best-fit customers do not hear a message that resonates because the message is designed for everyone. You spend marketing budget on prospects who will never buy while your actual buyers are not receiving a focused signal.

**What to do instead**: Target as narrowly as you can to meet your near-term sales objectives. Calculate how many deals you need this year. Determine whether your focused segment is large enough to produce that number. If yes, stay narrow. If no, broaden just enough to reach your target — no further. Narrow now does not mean narrow forever. You expand after you have won the first beachhead.

### 7. Survey All Customers

**Stage**: Stage 1A (identifying best-fit customers)

**What it is**: Surveying your entire customer base to extract positioning insights, rather than isolating your best-fit customers first.

**Diagnostic signal**: The team proposes a broad survey to "understand what customers think." Results come back mixed. No clear patterns emerge. Stakeholders disagree on what the data means.

**Why it fails**: A broad survey includes ecstatic fans, moderately satisfied customers, and customers who should never have bought. Moderately satisfied customers give mixed signals. Their feedback reflects indifference, not insight. The noise from mediocre fits overwhelms the signal from great fits. You cannot build positioning from averaged-out opinions.

**What to do instead**: Survey only the ecstatic fans — customers who understood the product quickly, bought quickly, became active users, and refer others. This group has clear patterns. Their language, their problems, their prior tools, and their outcomes will cluster. Dunford's experience: "If I only surveyed the ecstatic fans and left the moderately happy customers out of it — suddenly a clear pattern emerged." Start with the fans.

### 8. Category Without Validation

**Stage**: Stage 5 (market category)

**What it is**: Choosing a market category based on internal vision or technology trends without verifying that the category exists in buyers' minds.

**Diagnostic signal**: The team selects a category it finds exciting or strategically correct, without evidence that target buyers recognize or use the category label. The category exists in analyst reports but not in buyer conversations.

**Why it fails**: The entire function of a market category is to trigger useful assumptions in the buyer's mind — what the product does, who else uses it, how much it costs, what it replaces. If the buyer does not recognize the category, no assumptions are triggered. The buyer receives no context. You create confusion instead of clarity. For emerging categories, you also create the burden of educating the entire market before a single sale can close.

**What to do instead**: Validate that enough buyers understand the category to meet your revenue targets within your required timeframe. For emerging categories, verify that adoption is growing fast enough that buyers will reach sufficient understanding before you run out of runway. Do not confuse your knowledge of technology trends with what average buyers currently understand. ### 9. Trend Without Market

**Stage**: Stage 6 (trend layer)

**What it is**: Describing the product primarily in terms of a trend, without first establishing what market category it belongs to.

**Diagnostic signal**: Product is described as "[Trend] for [audience]" without specifying what the product actually is. "Sharing economy for pets." "AI-native for finance teams." The category is absent; only the trend is present.

**Why it fails**: Without knowing WHAT something is (market category), knowing WHY NOW (trend) is meaningless. "Sharing economy for pets" sounds interesting but tells the buyer nothing about what they are evaluating, how to compare it to alternatives, or whether it is relevant to their situation. You need the market category to establish the frame before the trend adds resonance.

Dunford's rule: "It's always better to be a little boring than completely baffling."

**What to do instead**: Always establish the market category first, then layer the trend. "Pet services marketplace" (market) + "built on the sharing economy model" (trend). The market category does the work of establishing context. The trend does the work of explaining momentum and timing. Neither substitutes for the other.

### 10. Trend Without Product Link

**Stage**: Stage 6 (trend layer)

**What it is**: Using a trend in positioning when the product has no genuine connection to that trend's underlying capabilities or model.

**Diagnostic signal**: The team cannot explain in one sentence how the trend connects to actual product capabilities. The connection requires interpretation or analogy. The trend appears in marketing language but not in the product roadmap or architecture.

**Why it fails**: Long Blockchain (an iced tea company) changed its name to capitalize on blockchain hype. The company had no blockchain product and no blockchain strategy. The stock briefly surged on the name alone, then collapsed. The company was ultimately delisted from Nasdaq. Hype without substance destroys credibility when buyers investigate.

Beyond reputation risk, a trend without genuine product connection produces a promise you cannot keep. Customers who buy based on the trend will discover the gap immediately upon using the product. The mismatch between positioning and reality is one of the fastest ways to generate churn and negative word of mouth.

**What to do instead**: Apply the Venn Diagram test. Draw three circles: your product's genuine strengths, the market context that makes those strengths relevant, and what your best-fit customers care about. The trend must appear in all three circles — it must be something your product genuinely embodies, something buyers in your market care about, and something your target customers are motivated by. If the product does not genuinely leverage or embody the trend, omit the trend layer entirely. "None" is always a valid answer for Stage 6.

## Quick Diagnostic Reference

| Anti-Pattern | Key Diagnostic Signal | Relevant Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Default Positioning Trap | "We've always been [X]" | Pre-process |
| Positioning Statement | FOR/IS A/UNLIKE template as deliverable | Stage 7 |
| Starting with Features | "What makes us special?" before alternatives | Stages 2-3 |
| Investor = Customer | Sales deck looks like investor pitch | General |
| Demographic Segmentation | Segment described by census attributes | Stage 4 |
| Broad Targeting | "Anyone could use our product" | Stage 4 |
| Survey All Customers | Mixed survey results, no clear patterns | Stage 1 |
| Category Without Validation | Category unknown to actual buyers | Stage 5 |
| Trend Without Market | "[Trend] for [audience]" with no market | Stage 6 |
| Trend Without Product Link | No real product-trend connection | Stage 6 |

When multiple anti-patterns co-occur, address them in stage order. Fixing Stage 2-3 anti-patterns while Stage 1 anti-patterns remain unresolved produces unreliable results.
